Lesson 1

Box-Cox transformations

Some models assume the response is roughly normal with the same spread everywhere. When the spread grows with the size of the value, a Box-Cox transformation reshapes the response so it doesn’t.

When the spread isn’t even

Several models in this course make normality assumptions about the response. If the response is far from normal, the model can give biased results.

The lecture’s example: a histogram of the heights of American women in their 40s is a good fit to the normal distribution, with about the same variance across the whole range. A histogram of the weights of the same women is not — there is less variation among smaller weights and much more among larger ones.

Heteroscedasticity

Unequal variances: the spread of the data changes across its range. The opposite, roughly equal spread everywhere, is homoscedasticity.

Why it matters for regression: the high-variance end produces larger estimation errors, and because least squares squares every error, those points pull hardest on the fit. The model ends up fitting the noisy end better than the rest.

The same thing appears in time series. A stock index’s quarterly close, or the prediction errors of an exponential smoothing model some days ahead, often vary little in early periods and much more in later ones.

quick checkanswered

Monthly electricity bills for small shops vary by about ±$20; for large supermarkets they vary by about ±$2,000. Which problem does this describe?

Check 1

Why can heteroscedasticity bias a least-squares regression, rather than just making it a bit noisier?

Show answer

The high-variance points make larger errors, and squaring makes large errors dominate the sum. Minimising the SSE therefore bends the fit toward those points at the expense of the rest.

The transformation

A Box-Cox transformation stretches out the smaller range to enlarge its variability and shrinks the larger range to reduce it. You choose the value of λ that makes the transformed response as close to normal as possible.

t(y) = (yλ − 1) / λy is the vector of responses and t(y) the transformed vector. λ is chosen so that t(y) is close to normally distributed. Good statistical software finds the best λ for you.
t(y) = ln y  when λ = 0The limit of the formula as λ → 0. Standard definition; the lecture does not write it out.
λEffect on the response
1No change in shape — just a shift by 1
½Square-root-like: a mild pull on the large values
0Logarithm: a strong pull on the large values
−1Reciprocal-like: stronger still

Lecture vs standard practice — is Box-Cox “logarithmic”?

Lecture: describes Box-Cox as a logarithmic transformation.

Standard description: it is a family of power transformations, of which the log is the λ = 0 member. For the λ values usually chosen (between −1 and 1) it behaves like a log — pulling in large values more than small ones — which is the sense the lecture means. It needs every y to be positive.

Check 2

In a Box-Cox transformation, what does the software choose, and by what criterion?

Show answer

It chooses λ — a single number — so that the transformed response t(y) = (yλ − 1)/λ is as close to normally distributed as possible.

Check whether you need it

The important step is remembering to check whether a transformation is needed at all — for example with a Q-Q plot, which plots the data’s quantiles against those of a normal distribution. Points that follow the straight line suggest normal data; a systematic bend suggests a transformation may help.

One practical consequence the lecture leaves implicit: a model fitted to t(y) predicts t(y). Before reporting a prediction, undo the transformation — y = (λt + 1)1/λ, or eᵗ when λ = 0.

Why “Box”?

There is no box. Unlike the box in a box-and-whisker plot, the Box in Box-Cox is a name: the statistician George Box, who developed the technique with David Cox.

Lesson 2

Detrending

A trend — a steady increase or decrease over time — can make identical conditions look different just because they happened in different years. Detrending subtracts it before a factor-based model is fitted.

Why a trend gets in the way

Trend

Data that is increasing or decreasing over time — the same word used in the time-series lessons.

The lecture’s example is the end-of-year price of gold, which has risen over time. Part of that rise is inflation: the dollar has been losing value, which gives an upward trend to the price of almost everything. Adjusted for inflation, the true fluctuations in gold’s price relative to other things stand out, without the upward trend in the way.

Now suppose a regression estimates the gold price from other factors. With the trend left in, the same factor values in 1974 and 2009 could go with very different prices. Adjusted for inflation, the baseline price in those two years is about the same.

It isn’t only the response: some predictors may need detrending too. In general, at least consider detrending whenever you use a factor-based model — regression, support vector machines and so on — on time-series data.

quick checkanswered

An SVM classifies each week of a bakery’s last ten years as “busy” or “quiet” from weather and local events. Footfall has grown steadily as the town has grown. What is the risk?

Check 4

When should you at least consider detrending, and which variables might need it?

Show answer

Whenever you use a factor-based model, such as regression or an SVM, on time-series data.

The response, and possibly some of the predictors as well.

How to detrend

In the gold example the lecture could adjust each data point by historical inflation, found on any number of websites. In most cases, though, you don’t know what the trend has been except from the data you’re trying to detrend. A simple method works factor by factor:

  1. Fit a one-dimensional regression of the factor on time — usually a line, y = a₀ + a₁·t, though a more complicated function is possible.
  2. Subtract the fitted trend from each actual value.
detrended value = actual − (a₀ + a₁·t)For the lecture’s gold data, the linear fit is price ≈ −45,600 + 23.2 × year, so the detrended price = actual price − (−45,600 + 23.2 × year).

The linearly detrended series looks much like the inflation-adjusted one, but not exactly: a straight line assumes a constant rate of inflation, while the inflation-adjusted series uses a different rate each year. Without inflation data, the simple procedure still works pretty well.

Check 6

What is the difference between the problem detrending fixes and the problem Box-Cox fixes?

Show answer

Detrending removes a change in level over time — a steady rise or fall.

Box-Cox fixes a change in spread — variance that grows or shrinks across the range of the response. A series can need either, both or neither.

Lesson 3

Introduction to PCA

Principal component analysis rotates the data onto new directions that are uncorrelated and ranked by how much variance each carries, so you can keep the few that matter most.

Too many predictors, and they overlap

Could the performance of certain stocks on one day predict the overall market the next? About 3,000 securities trade on the New York Stock Exchange, roughly another 3,000 on the Nasdaq, and a few hundred elsewhere. (External events — wars, elections, natural disasters — will always move markets, but on other days a good combination of stocks might predict well.) Two difficulties appear:

  1. Too many predictors. With more than 6,000 factors, before interactions, you need a lot of data — and each data point is one day, so you need many years of it. So many that the situation itself changes: Tesla wasn’t publicly traded until mid-2010, and Kmart, perhaps a good predictor in 1997, merged with Sears in 2004.
  2. Correlated predictors. ExxonMobil, Chevron and Phillips 66 may move up and down roughly in sync; so may General Motors and Ford.
Feature extraction

Building a smaller set of new factors from the original ones, rather than choosing a subset of the originals. PCA is a feature-extraction method.

quick checkanswered

A hospital has 400 lab measurements per patient, many of them near-duplicates (several ways of measuring kidney function, for example), and records for 900 patients. Why is PCA a natural first step before regression?

Check 7

Name the two problems with using thousands of stock prices as predictors.

Show answer

So many predictors need many years of daily data — so many that the underlying situation changes over that time.

Many predictors are highly correlated, like oil companies or car makers that move together.

What PCA does

PCA transforms the data to do two things:

  1. It changes coordinates to remove correlation: the new directions are orthogonal, so the data expressed in them has zero correlation.
  2. It ranks the new directions by variance, so the most important come first.

Rotating the picture only makes the new axes easier to see; rotate it back and the correlation in the new coordinates is still zero.

Keeping only the first n components has another benefit: less randomness. If the random noise is about the same size in every factor, the first components — where the real variation concentrates — have a higher signal-to-noise ratio. Further down the list, noise makes up more of what’s left.

quick checkanswered

Class discussion — Lesson 3click to type; saves automatically
Check 8

Why are the first principal components likely to have a higher signal-to-noise ratio than later ones?

Show answer

If noise is about the same in every factor, it spreads evenly across all directions, while real effects concentrate in the directions of greatest variance. So the top components carry a higher ratio of real variation to random variation, and the ratio falls as you go down the list.

Lesson 4

Using PCA

Centre the data, take the eigenvectors of XᵀX sorted by eigenvalue, and multiply. Each component is a weighted sum of the original factors — which is also what lets you translate a PCA model back.

The recipe

Start with a data matrix X, where xij is the jth factor value for data point i — after scaling, so that for each factor j the average over all data points is shifted to zero.

  1. Find all the eigenvectors of the matrix XᵀX (Lesson 5 covers eigenvectors).
  2. Put them as the columns of a matrix V, sorted from largest eigenvalue to smallest.
  3. The principal components are XV: the first is X times the first column of V, the second X times the second column, and so on.

V is a linear transformation of the data from X to the principal components, so each new factor is a linear combination of the original ones:

tik = xi1v1k + xi2v2k + … + ximvmkThe kth new factor value for data point i: the original factor values weighted by the entries of the kth eigenvector.
put in ordersolved

Put the steps of PCA in order. Drag, or use the arrows.

  1. Shift each factor so its average over all data points is zero
  2. Find the eigenvectors of XᵀX
  3. Sort them from largest eigenvalue to smallest, as the columns of V
  4. Multiply X by V to get the principal components
  5. Keep all the components, or only the first n, depending on the goal

Check 9

What is the second principal component, in terms of X and V?

Show answer

X times the second column of V — the eigenvector of XᵀX with the second-largest eigenvalue.

How many components?

GoalUse
You have enough data and only want to remove correlationThe whole set of principal components
You also want fewer variables in the modelThe first n components, for an n you choose

The math above finds a linear transformation. Using kernels — the same idea as in the support vector machine lessons — PCA can use nonlinear functions too. For this course, just know that it can be done, the idea is the same, and most good analytics software can do it for you.

quick checkanswered

You have 20 correlated factors and 50,000 data points, and you only want the regression coefficients to stop swinging about. How many components should you use?

Check 10

How can PCA handle relationships that aren’t linear?

Show answer

With kernels, the same idea used to make SVMs nonlinear. The principle is unchanged and standard software does the math.

Translating the model back

Suppose you fit a regression on L principal components: yi = b₀ + Σk=1..L bktik. The components have no intuitive meaning — each is a blend of the original factors. Luckily, plugging in the formula for each tik gives an implied coefficient for every original factor:

aj = Σk=1..L bkvjkThe implied coefficient on original factor j. With it you can explain the model in terms of the factors people understand.

Worth knowing — which units aj is in

The aj apply to the factors as they entered PCA. If you only centred them, aj is per original unit. If you also scaled each factor to standard deviation 1 (common in software), divide aj by that factor’s standard deviation to get the effect per original unit. The lecture doesn’t go into this.

Lesson 5

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches or shrinks, never turns; the eigenvalue is how much. PCA’s directions are the eigenvectors of XᵀX.

The definition

A v = λ vFor a square matrix A: if a vector v and a constant λ satisfy this, v is an eigenvector of A and λ is its eigenvalue.

Apply the linear transformation A to v and you get a vector pointing in exactly the same direction — longer or shorter, scaled by λ, but not turned.

Every value of λ for which the determinant of A − λI is zero is an eigenvalue of A. Plug each one into Av = λv and solve for its eigenvector v. Plenty of software does this for you.

det( A − λI ) = 0I is the identity matrix. Each solution λ is an eigenvalue.

Lecture vs standard notation — a slip of the tongue

Lecture (spoken): “the determinant of A minus lambda times A identity matrix.”

Standard: det(A − λI) = 0 — λ times the identity matrix. The meaning is the same; write it the standard way.

quick checkanswered

A = [[2, 0], [0, 3]] and v = (0, 1). Is v an eigenvector, and with what eigenvalue?

Check 12

In words, what does Av = λv say about v?

Show answer

Transforming v by A doesn’t change its direction; it only scales its length by λ (a negative λ flips it to point the opposite way along the same line).

Why PCA needs them

The first step of PCA is finding all the eigenvectors of XᵀX; multiplying X by each eigenvector gives the principal components. The course skips the theory, but the properties of eigenvectors and eigenvalues give not just a transformed set of coordinate directions, but directions that are all orthogonal to each other. Standard fact, not stated in the lecture: each eigenvalue measures how much of the data’s variance lies along its eigenvector, which is why sorting by eigenvalue ranks the components.

Lesson 6

PCA: good and bad

PCA ranks directions by the spread of the predictors and never looks at the response. Usually the widest direction is also the most useful one — but not always.

Same components, different answer

Because PCA depends only on the independent variables, nothing about the response enters the calculation. So although it isn’t likely, the response could be affected only by variables with low variability, not by the ones with high variability. The lecture shows this with classification, but the idea is the same for other kinds of question.

Figure 1: D1 explains more variance than D2, and it also separates red from blue — put the dividing line exactly on the D2 axis and D1 splits the data perfectly. Reducing from two dimensions to one, PCA would suggest D1, and D1 still gives an excellent separator.

Figure 2: PCA gives exactly the same recommendation — D1 explains more variance — but now D1 doesn’t separate red from blue at all. The helpful separator is D2, the one PCA says to drop.

quick checkanswered

Why does PCA give the same recommendation in Figure 1 and Figure 2?

Check 14

In one sentence, what is PCA’s blind spot?

Show answer

It ranks directions by how much the predictors vary, without ever looking at the response, so a low-variance direction that actually drives the response can be ranked last and discarded.

So why use it?

Because often the dimensions with more variability are the better differentiators — precisely because they carry more information. It isn’t always true, so reducing dimension with PCA isn’t guaranteed to keep the explanatory or predictive power. Overall, though, it is often an approach that reduces dimension without losing too much.

Exam target

A ride-hailing company models trip fares. Residuals are within ±$1 for short trips and ±$25 for long ones. What would you check, and what might you do?

Show answer

That is heteroscedasticity: the spread of the response grows with its size, so the long trips dominate a least-squares fit. Check with a Q-Q plot of the fares (or the residuals). If it bends, let software choose a Box-Cox λ, refit on t(y), and convert predictions back to dollars before reporting them.

Exam target

A university regresses 25 years of annual applications on tuition and ranking. Applications have grown steadily with population. Before fitting, what should happen, and to which variables?

Show answer

Consider detrending: it is a factor-based model on time-series data. Fit a line of applications against year and subtract it; do the same for tuition, which has almost certainly trended upward too. Otherwise the model can credit tuition with growth that really came from population — same tuition-to-ranking conditions, very different application counts in different decades.

Exam target

A trend line for a town’s annual water use is 1,800 + 45·(year − 2000) megalitres. In 2014 the town used 2,300. What is the detrended value, and what does it mean?

Show answer

Trend in 2014: 1,800 + 45×14 = 2,430. Detrended: 2,300 − 2,430 = −130 megalitres. The town used 130 ML less than its long-run trend predicted — perhaps a wet year or restrictions — which is exactly the kind of variation a factor-based model should try to explain.

Exam target

A soil lab measures 120 chemical properties for 300 samples, many strongly correlated. An agronomist wants a regression for crop yield she can explain factor by factor. Outline an approach.

Show answer

Centre (and, since the units differ, scale) the properties; run PCA; keep the first n components, choosing n by validation; regress yield on them. Then convert back with aj = Σk bkvjk to get one implied coefficient per original property (divided by each property’s standard deviation if they were scaled). That gives her the explanation in soil-chemistry terms without fitting 120 correlated coefficients to 300 samples.

Exam target

For A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]], check that v = (1, 1) is an eigenvector and find its eigenvalue.

Show answer

Av = (4·1 + 1·1, 2·1 + 3·1) = (5, 5) = 5·(1, 1). Same direction, five times as long: v is an eigenvector with λ = 5. (The other eigenvalue is 2, with eigenvector (1, −2): det(A − λI) = (4 − λ)(3 − λ) − 2 = λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0.)

Exam target

A factory monitors 60 vibration sensors to predict a rare bearing failure. PCA’s first 4 components explain 95% of the variance, but a model on them misses most failures. Explain what may be happening.

Show answer

PCA ranked directions by how much the sensors vary, not by how well they predict failure. The big components probably track normal operating swings — load, speed, temperature — while an early failure shows up as a small, specific pattern in a low-variance direction. Check how each lower-ranked component relates to failure, and choose how many to keep by validation, not by the 95% rule.

How the module fits together

The through-line across all six lessons

  • Lesson 1Fixes the response’s spread: Box-Cox chooses λ so the transformed response is close to normal and evenly spread.
  • Lesson 2Fixes drift over time: fit a line to each trending factor and subtract it before a factor-based model sees the data.
  • Lesson 3Fixes too many overlapping predictors: PCA rotates to uncorrelated directions, ranked by variance.
  • Lesson 4Gives PCA’s recipe — centre, eigenvectors of XᵀX, sort, multiply — and the formula to translate a PCA model back to the original factors.
  • Lesson 5Supplies the linear algebra: Av = λv, eigenvalues from det(A − λI) = 0, and orthogonal directions.
  • Lesson 6Marks the limit: PCA never sees the response, so the direction it discards can occasionally be the one that matters.

Four things worth noticing

  • Each fix targets a different part of the data. Box-Cox reshapes the response; detrending works on the response and any trending predictor; PCA works only on the predictors.
  • Each fix changes the units you report in. Box-Cox predictions must be transformed back; detrended values are deviations from a trend; PCA coefficients must be translated back to the original factors.
  • Software does the arithmetic, you make the calls. λ, the trend line and the eigenvectors are all computed; whether to transform, what to detrend and how many components to keep are yours.
  • PCA and Module 8 meet at correlation. Correlated predictors made regression coefficients hard to interpret; PCA removes the correlation, and aj = Σ bkvjk restores the interpretation.

Extra 1

PCA in practice

Three decisions the math leaves to you: whether to scale, how many components to keep, and where in the workflow PCA gets fitted.

Centre, and usually scale

The lecture centres each factor. Software such as R’s prcomp() centres by default and can also scale each factor to standard deviation 1 (scale. = TRUE). Without scaling, PCA ranks directions by raw variance, so a factor recorded in large units — income in dollars against age in years — dominates the first component purely because of its units, the same problem the scaling lessons in Module 2 warned about for distances. When factors are in different units, scaling is almost always right; when they share units and their spread is meaningful (say, 40 sensors all in millimetres), leaving them unscaled can be reasonable.

How many components

  • Proportion of variance explained — each eigenvalue divided by their total. A scree plot of these often shows an “elbow” after which each component adds little.
  • Cumulative thresholds such as 80–95% are common rules of thumb, but, as Lesson 6 shows, variance is not the same as usefulness.
  • Validation — when the components feed a model, treat n like any other choice and compare candidates on validation data (Module 3). This is the only one of the three that looks at the response.

Fit PCA inside the validation loop

The eigenvectors are estimated from data, so they are part of the model. Computing them on the whole dataset and then splitting into training and test lets the test data influence the model — a form of leakage. Fit the centring, scaling and eigenvectors on the training data only, then apply that same transformation to validation and test points.

quick checkanswered

You run unscaled PCA on house data with price in dollars, floor area in square metres and number of rooms. The first component is almost exactly “price”. Why?

Check 16

Why should PCA be fitted on the training data only, rather than the whole dataset before splitting?

Show answer

The eigenvectors (and the centring and scaling) are estimated from data, so they are part of the model. Estimating them with the test data included lets the test set influence the model, which makes the test estimate optimistic.

Practice and reading

Problems with worked solutions, and where to go next.

Practice

  1. Box-Cox by hand. With λ = ½, transform y = 4, 16 and 64. How much further apart are the largest two than the smallest two, before and after? (Before: 12 and 48. After: t = 2(√y − 1) = 2, 6, 14, so gaps of 4 and 8 — the large-value gap shrinks from 4× to 2× the small one.)
  2. Detrending. Quarterly sales follow the trend 500 + 12t. In quarter t = 20 sales were 690. Detrended value? (Trend 740; detrended −50.)
  3. Back-translation. A model on three components has b = (1.5, −0.5, 2). Factor j has vj· = (0.2, 0.4, −0.1). Find aj. (0.3 − 0.2 − 0.2 = −0.1.)
  4. Eigenvalues. For A = [[3, 1], [0, 2]], solve det(A − λI) = 0. ((3 − λ)(2 − λ) = 0, so λ = 3 and 2.)
  5. Variance explained. Eigenvalues are 6, 2, 1, 0.6, 0.4. What share do the first two explain? (8/10 = 80%.)
