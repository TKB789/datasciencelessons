Section 1

How you are assessed

Three proctored quizzes decide 75% of the grade, so everything else — homework, the project, the syllabus quiz — is worth less combined than any single quiz.

This page is not lecture content

Everything here comes from a course syllabus rather than from a lecture recording, and it has been rewritten in general terms with the institution, course code, instructor, platform names, contact details and calendar dates removed. Timings are given relative to the semester rather than as dates. Syllabus terms change between offerings. Treat this as a study aid and check every number against your own current syllabus before relying on it.

There is also an onboarding quiz. It carries no marks — its job is to verify identity and confirm that the proctoring software works on your machine. Doing it late is the classic way to discover a webcam problem on the morning of a midterm.

What the weights imply for effort

Homework runs most weeks and the two lowest scores are dropped. With fourteen assignments and two dropped, twelve count, so each surviving homework is worth about 1.25% of the grade — roughly one twentieth of a single quiz. Homework is where the learning happens; it is not where the grade is.

When things happen

Content is released weekly across a fifteen-week teaching schedule, with the final quiz window running past the end of it. Each quiz opens once its material has been released and stays open for about eleven days — you pick your slot inside that window, but there is no extension beyond it.

Section 2

What each quiz covers

Midterm 1 takes the first ten modules, midterm 2 focuses on the next six while still assuming the first ten, and the final is comprehensive — so the early modules are examined three times and the last few only once.

The coverage map

The course runs to twenty-one modules of lecture content. Coverage divides like this:

  • Midterm 1 — modules 1 to 10.
  • Midterm 2 — primarily modules 11 to 16, but it requires knowledge from modules 1 to 10 as well.
  • Final — comprehensive, covering every module.

Reading around the course

The syllabus states plainly that there is no set textbook — all material is delivered online, and the quizzes are written from the lectures. A general statistical learning textbook is still useful for depth on the modelling topics, provided you treat it as background rather than as coverage.

Where a statistics textbook helps, and where it doesn’t

An introductory statistical-learning text overlaps well with about half this course: classification, validation, clustering, regression, tree-based methods, variable selection and principal component analysis are all standard textbook material. It will give you almost nothing on change detection, classical time-series models, design of experiments, simulation, or optimization — those are operations-research topics that such books do not carry. Reading a chapter that has no counterpart in the lectures is time spent on material that cannot be examined.

Section 3

The note sheet

Notes must be handwritten on a fixed number of sheets, and the constraint is the point — what survives the squeeze is what you actually needed.

The rules

QuizWhat you may bring
Midterm 1 and 2One sheet of paper, either 8.5″ × 11″ or A4, handwritten on both sides — two sides in total
FinalTwo sheets of the same size, handwritten on both sides of each — four sides in total
All quizzesBlank sheets of paper for scratch work. No calculators — no question requires one

Handwritten, and shown on camera

Printed or typed notes do not qualify. Every sheet of notes and every sheet of scratch paper must be held up to the camera, both sides, during the pre-quiz room scan. A sheet that is not shown is a sheet you should assume you cannot use.

What actually belongs on it

With no calculators and around two minutes a question on a midterm, the sheet is not a place for worked arithmetic. It is a place for the things that are quick to look up and expensive to misremember.

  1. Draft it while revising, not after. Deciding what earns a place is the revision — writing the sheet at the end wastes the mechanism.
  2. Put down what distinguishes methods, not what defines them. You will not forget what clustering is; you may well blank on what separates a hard from a soft classifier, or scaling from standardization.
  3. Include the formulas you would otherwise half-remember. Anything with a subtraction that could go either way, or a term that could sit in a numerator or a denominator.
  4. Include the direction of every parameter. Larger λ means what? Larger C? Larger k? These are pure recall and cheap to write down.
  5. Include the assumptions and failure modes. When a method breaks is more examinable than how it works.
  6. Leave off anything you have never once needed while practising. Space is the binding constraint; nostalgia is not a criterion.

Section 4

Rules that cost marks

Almost none of the avoidable losses in this course come from not knowing the material — they come from missed windows, skipped peer assessments, and proctoring set up too late.

Proctoring

Quizzes are remotely proctored and include a full scan of the room you are sitting in. Two consequences worth planning around:

  • Anything in shot gets recorded. Either clear or cover what you would rather not have scanned, or take the quiz somewhere quiet and private — a library study room, for instance.
  • Notes and scratch paper are part of the scan. Have them ready to show, both sides.

The hard deadline

A missed quiz cannot be taken after its window closes, for any reason. In appropriate circumstances an instructor may impute a score from your other quiz results, but that is a discretionary remedy, not an entitlement, and it is not something to build a plan around. Book your slot early in the eleven-day window so a technical failure still leaves you days of room.

Peer assessment is not optional

The rule that surprises people

Both the homework and the project are peer-graded, and you must grade others in return. Skip your peer assessments and you receive no credit for your own homework submission; skip them on the project and the project scores zero. The work being finished and submitted does not protect you.

Scores are the median of what your peer graders award, not the mean — so a single harsh or generous grader barely moves the result, and there is little value in agonising over one outlier grade.

What is the right call on the homework?

Integrity, and the AI rule in particular

Two policies bear directly on how you work rather than on what you know:

  • Plagiarism. Submitted material must be your own, and any background material you draw on must be cited.
  • AI use. Code or text produced with AI must be cited specifically — you must indicate which part of a submission it generated. The allowance is the same as for a snippet lifted from a programming forum: small, cited pieces are fine, and anything beyond that is not, unless an assignment says otherwise. You remain responsible for any error the tool introduces.

Things worth noticing

  • The per-question time budget is the hidden difficulty setting. A midterm gives 1.8 to 2.0 minutes a question; the final gives three. The final covers twice as much material with at least half again as much thinking time per item — which usually makes it the more comfortable paper despite being comprehensive.
  • The note sheet allowance scales with coverage, so it is not a bonus. Two midterm sheets cover ten and six modules; two final sheets cover twenty-one. Per module you have less room on the final, which is why it should be a compression of the midterm sheets rather than a fresh document.
  • Coursework is worth less than it feels. Homework, project and syllabus quiz together are 25% — the weight of one quiz. That is not an argument for skipping homework, which is where the practice happens; it is an argument against letting homework crowd out quiz revision.
  • Two rules produce zeroes rather than deductions. Missing a quiz window and skipping peer assessments. Neither is recoverable, and both are calendar problems rather than knowledge problems.
  • The stated course goal is not recall. The syllabus says the aim is choosing an appropriate model for a question, specifying the data it needs, knowing what its answer will and will not tell you, and judging whether someone else’s modelling was reasonable. Questions written from that goal reward knowing when a method applies far more than knowing its derivation.

Before each quiz

  • Confirm the proctoring setup works — well before the window, not on the day.
  • Book a slot early in the eleven-day window so a failure still leaves room to re-sit within it.
  • Hand-write the note sheet at the allowed size and do one timed run using only that sheet.
  • Have notes and blank scratch paper ready to show both sides during the room scan.
  • Leave the calculator in a drawer — none of the questions need one.
  • For midterm 2, refresh modules 1 to 10 for the ideas even though they are not the focus.
  • For the final, give modules 17 to 21 a proper pass — it is their only appearance.
