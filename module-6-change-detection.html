Lesson 1

Change detection

When the unusual points stop being unusual, the question changes from Module 5’s “is this point wrong?” to “has the whole process moved?” — and the answer has to arrive early enough to act on.

What it is

Change detection

Determining whether something has changed. Because change is the subject, the data is nearly always a time series, or at least carries some time attribute.

The questions it answers fall into three families.

FamilyExamples
Do we need to do something?Does a machine showing variation in performance need maintenance, or is this just random variation? Has global temperature changed? Have sales of a product fallen because a new competitor arrived?
Did the action we took have an effect?Did a policy change produce the intended result — did lowering business tax rates lead firms to hire more people? Did a discount promotion actually move sales?
What has drifted over time?Have voting patterns shifted? Anything where the point is to understand the change in order to plan or analyse for the future.

The first family is the demanding one, because acting late is expensive. The other two can usually be answered at leisure with all the data in hand.

Detecting a problem that isn’t a problem yet

One class of application is harder than it looks: you need to detect a change now that predicts trouble later, even though nothing is wrong at the moment.

Traffic on a busy stretch of motorway is the standard illustration. Loop sensors in the road report vehicle counts and average speeds every few seconds. A jam does not appear from nowhere — density builds, speeds start to vary more, and gaps between vehicles shorten, for several minutes before flow actually collapses. By the time speed has dropped, the queue exists and the options for preventing it are gone. The useful question is therefore not “is traffic bad now?” but “is what I am seeing the beginning of a build-up that will be bad in five minutes?”

Why this is a change-detection problem rather than a prediction problem

You are not forecasting a number. You are asking whether the current stream of readings still comes from the same regime it did an hour ago. Once you accept that framing, the method has to work on a running basis, with the data that has arrived so far and no more.

The noise problem, and why a hypothesis test isn’t enough

Consider condition monitoring on a rotating machine — a large pump or motor, with a temperature probe on the bearing housing. Bearings usually fail gradually, and temperature rises before they seize. If the rise can be caught early enough, the machine can be stopped and the bearing replaced during planned downtime instead of failing in service and taking the line with it.

In the ideal case the first high reading would be a reliable warning: the temperature is genuinely elevated, the machine comes out of service, and the failure is prevented.

Real sensor readings do not behave like that. There is a great deal of random fluctuation between readings — ambient temperature, load, probe contact, plain measurement noise. Individual high readings appear routinely and mean nothing. The difficulty is symmetrical: react to every high reading and you stop a healthy machine repeatedly, but wait until you are really sure and the bearing may already have failed.

A natural first attempt is a hypothesis test: has the mean temperature risen, at the usual level of significance? The trouble is timing. Waiting until a test meets a standard threshold means waiting for enough evidence to be confident, and with data this noisy that can take longer than the component has left. The test gives the right answer too late to be worth anything.

The trade that defines the topic

What is needed is a method that identifies potential problems sooner, on the explicit understanding that some of its alarms will be false. Accepting false positives is not a weakness of the approach — it is the price of an answer arriving in time.

Lesson 2

CUSUM

CUSUM accumulates how far above expectation the data has been running, with C setting how much evidence is dismissed as noise and T setting how much is needed before you call it a change.

The question it answers

CUSUM — cumulative sum

A running-total method that answers one question: has the mean of the observed distribution gone beyond a critical level? It can detect a move to a higher level, a move to a lower level, or both at once.

Set up the notation. Let xt be the observed value at time t — a share price on day t, a bearing temperature at reading t, a reaction time on trial t. Let μ be the expected value of those observations if nothing has changed. Then xt − μ is how far above expectation the process ran at time t.

St  =  max { 0 ,   St−1 + ( xt − μ − C ) }    with S0 = 0

declare a change when   St ≥ T Three moving parts. The running total carries evidence forward; C subtracts a little each period so ordinary noise doesn’t accumulate; the max with zero throws the total away whenever it would go negative.

Why each piece is there

  1. The running total. A single high reading proves nothing; several in a row is evidence. Adding each period’s excess to the previous total is what turns a sequence of weak signals into a strong one.
  2. The reset at zero. If the total would go below zero, set it to zero instead. A run of lower-than-expected observations is irrelevant to the question of whether an increase happens later, and letting it bank negative credit would delay the eventual detection. This is what lets CUSUM react quickly.
  3. The constant C. Roughly half the time, an observation will exceed the expectation purely at random. Without something pulling the total down, that noise alone would eventually push St past any threshold. C is the amount of excess dismissed as noise each period.

How the two parameters behave

The bigger C is, the harder it is for St to get large, and the less sensitive the method. The smaller C is, the more sensitive, because St grows faster. T works the same way in the other place: a high threshold means more evidence before an alarm, so later detection and fewer false ones. Neither has a formula — they are model parameters you fit to data.

put in ordersolved

Drag one period of the CUSUM calculation into sequence — or use the arrows.

  1. Observe xt, the value at this time period
  2. Work out xt − μ, how far above expectation it ran
  3. Subtract C from that, then add the result to the previous total St−1
  4. If the result is below zero, reset the total to zero
  5. Compare the new total St against the threshold T
  6. Declare a change if St has reached T; otherwise wait for the next period

Exam target

Write the CUSUM recursion for detecting an increase, from memory.

Show answer

St = max{0, St−1 + (xt − μ − C)}, starting from S0 = 0, with a change declared as soon as St ≥ T.

Choosing C and T is a cost question

There is no simple answer for what C and T should be. Part of the decision is statistical, and part of it is about which mistake hurts more.

Take a chip manufacturing process where a machine has begun placing a component slightly too far to the left. Every chip produced between the moment the drift starts and the moment it is detected has to be scrapped, so late detection costs material and time. But if CUSUM calls a change when the machine is fine, production stops while a crew inspects and adjusts equipment that was working. Setting C and T is trading those two costs against each other.

If you…Then…
Lower C or lower TChanges are detected sooner, and false alarms become more frequent. Right when late detection is the expensive mistake.
Raise C or raise TFalse alarms become rarer, and real changes take longer to surface. Right when interrupting a healthy process is the expensive mistake.

Check

Both the C = 0 and C = 15 settings detect the change against T = 150. Why prefer the second?

Show answer

It costs one extra period of delay and removes almost all the false-alarm risk: the pre-change total peaks at 23 rather than 119. Whether that trade is right depends on the costs, but here it is cheap.

Detecting decreases, and both at once

Nothing about the method is specific to increases. Flip the sign inside the sum and the same machinery looks for a drop:

increase:   St  =  max { 0 ,   St−1 + ( xt − μ − C ) }
decrease:   St  =  max { 0 ,   St−1 + ( μ − xt − C ) } Same threshold comparison in both cases. To watch for a change in either direction, run both recursions simultaneously on the same data and alarm if either one reaches its threshold.

CUSUM is mathematically simple enough to implement in a spreadsheet — one column for the observations, one for the excess, one for the running total, and a comparison against T. That simplicity is a large part of why it is the method taught first.

What to take from this lesson

CUSUM is one method among several, and the course does not attempt to cover the rest. What generalises is the underlying idea — accumulate evidence over time rather than judging each observation alone — and the trade-off between early detection and false detection, which every change-detection method has to make somewhere.

Things worth noticing

  • This is Module 5’s question at a different time scale. An outlier is one point that doesn’t fit its context; a change is every subsequent point failing to fit the old context. Module 5’s contextual outlier and this module’s level shift differ mainly in how many points are involved.
  • CUSUM is the residual trick with a memory. Module 5 found outliers by fitting a model and looking at where the error was large. CUSUM does the same thing — μ is the model, xt − μ is the residual — except that it accumulates residuals instead of judging each one alone. That is the entire difference, and it is what buys early detection.
  • The reset at zero is the whole idea, compressed. Evidence for an increase is worth keeping; evidence against one is thrown away, because the question is only ever “has it gone up?”. That asymmetry is what makes CUSUM fast, and it is also why C has to exist.
  • Neither lesson tells you how to set anything. C, T and μ are all left to you. Module 3’s validation material is the missing piece, with one twist: “performs well” here has two components — detection delay and false-alarm rate — whose relative weight comes from costs rather than from data.
  • Statistical correctness is not the goal. Lesson 1 rejects hypothesis testing even though it gives the right answer. That is unusual enough to be worth remembering: a method can be correct and still useless, if the deadline is part of the problem.
