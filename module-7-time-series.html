Lesson 1

Exponential smoothing

Today’s best estimate of the true level is a blend of what you just saw and what you believed yesterday — and one number, α, sets the mix.

Time series data, and the three things that move it

Time series data

Data where the same response is recorded for many time periods: the day’s high temperature at one airport, a stock price every two seconds, daily burger sales at one restaurant, a patient’s blood pressure over weeks.

Values like these vary for three different reasons, and a model has to tell them apart:

  • Trend — a steady drift up or down. A stock rising as the company gains market share; burger sales slipping as the menu widens.
  • Cyclical (seasonal) variation — a repeating pattern. Warmer summers, busier weekends.
  • Randomness — plain noise. Some days people simply want more burgers. A blood pressure reading taken during a tense match comes out high.

Randomness is dealt with first, because it hides the other two. If a patient’s reading today is higher than usual, a doctor needs to know: has the underlying level risen, or was today just a noisy day?

Check

Name the three sources of variation in a time series.

Show answer

Trend, cyclical (seasonal) variation, and randomness. Exponential smoothing handles randomness first; Lesson 2 adds the other two.

Two extreme answers, and the blend

xtThe observed response at time t — the reading actually taken
StThe expected baseline at time t — what the level “really” is once noise is removed

Faced with an unusual reading, there are two opposite beliefs you could hold:

  • The reading is the truth: St = xt. The baseline has moved.
  • The reading is luck: St = St−1. Nothing has changed.

Exponential smoothing takes a weighted average of the two:

St = α·xt + (1 − α)·St−1,    0 < α < 1 α is the weight on the new observation; (1 − α) is the weight on yesterday’s belief. At α = 1 you believe every reading; at α = 0 you never update.
Choosing α

Lots of randomness → α near 0. A fluctuation is probably noise, so lean on yesterday’s baseline. Little randomness → α near 1. A fluctuation probably reflects a real change, so trust what you see.

One detail remains: the first period has no yesterday. The standard starting condition is S1 = x1 — take the first reading as the first baseline and build up from there.

Yesterday’s baseline was 120. Today’s reading is 135. With α = 0.2:

St = 0.2 × 135 + 0.8 × 120 = 27 + 96 = 123 A 15-point jump in the reading moves the baseline only 3 points. With α = 0.8 the same reading would give 132.

St always lands between which two numbers?

Show answer

More examples: where exponential smoothing came from, and where it runs today

A little history

The method came out of 1950s operations research, not finance. Robert G. Brown developed simple exponential smoothing for US Navy inventory control. Charles Holt added a separate trend in a 1957 Office of Naval Research memo. Peter Winters tested Holt’s methods on real sales data in 1960 and added the seasonal part, which is why triple smoothing carries both names. Its original purpose — deciding how much stock to hold — is still one of its main uses.

Example: your internet connection runs this equation

Every TCP connection has to decide how long to wait for a reply before assuming a packet was lost. Round-trip times are noisy, so the internet standard for this (RFC 6298) smooths them with exactly the Lesson 1 equation, using α = 1/8:

SRTT ← (1 − 1/8)·SRTT + (1/8)·R′ SRTT is the smoothed round-trip time (our St); R′ is the newest measurement (our xt).

It also smooths how far each measurement lands from the average, with weight 1/4, and waits for the average plus four times that spread. Suppose SRTT = 100 ms, the smoothed spread is 20 ms, and a slow reply takes 180 ms:

Spread0.75 × 20 + 0.25 × |100 − 180| = 15 + 20 = 35 ms
Smoothed RTT0.875 × 100 + 0.125 × 180 = 87.5 + 22.5 = 110 ms
Timeout110 + 4 × 35 = 250 ms (the standard then raises anything below 1 second up to 1 second)

One slow reply moves the average only 10 ms — a small α at work. The spread term is the same idea applied to variability, which is where Lesson 6 ends up.

Try it: move α and watch the baseline

Things to try: drag α to about 0.05 and count how many periods the line takes to accept the step up. Then drag it to 0.95 and see how much of the noise it copies. Somewhere in between is the trade-off the optimizer in Lesson 4 settles for you.

Worth noticing

TCP uses α = 1/8. What does that choice say about how its designers view a single slow reply?

Show answer

That it is mostly noise. A small α means one unusual round-trip time moves the estimate only an eighth of the way, so the timeout doesn’t lurch around with every delayed packet.

Lesson 2

Trends and cycles

Trend and seasonality get the same treatment as the baseline: each has its own running estimate, its own weight, and its own starting value.

Adding a trend

Let Tt be the trend at time t — how much the baseline changes per period. Two things change.

First, “yesterday’s belief” now has to include yesterday’s trend. If the level was 100 and rising by 5 a period, the prior guess for today is 105, not 100:

St = α·xt + (1 − α)·(St−1 + Tt−1)

Second, the trend itself is smoothed the same way. The observed trend this period is how much the baseline just moved, St − St−1, and it is blended with the previous trend estimate using a new constant β:

Tt = β·(St − St−1) + (1 − β)·Tt−1,    0 < β < 1 Starting condition: T1 = 0 — assume no trend until the data says otherwise, just as S1 = x1 assumes nothing about the baseline.
Check

What is the “observed trend” that β weights?

Show answer

The change in the baseline estimate from last period to this one, St − St−1 — not the change in the raw observations, which would carry all the noise with it.

Adding seasonality — multiplicatively

A cyclic effect could be added as another additive term, and sometimes that is the right choice. The lecture shows the alternative, a multiplicative seasonal factor, so both are in your toolbox.

LThe length of one cycle. Daily readings with a weekly cycle: L = 7. Hourly readings with a daily cycle: L = 24.
CtThe seasonal factor for period t. Above 1 inflates, below 1 deflates.

The observation is divided by its seasonal factor before it is blended in — that strips the season out so the baseline sees only the underlying level:

St = α·(xt / Ct−L) + (1 − α)·(St−1 + Tt−1) Why Ct−L and not Ct−1? It is the most recent factor from the same stage of the cycle. If today is Monday, use last Monday’s factor.

The factor is then updated with a third constant, γ, blending “how far above the baseline was this period?” with the old factor for this stage:

Ct = γ·(xt / St) + (1 − γ)·Ct−L,    0 < γ < 1 Starting condition: the first L factors are all 1 — no seasonal effect assumed — and the data fills in the rest.

Reading a factor

C = 1.1 on a Sunday means Sunday sales run 10% above baseline just because it is Sunday. Sell 550 burgers that day and 500 of them are baseline; the other 50 are the Sunday lift.

Worth noticing

When would an additive seasonal term fit better than a multiplicative one?

Show answer

When the seasonal swing stays roughly the same size in absolute terms as the level changes — say, always about 50 extra units on Sundays. Multiplicative fits when the swing grows with the level — always about 10% extra. (This rule of thumb is standard practice rather than lecture content.)

More examples: real data with each kind of seasonality

Multiplicative: airline passengers, 1949–1960

The classic example in almost every time series textbook is Box and Jenkins’ monthly totals of international airline passengers. It is small, real, and has all three components at once.

Read across the table. The average month grew about 3.7 times, and the absolute swing grew more than 5 times. The ratio of peak to trough stayed in a narrow band around 1.4–1.6. A seasonal effect that stays roughly constant as a proportion is exactly what a multiplicative factor Ct describes. Lesson 4 fits both versions to this data and compares them.

Additive: carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa

The Keeling curve — atmospheric CO₂ measured on Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958 — has a strong trend and a yearly cycle. Northern Hemisphere plants pull CO₂ out of the air as they grow in spring and summer, and it returns as they die back. The peak-to-trough swing averages roughly 6 ppm. While the level rose from about 316 ppm to over 420 ppm, that swing changed only modestly. A swing that stays about the same size while the level climbs fits an additive seasonal term better than a multiplicative one.

More than one cycle: electricity demand

Half-hourly electricity demand has a daily cycle and a weekly one. Basic Holt-Winters allows one L. In 2003, James Taylor extended the method to two seasonal cycles for exactly this problem. When your data has several cycles, look for “double seasonal” or “multiple seasonal” versions in your software.

Lesson 3

Where the name comes from

“Smoothing” because spikes get pulled toward the old baseline; “exponential” because, unrolled, every past observation is in there with a weight that shrinks by a constant factor each step back.

Smoothing

Take any α strictly between 0 and 1 — think of 0.5. A very high reading xt is only counted with weight α, and the other (1 − α) goes to the older, lower baseline, so St ends up below the spike. A very low reading gets pulled up the same way. Peaks shave down, valleys fill in, and the line through them is smoother than the data.

The trend and seasonal equations work exactly the same way, so everything in this lesson applies to them too.

Exponential

St−1 is itself given by the same equation: St−1 = αxt−1 + (1 − α)St−2. Substitute that in, then do the same for St−2, St−3, and so on:

St = αxt + α(1−α)xt−1 + α(1−α)²xt−2 + α(1−α)³xt−3 + … + (1−α)kSt−k The weight on the observation k periods back is α(1−α)k. The exponent rises by one each step — that is the “exponential”.
Exam target

What weight does xt−3 receive in St?

Show answer

α(1 − α)³. With α = 0.5 that is 0.0625; with α = 0.2 it is 0.1024 — the smaller α gives more weight to an observation three periods old.

What the unrolled form reveals

The one-line equation makes it look as if only the latest observation matters. The expanded form shows the truth:

  • Every past observation contributes to St. The older ones are baked into St−1.
  • Recent observations count more. Because 1 − α < 1, each step back multiplies the weight by something smaller than one.

So the method has a long memory and a recency bias at the same time — which is usually exactly what you want when estimating what is happening now.

Worth noticing

Do the weights α(1−α)k add up to 1?

Show answer

Over an infinitely long history, yes — it is a geometric series: α / (1 − (1 − α)) = 1. Over a finite history the leftover (1 − α)k sits on the starting value S1 = x1. That leftover is why the choice of starting value fades in importance as data accumulates.

More examples: how long is α’s memory?

Two numbers make α concrete. The half-life is how many periods back the weight falls to half of today’s weight: ln(0.5) / ln(1 − α). The average age of the data behind St — the weighted average of how many periods old each observation is — works out to (1 − α) / α.

αWeight on todayHalf-lifeAverage age of the dataShare of weight on the last 10 periods
0.110%6.6 periods9 periods65%
0.220%3.1 periods4 periods89%
0.330%1.9 periods2.3 periods97%
0.550%1 period1 period99.9%
0.880%0.4 periods0.25 periods≈100%

So α = 0.1 on daily data behaves like an average over roughly the last week and a half, and α = 0.5 is essentially looking at yesterday and today. When someone gives you a fitted α, translating it into an average age is the quickest way to explain it to a non-specialist.

Compared with a simple moving average

A 5-day moving average gives each of the last five days a weight of 0.2 and every earlier day a weight of 0. That cliff causes a quirk: when a big value drops out of the window, the average jumps, even though nothing happened today. Exponential weights fade smoothly instead, so there is no drop-out jump. They also need only one stored number, St−1, rather than the whole window — a big reason the method suited 1950s inventory systems.

Lesson 4

Forecasting with exponential smoothing

You don’t know next period’s observation, so your best guess for it is your current baseline — and that single substitution turns a smoother into a forecaster.

The one-step-ahead forecast

Write the smoothing equation for the next period, t + 1:

St+1 = α·xt+1 + (1 − α)·St

xt+1 hasn’t happened yet. The best guess for it is the current baseline St. Substitute, and the α terms cancel:

Ft+1 = α·St + (1 − α)·St = St The forecast for next period is simply the latest baseline estimate.

The same argument applies to t + 2, t + 3 and beyond, so with baseline only the forecast is a flat line at St. What does change is the uncertainty: the further ahead you look, the larger the expected forecast error — which is the widening band in the picture at the top of this page.

Check

Using single exponential smoothing, what is the forecast five periods ahead?

Show answer

St — the same as the one-period-ahead forecast. Only the expected size of the error grows.

With trend, and with seasonality

The best estimate of next period’s baseline is the current baseline; the best estimate of next period’s trend is the current trend. So:

Ft+1 = St + Tt

Add multiplicative seasonality, and the best estimate of next period’s factor is the latest one from that same stage of the cycle, C(t+1)−L:

Ft+1 = (St + Tt) · C(t+1)−L

Looking more than one step ahead

The lecture presents the one-step form. In the standard Holt-Winters method the trend is applied once per step, so k periods ahead the forecast is (St + k·Tt) · C(t+k)−L, using the factor from the matching stage of the most recent full cycle. Check which form your software reports.

A worked example

α = 0.2, β = 0.5. Last period: St−1 = 100, Tt−1 = 2. This period’s observation is xt = 120. No seasonality yet.

St = 0.2 × 120 + 0.8 × (100 + 2) = 24 + 81.6 = 105.6
Tt = 0.5 × (105.6 − 100) + 0.5 × 2 = 2.8 + 1 = 3.8
Ft+1 = 105.6 + 3.8 = 109.4 If next period’s stage of the cycle had a factor of 1.2, the forecast would be 109.4 × 1.2 = 131.28.

St = 200, Tt = 4, and the seasonal factor from the matching stage last cycle is 0.9. What is Ft+1?

Exam target

Why does the α term vanish from the single-smoothing forecast?

Show answer

Because the unknown xt+1 is replaced by St, both terms of the weighted average are St, and α + (1 − α) = 1. α still matters — it shaped St — but it doesn’t appear in the forecasting step.

Fitting α, β and γ

Every past period gives a forecast you can check. Measure each one’s squared error, (Ft − xt)², add them up over the training data, and choose the constants that make the total smallest:

minimize   Σt (Ft − xt)²   over α, β, γ   subject to   0 ≤ α, β, γ ≤ 1 This is an optimization problem — the course covers optimization as a topic of its own. Statistical software solves it for you.
put in ordersolved

Put the steps for fitting and using a Holt-Winters model in order. Drag, or use the arrows.

  1. Choose the cycle length L and set S1 = x1, T1 = 0, and the first L factors to 1
  2. Pick trial values of α, β and γ between 0 and 1
  3. Run the update equations through the training data, recording each one-step forecast Ft
  4. Add up the squared errors (Ft − xt)²
  5. Let the optimizer adjust α, β and γ until that total is as small as possible
  6. Forecast forward from the final St, Tt and seasonal factors

Lesson 5

ARIMA

Difference the data until it stops drifting, predict those differences from their own recent past and from recent errors — and exponential smoothing turns out to be one special case.

ARIMA stands for AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average. The lecture covers what it does rather than the theory behind it. It has three parts, one per letter group.

1 · Differences (the “I”)

Stationary

A series whose mean, variance and other measures are expected to stay constant over time.

Exponential smoothing’s unrolled equation estimates St from xt, xt−1, xt−2, … — which works well when the data is stationary. Trend or seasonality break that. But even when the data isn’t stationary, its differences may be:

1st-order differencesxt − xt−1, the change between consecutive observations
2nd-order differencesThe differences of those differences
d-th-order differencesRepeat the differencing d times

2 · Autoregression (the “AR”)

Regression predicts a value from other factors — a child’s adult height from the parents’ heights and the child’s own height at two. Autoregression predicts a value from earlier values of the same thing: the child’s height at eighteen months, at one year, and so on. That only works with time series data.

The unrolled exponential smoothing equation is already an autoregression — one that reaches back through all the data, so it is order ∞. Using only the last p periods gives an order-p autoregressive model.

ARIMA applies the autoregression to the d-th-order differences rather than to the raw observations.

3 · Moving average (the “MA”)

Besides past values, ARIMA also uses past forecast errors as predictors. Going back q periods gives an order-q moving average.

Vocabulary

In ARIMA(p, d, q), which letter controls which part?

Show answer

p — how many past values feed the autoregression. d — how many times the data is differenced. q — how many past errors feed the moving average.

Putting it together

Writing Dt for the d-th-order difference at time t and εt for the forecast error, one common way to write the model is:

Dt = μ + Σi=1..p φi·Dt−i + Σi=1..q θi·εt−i + εt A constant, p autoregressive terms on past differences, and q terms on past errors. Sign conventions for the θ terms differ between textbooks and software; the structure is what matters.

Once you choose p, d and q, software finds the best constants — again by solving an optimization problem — and does it quickly. So you can try several (p, d, q) combinations and compare them with the validation approaches from earlier modules. Lesson 8 covers how those approaches need adjusting for time series.

Seasonality can be added too. The course doesn’t cover seasonal ARIMA, but good software does it for you.

Familiar models in disguise

Particular choices of p, d and q reproduce simpler models. That is part of ARIMA’s power: one framework generalises many.

More examples: seeing smoothing inside ARIMA

Rewrite smoothing as “last forecast plus a correction”

Since Ft+1 = St, the smoothing equation can be rearranged:

Ft+1 = αxt + (1 − α)Ft = Ft + α·(xt − Ft) New forecast = old forecast + α × last error. This is called the error-correction form.

Now look at it through ARIMA’s eyes. The change in the series, xt+1 − xt, is a first difference (d = 1). Working through the algebra, that difference is explained by the current random error plus a multiple of the previous error (q = 1), with no past values needed (p = 0). That is ARIMA(0, 1, 1). α is doing the job of the moving-average coefficient.

Example with α = 0.3: last forecast 200, actual 220, error +20. New forecast = 200 + 0.3 × 20 = 206. A single surprise shifts every future forecast by 30% of its size.

Example: an AR(1) model forecast

Weekly deviations of a warehouse’s temperature from its setpoint follow Dt = 0.6·Dt−1 + εt (ARIMA(1, 0, 0), no constant). This week’s deviation is +5°.

next week: 0.6 × 5 = 3.0    in two weeks: 0.6 × 3.0 = 1.8    in three: 1.08 … → 0 A stationary AR(1) forecast decays back toward the mean. Contrast the random walk below, whose forecast never decays.

Example: stock prices as a random walk

Daily closing prices are often close to ARIMA(0, 1, 0): tomorrow = today + an unpredictable change. The best forecast for any future day is today’s price, and the uncertainty grows with the horizon. That is why the price itself is hard to forecast — and why Lesson 6 turns to forecasting how variable the changes will be.

Example: the “airline model”

Box and Jenkins modelled the Lesson 2 airline data with what became known as the airline model, written ARIMA(0, 1, 1)(0, 1, 1)12 and fitted to the log of the passenger counts. The second bracket is the seasonal extension the lecture mentions:

logTurns the growing, proportional swings into constant-size ones
(0, 1, 1)One ordinary difference and one past error — smoothing from month to month
(0, 1, 1)12One difference against the same month last year, and one past error from 12 months back — smoothing from year to year

Fitted on 1949–1958 for this page, its two error coefficients came out at about −0.32 and −0.57, similar to published fits. Its 1959–60 forecasts had a MAPE of 8.4% (RMSE 43) — in the same range as Holt-Winters in Lesson 4, with just two parameters. Lesson 7 shows how the correlation plots point to this exact model.

calculationanswered

Using the error-correction form with α = 0.25: last period’s forecast was 80 and the actual value was 72. What is the new forecast?

Check

An AR(1) forecast fades toward the mean; a random-walk forecast stays flat at today’s value. What single difference in the model causes that?

Show answer

The random walk is differenced (d = 1), so shocks are permanent. The stationary AR(1) has a coefficient below 1 on the undifferenced data, so each shock’s effect shrinks every period.

Lesson 6

GARCH

The same machinery as ARIMA, pointed at variance instead of value — so you can say not just what will happen, but how uncertain that is.

GARCH stands for Generalized AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity. It estimates or forecasts the variance of something recorded as a time series.

Why forecast variance at all?

  • Size the error around a forecast. A pickup truck maker forecasting demand needs to know how far above or below the true figure the forecast might land, so it can plan production and stock.
  • Measure risk in investing. Classic portfolio optimization balances expected return against volatility. Some investors accept more risk for more expected return; others trade return for safety. Variance is the usual stand-in for volatility — and GARCH is a common way to estimate it.
Vocabulary

What does “heteroscedasticity” mean?

Show answer

Variance that isn’t constant. “Conditional” adds that the variance at time t depends on what happened in earlier periods. (The word breakdown is standard usage rather than lecture content.)

What the model looks like

The lecture doesn’t go deep into the mathematics, but the structure is:

σt² = ω + Σi=1..p βi·σt−i² + Σi=1..q γi·εt−i² Today’s variance from a constant, p past variances, and q past squared errors. Software packages differ over which order is written first — check the documentation before reading off p and q.

Set that beside ARIMA and there are only two differences:

Variances, not valuesGARCH works with variances σ² and squared errors ε², where ARIMA works with observations and plain errors.
No differencingGARCH uses the variances directly, never differences of them. So there is no d: the model is GARCH(p, q), not GARCH(p, d, q).

As with ARIMA, good software fits the constants for any p and q you choose. GARCH(1, 1) is the common default in practice.

quick checkanswered

Why does a GARCH model have two order parameters when ARIMA has three?

An investment team wants next week’s volatility estimate for each fund to feed a risk-versus-return portfolio model. Which method fits the question?

The three methods side by side

Exponential smoothing — estimates and forecasts the value, with optional trend and seasonality. ARIMA — generalises exponential smoothing for the value. GARCH — an ARIMA-like model for the variance. Other approaches exist, but these are the most common and most generally useful.

Exam target

Name the two structural differences between GARCH and ARIMA.

Show answer

More examples: GARCH in numbers

Where it came from

Robert Engle introduced ARCH in 1982: model today’s variance from past squared errors. His student Tim Bollerslev added past variances in 1986 and called it generalized ARCH — GARCH. Engle shared the 2003 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for methods of analysing time series whose volatility changes over time. The pattern he captured is that markets swing between calm spells and turbulent ones.

Worked example: one day of GARCH(1, 1)

Measure daily returns in percent. A fitted model has ω = 0.05, weight β = 0.85 on yesterday’s variance and weight γ = 0.10 on yesterday’s squared surprise. Yesterday’s variance estimate was 1.0 (a 1% standard deviation).

Big move: return −3%σ² = 0.05 + 0.85 × 1.0 + 0.10 × 9 = 1.80, so σ ≈ 1.34%
Quiet day: return 0.5%σ² = 0.05 + 0.85 × 1.0 + 0.10 × 0.25 = 0.925, so σ ≈ 0.96%

One large surprise raises tomorrow’s expected spread by a third. That is volatility clustering expressed as an equation.

Three more quantities fall straight out of the constants:

Persistenceβ + γ = 0.95. It must be below 1 for the variance to settle; close to 1 means shocks linger.
Long-run varianceω / (1 − β − γ) = 0.05 / 0.05 = 1.0, a 1% daily standard deviation. With about 252 trading days a year, that is roughly 1% × √252 ≈ 16% a year.
Half-life of a shockln(0.5) / ln(0.95) ≈ 13.5 trading days for the extra variance to halve — the same half-life idea as Lesson 3.

The link back to Lesson 1

Drop ω and make the two weights add to 1, and GARCH(1, 1) becomes:

σt² = λ·σt−1² + (1 − λ)·εt−1² That is exponential smoothing applied to squared returns — with λ playing the role of (1 − α). J.P. Morgan’s RiskMetrics system popularised this version in the 1990s with λ = 0.94 for daily data.

Full GARCH adds ω, which pulls the variance back toward its long-run level over time. Exponential smoothing of squared returns has no long-run level to return to.

How the forecast gets used: value at risk

Banks use volatility forecasts like these in value-at-risk calculations — the loss a portfolio should exceed only with some small probability — which feed regulatory capital requirements. The Nobel committee’s own illustration: $1 million held in the S&P 500 on 31 July 2002 had, by such a model, a 99% chance of losing no more than about $61,500 the next day.

calculationanswered

A GARCH(1, 1) has ω = 0.04, β = 0.90 on the past variance and γ = 0.05 on the past squared error. Yesterday’s variance was 0.8 and yesterday’s return was 2%. What is today’s variance, and what is the long-run variance?

Check

What happens to a GARCH(1, 1) forecast of variance many days ahead?

Show answer

It moves toward the long-run variance ω / (1 − β − γ), faster when persistence β + γ is smaller. A turbulent day raises the near-term forecast, but not the distant one.

Lesson 7

Stationarity and choosing p, d, q

Difference just enough to stop the drift, read the leftover correlation to suggest p and q, then let validation make the final call.

Beyond the lectures

The lecture says software fits the constants once you pick p, d and q. This lesson adds the standard ways of picking them. It draws on common forecasting practice, not on lecture content.

Spotting non-stationarity

  • The mean drifts — a trend, or a level that wanders and never comes back.
  • The pattern repeats — seasonality means the expected value depends on the stage of the cycle.
  • The spread changes — swings get bigger as the level rises, or come in bursts.

Differencing fixes the first two. It does not fix the third. A spread that grows with the level is usually handled by taking logs first; a spread that comes in bursts is what GARCH models.

How many differences?

Difference once and look again. A straight-line trend disappears after one difference; a curving (quadratic) trend needs two.

Series3, 7, 11, 15, 19
1st differences4, 4, 4, 4 — constant, so d = 1 is enough

Stop as soon as the series looks stationary. Differencing more than needed adds noise and makes forecasts worse, so the usual choice is d = 0, 1, or occasionally 2.

calculationanswered

A series runs 2, 5, 10, 17, 26, 37. What is the smallest d that makes it stationary?

Check

A series’ swings double in size as its level doubles. Will differencing make it stationary?

Show answer

Not on its own. Differencing removes a drifting mean, not a growing spread. Take logs first so a proportional change becomes a constant-size change, then difference if needed.

Suggesting p and q

Two plots of the differenced series do most of the work:

ACF (autocorrelation function)How strongly the series correlates with itself k periods back, including indirect links through the periods in between.
PACF (partial autocorrelation)The same, but with the in-between periods’ influence removed — only the direct link.
PatternACFPACF
AR(p)Dies away graduallyCuts off sharply after lag p
MA(q)Cuts off sharply after lag qDies away gradually
Mixed ARMADies awayDies away — try small p and q and compare

Real plots are rarely this clean, so treat them as a shortlist. Information criteria such as AIC and BIC then score each candidate on fit with a penalty for extra parameters. Automated searches, such as auto.arima in R, do exactly that. Whatever they pick, confirm it on held-out data — Lesson 8.

Worth noticing

Stock prices usually need d = 1. What is GARCH normally fitted to, and how does that relate?

Show answer

To returns — the period-to-period percentage change in price. A return is essentially a first difference of the log price. So GARCH “needs no d” partly because the differencing was done before the data reached it.

More examples: finding the airline model from the data

Here is the Lesson 7 procedure run on the real airline series. These correlations were computed for this page.

  1. Look at the raw series. Its autocorrelations start at 0.95 and fade very slowly (still 0.71 at lag 13). That slow fade is the signature of a trend — not stationary.
  2. Fix the growing swing first. Take logs, so a proportional seasonal effect becomes a constant-size one.
  3. Take one ordinary difference. The trend goes, but a huge spike remains at lag 12 (0.84), and again at lag 24 (0.74). Each month still looks most like the same month last year — the season is still in the data.
  4. Take a seasonal difference as well (this month minus the same month last year). Now almost every bar sits inside the noise band, except two negative spikes: lag 1 (−0.34) and lag 12 (−0.39).
  5. Read the spikes. An ACF that cuts off after lag 1 suggests one MA term. A spike at exactly lag 12 suggests one seasonal MA term. With one ordinary and one seasonal difference, that is ARIMA(0, 1, 1)(0, 1, 1)12 — the airline model from Lesson 5.

The log step matters for Holt-Winters too. Fitting additive Holt-Winters to the logged series and converting back gave a 1959–60 MAPE of 2.2% (RMSE 14) — far better than either version on the raw counts in Lesson 4. Logging turned the multiplicative pattern into a clean additive one.

diagnosisanswered

Monthly ice-cream sales, after one ordinary difference, still show an ACF spike of 0.8 at lag 12. What should you do next?

Check

Why take logs before differencing in the airline example, rather than after?

Show answer

Differences of the raw counts would still have swings that grow over time. Taking logs first makes those swings a constant size, so the differences that follow can be stationary. A log difference is also roughly a percentage change, which is easy to interpret.

Lesson 8

Judging a forecast

Validate in time order, score errors in units people understand, and always check you beat the lazy forecast.

Beyond the lectures

The lecture points to the validation approaches from earlier modules and says time series quality measures are covered later. This lesson shows how the earlier approaches change when order matters. It follows standard forecasting practice.

Why ordinary cross-validation cheats

Random k-fold cross-validation shuffles rows into folds. With time series, that means training on March and July to “predict” May. Neighbouring periods are strongly related, so the model has effectively seen the answer. The error it reports is optimistic, sometimes wildly so.

The fix: always train on the past and test on the future. The simplest version holds out the last stretch of data. A better version repeats that many times:

put in ordersolved

Put the steps of walk-forward validation in order.

  1. Fit the model on the first stretch of data, say periods 1 to 40
  2. Forecast period 41
  3. Record the error against what actually happened in period 41
  4. Add period 41 to the training data and refit
  5. Repeat until the end of the data is reached
  6. Average the recorded errors and compare candidate models on that average

Exam target

In one sentence, what rule must any time series validation scheme obey?

Show answer

Every forecast being scored must be made using only data from before the period it forecasts.

Scoring the errors

With error et = Ft − xt:

MAE — mean absolute errorAverage size of the miss, in the data’s own units. Easy to explain.
RMSE — root mean squared errorSquare root of the average squared miss. Punishes big misses more; matches the squared-error fitting in Lesson 4.
MAPE — mean absolute percentage errorAverage of |et| / xt. Unit-free, so it compares across series — but breaks down when any actual value is zero or near zero.

Three periods, actuals 100, 110, 90; forecasts 105, 100, 95. Errors: +5, −10, +5.

MAE = (5 + 10 + 5) / 3 ≈ 6.67    RMSE = √((25 + 100 + 25) / 3) = √50 ≈ 7.07    MAPE ≈ 6.5% Note that the plain average error is (5 − 10 + 5) / 3 = 0. Signed errors cancel, which is why accuracy is never measured on them — though their average is a useful check for bias.

The benchmarks to beat

  • Naive: Ft+1 = xt. Next period equals this period. That is ARIMA(0, 1, 0), or exponential smoothing with α = 1.
  • Seasonal naive: Ft+1 = x(t+1)−L. Next Monday equals last Monday.

A model that can’t beat these on walk-forward validation hasn’t earned its complexity.

Finally, check what the model leaves behind. If the model has captured the pattern, the leftover errors should look like white noise — ARIMA(0, 0, 0). Remaining correlation means there is structure left to model.

Check

Why is MAPE a poor choice for daily sales of a slow-moving spare part?

Show answer

Many days have zero sales, and MAPE divides by the actual value. On those days it is undefined, and near-zero days blow it up. MAE or a scaled error measure works better.

More examples: what large forecasting competitions found

Since 1982, Spyros Makridakis has run the “M” competitions, which test many forecasting methods on the same large set of real series. The fourth, M4, finished in 2018 with 100,000 series and 61 methods.

  • Pure machine-learning methods did worse than standard statistical ones such as ARIMA and exponential smoothing, and worse still than combinations of them.
  • The winner was a hybrid. Slawek Smyl, then at Uber, combined exponential smoothing with a recurrent neural network: the smoothing handled level and season, and the network learned the rest.
  • The key benchmark was a simple average. “Comb” averaged the forecasts of single, Holt and damped-trend exponential smoothing.
M4 method (all 100,000 series)sMAPE
Single exponential smoothing13.09%
Holt (with trend)13.77%
Damped-trend smoothing12.66%
Comb — the average of the three12.55%

Two surprises for a student. Adding a trend hurt on average: Holt projects the latest trend forever, and real trends tend to fade. The damped version shrinks the trend a little each step ahead and did better. And averaging three simple methods beat each of them — combining forecasts is one of the most reliable improvements in the field.

The airline comparison in Lesson 4 used a single 24-month holdout — one forecast origin. Walk-forward validation, with many origins, would give a steadier ranking, especially where the models are as close as the two Holt-Winters versions were.

interpretationanswered

In M4, Holt’s trend method was less accurate overall than single exponential smoothing, and damped-trend smoothing beat both. What is the most likely explanation?

Worth noticing

Why might averaging three forecasts beat the best one of them?

Show answer

Their errors are partly independent, so they partly cancel. The average is also safer: you don’t have to know in advance which method will be best for a given series, and picking wrong is costly.

Section B — Short answer and discussion, final style

Each of these is worth roughly 4–8 marks in a typical paper. Write the answer out before opening it.

B1 · Explain the name. Starting from St = αxt + (1 − α)St−1, show why the method is called “exponential smoothing”, and state what the expanded form reveals about how past data is used.

Model answer

Smoothing: with 0 < α < 1, a high reading is weighted by only α and pulled down by the older baseline; a low reading is pulled up the same way. Peaks and valleys are damped.

Exponential: substitute St−1 = αxt−1 + (1 − α)St−2 and keep going: St = αxt + α(1−α)xt−1 + α(1−α)²xt−2 + … The weight on the observation k periods back is α(1−α)k — an exponent that rises by one each step back.

What it reveals: every past observation contributes, not just the latest; and because 1 − α < 1, newer observations get more weight than older ones.

Marking notes. Most marks go to the substitution and the general weight α(1−α)k. Saying “older data gets less weight” without showing the substitution usually earns about half. Pointing out that the one-line equation hides the older data is what separates good answers from complete ones.

B2 · Compute by hand. With α = 0.5, smooth the series 10, 14, 12, 18. Give S1 to S4, the forecast for period 5, and the sum of squared one-step errors for periods 2 to 4.

Model answer

S1 = 10 (starting condition). S2 = 0.5·14 + 0.5·10 = 12. S3 = 0.5·12 + 0.5·12 = 12. S4 = 0.5·18 + 0.5·12 = 15. F5 = S4 = 15.

One-step forecasts are F2 = S1 = 10, F3 = S2 = 12, F4 = S3 = 12. Errors: 10 − 14 = −4, 12 − 12 = 0, 12 − 18 = −6. Sum of squares = 16 + 0 + 36 = 52.

Marking notes. The common slip is scoring St against xt. St has already seen xt, so that isn’t a forecast. The error for period t uses the forecast made at t − 1. Stating S1 = x1 explicitly earns its own mark.

B3 · Explain Holt-Winters. Write the three update equations for triple exponential smoothing with multiplicative seasonality. Explain what each term does, why Ct−L is used, and give the starting conditions.

Model answer

St = α(xt/Ct−L) + (1−α)(St−1 + Tt−1) — blend the de-seasonalised observation with last period’s baseline projected forward by the trend.

Tt = β(St − St−1) + (1−β)Tt−1 — blend the observed change in baseline with the previous trend.

Ct = γ(xt/St) + (1−γ)Ct−L — blend this period’s observed ratio to baseline with the previous factor for the same stage.

Ct−L is the most recent factor from the same point in the cycle (last Monday for a Monday). Starting conditions: S1 = x1, T1 = 0, and C1 … CL = 1.

Marking notes. Two details are commonly missed: the trend is added to St−1 inside the (1 − α) term, and the observation is divided by the factor. Starting conditions for all three components are usually a separate mark.

B4 · Choosing α. Explain the trade-off α controls. How should the amount of randomness in a system guide it, and how is it chosen in practice?

Model answer

α trades trust in the new reading against trust in the existing baseline. A large α reacts fast to real changes but also chases noise. A small α filters noise but is slow to recognise a real shift.

High randomness means most fluctuations are noise, so α should be small. Low randomness means fluctuations probably reflect real change, so α should be large.

In practice α (with β and γ) is chosen by minimising the sum of squared one-step forecast errors over the training data, subject to staying between 0 and 1 — and the result is then checked on held-out data.

Marking notes. Full marks name both costs — noise-chasing and lag. Answers that say only “small α for noisy data” miss what the small α costs.

B5 · ARIMA in words. Explain the three components of ARIMA(p, d, q), and give three special cases with the models they correspond to.

Model answer

d — integrated: difference the data d times so a non-stationary series becomes stationary. p — autoregressive: predict the differenced value from its own previous p values. q — moving average: also use the previous q forecast errors as predictors.

Special cases: ARIMA(0,0,0) is white noise; ARIMA(0,1,0) is a random walk; ARIMA(0,1,1) is basic exponential smoothing. (Also acceptable: ARIMA(p,0,0) is a pure AR model; ARIMA(0,0,q) is a pure MA model.)

Marking notes. A key mark is saying that the autoregression runs on the differences, not the raw data. Naming ARIMA(0,1,1) as exponential smoothing is the special case examiners most often look for, because it links the two halves of the module.

B6 · GARCH. What does GARCH estimate, why would a business want that, and how does its structure differ from ARIMA’s?

Model answer

GARCH estimates and forecasts the variance of a time series. Variance tells you how far a forecast might miss, which matters for planning (production, stock). In investing it stands in for volatility, which portfolio optimization balances against expected return.

Structurally it resembles ARIMA with two changes: it models variances and squared errors rather than values and plain errors, and it does not difference, so there is no d — the model is GARCH(p, q).

Marking notes. Many answers explain the structure and skip the why. The portfolio-risk use is the example most papers expect. Giving the reason there is no d, not just the fact, earns the last mark.

B7 · Validation. Why is shuffled k-fold cross-validation inappropriate for most time series, and what should replace it?

Model answer

Shuffling places later observations in training for earlier test points. Neighbouring periods are strongly related, so the model is partly shown the answer, and the validation error is optimistic.

Use walk-forward validation: fit on data up to time t, forecast t + 1, record the error, add t + 1 to training, and repeat. Average the errors and compare models on that. Compare against a naive or seasonal naive benchmark.

Marking notes. The mark sits in the mechanism — “future information leaks into training” — not in “order matters.” Mentioning the naive benchmark shows you know the error needs a reference point.

B8 · Choosing a method. You have 28 months of spiky monthly sales. A colleague insists on ARIMA because it “includes exponential smoothing as a special case.” Respond.

Model answer

Being more general means ARIMA can represent smoothing, not that it will forecast better. It has more parameters to estimate, and the rule of thumb is about 40 data points — 28 is short. ARIMA also tends to do best on stable data, and this series is spiky.

Start with exponential smoothing, fit a few low-order ARIMA models, and let walk-forward validation against a naive benchmark decide.

Marking notes. Full marks need both lecture criteria — data size and stability — plus a validation-based way to settle it. “Generality is not accuracy” is the core point.

Where the marks hide

Across B2 and B7, what is the shared idea?

Show answer

A forecast only counts if it was made before the answer was known. Scoring St against xt and shuffled cross-validation both break that rule, in different places.

Section C — Multi-part cases, final style

C1 · Emergency department arrivals. A hospital records patient arrivals every hour for two years. Arrivals peak each evening, and the evening peak has grown as overall demand has risen. (a) What is L? (b) State the starting conditions for Holt-Winters. (c) Additive or multiplicative seasonality — justify. (d) Describe a validation plan. (e) The staffing manager asks how many extra nurses to roster “just in case.” What do you need to estimate, and with what?

Model answer

(a) L = 24 for the daily cycle in hourly data. (A weekly pattern would also exist; basic Holt-Winters handles one cycle, so note that as a limitation.)

(b) S1 = x1, T1 = 0, and the first 24 seasonal factors set to 1.

(c) Multiplicative. The evening peak grows as the level grows, so the seasonal effect is proportional rather than a fixed number of patients.

(d) Walk-forward: fit on the first year or so, forecast the next hour or day, record the error, roll forward, repeat. Compare against a seasonal naive forecast (same hour yesterday or last week) using MAE, which staff can read directly as “patients per hour.”

(e) The variance of the forecast error — how far actual arrivals may run above forecast. If that variance changes over time (bursty periods), a GARCH-type model estimates it; the extra staffing follows from that spread.

Marking notes. (c) earns its marks only with the justification “the peak grows with the level.” (e) is the link between the value half and the variance half of the module; answers that just propose a larger point forecast miss it.

C2 · Diagnosing a model from its symptoms. Name the likely cause and the fix. (a) Fitted α is 0.99 and the model barely beats a naive forecast. (b) A model on monthly data leaves errors strongly correlated at lag 12. (c) ARIMA with d = 2 on a series with a straight-line trend gives erratic forecasts. (d) Shuffled cross-validation RMSE is half the walk-forward RMSE.

Model answer

(a) The series behaves close to a random walk, so the latest value is nearly the best forecast. Not a bug — but it means little predictable structure. Report the naive benchmark alongside the model.

(b) Unmodelled yearly seasonality. Add a seasonal component — Holt-Winters with L = 12, or seasonal ARIMA.

(c) Over-differencing. One difference removes a straight-line trend; the second only adds noise. Use d = 1.

(d) Leakage from future periods into training. Trust the walk-forward figure.

Marking notes. Each part rewards naming the mechanism. For (a), saying “α is too high, lower it” loses marks — α was chosen by the data. For (c), the phrase “over-differencing” plus the reason earns the full mark.

C3 · Two funds. Funds A and B have the same forecast return next month. Over the past year, A’s daily returns were calm for months then very volatile for weeks; B’s were steadily moderate. (a) Which method forecasts each fund’s variance, and why? (b) Why does “calm then volatile” matter for that method? (c) A risk-averse investor must choose one. What would you tell them, and what is the limit of your advice?

Model answer

(a) GARCH, fitted to each fund’s returns. The question is about variance, which is what GARCH estimates and forecasts.

(b) A’s variance changes over time and depends on recent history — conditional heteroscedasticity. GARCH uses recent variances and squared errors, so its forecast reflects whether A is currently in a calm or volatile spell. A single historical variance would average those spells and misstate next month’s risk either way.

(c) With equal expected return, the fund with lower forecast variance is the better fit for a risk-averse investor; that depends on A’s current state. The limit: these are model estimates, variance is only a proxy for risk, and this is analysis, not financial advice.

Marking notes. (b) is the core of the question: a constant variance estimate is wrong for A because of the clustering. In (c), answers that always pick B without checking A’s current regime lose the mark.

Things worth noticing

  • One idea, three times. Baseline, trend and seasonal factor are each “new evidence blended with old belief.” Learn one equation and you have the pattern for all three.
  • α is Module 2’s bias–variance dial again. Small α: stable but slow (bias). Large α: responsive but noisy (variance). It is chosen the same way — by error on data the fit didn’t see.
  • Every model here nests inside the next. Single smoothing is ARIMA(0, 1, 1); the naive forecast is ARIMA(0, 1, 0); good residuals are ARIMA(0, 0, 0). ARIMA is the umbrella.
  • Value and variance are different questions. Smoothing and ARIMA answer “how much?”; GARCH answers “how sure?”. Many real decisions — stock, staffing, portfolios — need both.
  • This module sits next to Module 6. CUSUM asks whether the level has shifted; exponential smoothing tracks where the level is. Both separate real change from noise, for different decisions.
