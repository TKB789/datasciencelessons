Lesson 1

Introduction to validation

Every dataset contains real patterns and random ones. A model fits both, but only the real ones show up again — so scoring a model on its own training data always flatters it.

The question you will always be asked

Validation means checking how good a model is. When you present a model to a boss or a client, one of the first questions is how good it is. How accurately does it classify loan applicants? How well does it predict future sales? How often does it correctly determine whether a transplanted liver will transmit infectious encephalitis?

The tempting answer is to score the model on the points used to build it. In the lecture picture there are 24 data points and only three sit on the wrong side of the classifier, so the model gets 21 out of 24 — 87.5%. Easy calculation. Wrong thing to do.

quick checkanswered

You report that 87.5% figure to your client. What is the actual problem with it?

Core idea

Two kinds of pattern live in any dataset. Can you name both, and say which one survives into new data?

Show answer

Real effects — genuine relationships between attributes and the response. Random effects — patterns that appear by chance.

Only the real effects reappear in new data. The random ones are different every time, which is exactly why measured performance falls.

Why training scores are optimistic

Any dataset, including the training set, holds two kinds of patterns:

Real effects

Genuine relationships between the attributes and the response. These are present in any dataset drawn from the same source.

Random effects

Patterns that appear purely by chance. The data looks like it contains a relationship when it really doesn’t.

The trouble is that we can’t tell which is which. Fitting a model to a training set finds something matching both. Use that model on different data and only the real effects are still there — the random effects have changed. So you get fewer correct predictions on new data than on the data you trained with.

Watch for

Can you retell the random-draw demonstration in three sentences?

Show answer

Ten numbers were drawn uniformly at random from 1 to 50, and nine happened to land in the top third. A model guessing 42 looks excellent on those ten and terrible on any fresh draw, because the numbers are genuinely spread evenly.

A demonstration you can run yourself

Draw ten numbers uniformly at random from 1 to 50 and plot them. Often enough, the result looks like the chart above: nine of the ten bunched in the upper third, one stray low value. Anyone shown only this sample would conclude the numbers skew high. A predictive model would guess about 42, right in the middle of where nine of the ten points sit.

Which we know is wrong, because we generated the numbers ourselves and they are uniform by construction. The skew is pure randomness. On this sample the model looks excellent — within eight for nine of the ten. On a fresh draw it would be badly off for most of them. That is the entire argument, and the only reason it is obvious here is that we happen to know the truth.

Exam target

Explain in your own words why performance on training data is biased upward, without using the word “overfitting.”

Show answer

Because the model was shaped to match every pattern in that specific data, including the accidental ones. Scoring it there gives it credit for matching accidents it will never see again.

Was that just a lucky sample?

Reasonable question: was that particular clustering cherry-picked? In a sense, yes — but it doesn’t matter, because some spurious pattern is always available. It might have been a bunch of low values instead, or a cluster in the middle, or a run of even numbers, or of odd ones, or of multiples of three. The specific pattern is unpredictable. The existence of one is not, and a flexible enough model will find whichever one showed up.

Lesson 2

Validation and test sets

One held-out set gives an honest score for one model. The moment you pick a winner from several, that winner’s score is inflated by luck — so you need a third set that had no part in the choosing.

Two sets, for one model

The fix is to use two different sets of data: a larger one to fit the model, a smaller one to measure it. In the loan example — classifying applicants into those who fully repay and those who don’t — one set of points builds the classifier and a second set measures it. If the training set had unique random effects the classifier was designed around, those benefits aren’t counted the second time.

Validation set

The second set of data, held out from fitting, used to measure how the model actually performs.

In the lecture’s picture the same line classifies 90% of the training points correctly but only 80% of the validation points. The 90% is probably too optimistic; 80% is the more accurate measure.

Numbers to remember

What were the two accuracy figures in the loan classifier example, and which one would you quote?

Show answer

90% on training, 80% on validation. Quote the 80% — and only if this was the single model you built.

The complication: choosing among models

Now suppose you’re comparing more than one model. You have a support vector machine and a k-nearest-neighbor model — and to make it harder, five different SVMs and five different KNNs. Ten models, and you want the best one. Measure each on the validation set and pick the winner. In the lecture that’s SVM #4.

Can you expect that level of performance from it? Maybe not. Every performance estimate carries randomness. Sometimes randomness makes a model look worse than it is; sometimes better. And when you pick the highest of ten scores, part of what made that one highest may be luck.

The intuition, at its most extreme

Suppose all ten models were equally good. Then every difference you see on the validation set is pure randomness. One of them happened to do a little better on this particular set, and that’s the one you pick. Its measured score isn’t its true quality — its true quality is lower.

The winner is simultaneously the most likely to genuinely be best and the most likely to have received an above-average random boost. So its validation score can’t be used as an estimate of its quality.

quick checkanswered

You compare 10 models on a validation set. The best scores 84%. What is the most defensible thing to say about that 84%?

Why this is hard

Note most people find this confusing at first. What is the subtle part?

Show answer

That the very act of picking the best introduces bias. Each individual score is unbiased; the maximum of many scores is not, because the winner is disproportionately likely to have been lucky.

Two different biases

The training warning and the validation warning are not the same problem. What separates them?

Show answer

Training bias comes from fitting: the model was shaped around that data’s noise. Validation bias comes from selecting: nothing was fitted to it, but choosing the maximum of several noisy scores favours the lucky one.

The trap

What turns a test set into a validation set?

Show answer

Making a choice based on it. The label doesn’t matter — any set you select on stops being an unbiased estimator for whatever you selected.

Lesson 3

Splitting the data

There is no correct ratio — training should be large, validation and test merely large enough. How you split matters more than the exact percentages.

How much goes where

There is no hard and fast rule; different people prefer different amounts.

SetsRecommendations in circulation
Two (training + test)70/30 through 90/10
Three (training + validation + test)50/25/25, or 60/20/20, or 70/15/15

It isn’t clear that any of these is much better than the others. The competing pressures are simple: more training data means the model can fit as many important points as possible, but the test set also needs enough data to cover important points.

The rule that actually matters

The training set should be large. The validation and test sets should merely be large enough.

Exam target

If asked “what split should I use?”, what is the right shape of answer?

Show answer

That no ratio is clearly best, quote a couple of common ones, and give the principle: training large, the other two large enough to cover the important cases.

Two ways to split, and a third

With 1,000 points and a 60/20/20 target:

Random

Randomly choose 600 points for training, then randomly choose 200 of the remainder for validation, and the 200 left over are the test set.

Rotation

Take turns on a fixed cycle. On a five-point rotation: training, validation, training, test, training — then repeat, over and over.

Rotation’s advantage is that each part of the data is equally separated. If the data spans ten years, a random split might hand the validation set a higher fraction of years 1–2 and less of years 9–10, while the test set gets the opposite. If conditions differed between the early and late years, that causes inaccuracies in your measurements.

Rotation’s danger is creating a different bias. In that five-point rotation, every first, third and fifth point goes to training, every second to validation, every fourth to test. Now suppose this is daily sales data for a retailer open five days a week:

The Monday-Wednesday-Friday trap

Training would receive all the Monday, Wednesday and Friday data and no Tuesdays or Thursdays. Any pattern the model finds in Friday data would never be checked, because validation and test only hold Tuesdays and Thursdays. That could be a more significant problem than having different fractions of data in each set.

The in-between fixes it: randomly put 60% of the Mondays into training, 60% of the Tuesdays, 60% of the Wednesdays, and so on.

The general lesson

What property of the data made the five-point rotation dangerous?

Show answer

A repeating cycle in the data — a five-day business week — that lined up with the length of the rotation. Any time the split’s period matches a period in the data, whole categories get sorted into the same set.

Lesson 4

Cross-validation

Rotate which slice is held out, train k times, average the k scores. Every point gets used for training — and the model you finally deploy is none of the k you built.

The worry it solves

Something in the flowchart makes people nervous. What if a couple of important data points only show up in the validation or test set? Then they aren’t in the training set, the model never sees them, and it never fits itself to those important pieces of data.

k-fold cross-validation

Split the training-and-validation data into k parts. For each part, train on all the others and evaluate on the one held out. Average the k evaluations to estimate the model’s quality.

A note on the letter k

k-means, k-nearest-neighbor, and now k-fold cross-validation. It refers to something different in each — but in every case it says how many of something we have.

Cross-module link

What does k count in each of the three methods?

Show answer

k-means: how many clusters. k-nearest-neighbor: how many neighbours get a vote. k-fold: how many parts the data is split into.

How the rotation works

Say 20% of the data is reserved for testing and the other 80% is used for training and validation together. Split that 80% into k parts — the lecture uses k = 4 — and instead of training and validating once, do it four times on slightly different datasets:

When comparing models to decide which to choose, take the average of all four evaluations. There is no standard number to use as k, although k = 10 has become fairly common.

Numbers to remember

In four-fold cross-validation, how many of the four models does each data point help train?

Show answer

Three. It’s held out exactly once, so it trains in the other three rounds — which is the whole point: no important point is ever excluded.

So which model do you actually use?

Four-fold cross-validation on a support vector machine gives four slightly different classifiers, one per split. Which do you deploy?

None of them

And don’t average the coefficients across the four splits either — there’s no guarantee that will work well. Once the model is selected, train it again using all four parts of the data together.

Cross-validation helps you make better use of your data, and the averaging gives a better estimate of model quality. Together they help you choose a model more effectively, which is why it has become a commonly used approach in analytics.

Lesson 5

What validation is actually measuring

Test error splits into three pieces: how wrong the model’s shape is, how much it wobbles when the data changes, and noise nobody can remove. Every choice validation makes is a trade between the first two.

Two numbers, and the gap between them

Lesson 1 said the training score is inflated. This lesson says by how much, and why — and it does so by naming the quantity validation is trying to estimate.

Test error

The average error the model would make on a fresh observation drawn from the same source, that played no part in fitting it. It is the only number that answers the client’s question, and it can never be observed directly — only estimated.

For a numeric response the usual measure is mean squared error — average of (actual − predicted)². For a classification problem it is the misclassification rate — the fraction predicted wrongly. Everything in this module works identically for both; only the thing being averaged changes.

expected test error  =  variance of the fit  +  (bias of the fit)²  +  irreducible error All three terms are non-negative, so the expected test error can never fall below the irreducible term however good the model is.
TermWhat it isWhat increases it
VarianceHow much the fitted model would change if you handed it a different training set from the same sourceFlexibility. A very flexible model chases the particular points it was given — Lesson 1’s random effects, seen from the other side
BiasThe error from forcing a complicated reality into a simpler shape — a straight line through a genuinely curved relationship, sayRigidity. A model too simple to represent the truth is wrong no matter how much data it gets
Irreducible errorRandomness in the world itself: unmeasured causes, genuine unpredictability, measurement noise in the responseNothing you can do. It is a property of the problem, not the model

That third term is why “our model is only 81% accurate” is not automatically a criticism. Two identical applicants can take different decisions. Some ceiling exists, it is below 100%, and no method reaches past it.

Core idea

Why can a model have zero training error and still have large test error?

Show answer

Because zero training error is bought with flexibility, and flexibility is variance. The model has fitted this training set’s random effects exactly; on a different sample from the same source those effects are different, so the fit is wrong in a new way each time.

The U-shaped curve everything in this course lives on

Turn any flexibility dial slowly — the degree of a polynomial, k in nearest-neighbours, λ in an SVM, the depth of a tree — and the two movable terms move in opposite directions. Bias falls. Variance rises. Their sum is a U.

What validation is for, stated exactly

Validation is a way of estimating the red curve using only data you have. You cannot see the true test error, so you approximate it by holding data out. Every technique in the rest of this module — a single split, k-fold, leave-one-out, the bootstrap — is a different approximation, with different bias and different variance of the estimate itself. That is the subtlety: the estimate has its own bias–variance problem, separate from the model’s.

Lesson 6

Why one split isn’t enough

A single held-out set gives an honest estimate — but a noisy one, and a pessimistic one. Two separate defects, and cross-validation was invented to fix both.

The demonstration

Take a dataset, split it at random into training and validation halves, fit a series of models of increasing flexibility, and plot validation error against flexibility. You get a curve. Now do the whole thing again with a different random split. You get a different curve. Do it ten times and you get ten curves that agree on the big picture and disagree on the details — including, often, on which model actually wins.

What the picture licenses you to conclude is narrower than it first appears. The big effect — the rigid model is not good enough — is visible in every split and is safe. The small differences among the good models are within the noise, and a single split cannot resolve them. Reporting “model 4 is best, at 18.2” from one split claims a precision the procedure does not have.

Drawback one — the estimate is high-variance

Which observations happened to land in the validation set moves the number, sometimes a lot. Rerun with a different random seed and the answer changes.

Drawback two — the estimate is biased upward

The model being scored was trained on only part of the data. Methods generally perform worse with fewer observations, so the held-out score tends to overstate the error of the model you will actually deploy, which gets trained on everything. Split 50/50 and you are measuring a model built from half your data.

Notice these push in opposite directions from Lesson 2’s bias

Lesson 2’s selection bias makes the winner’s validation score too optimistic. This lesson’s training-size bias makes any held-out score too pessimistic. Both are real, they do not cancel in any reliable way, and confusing them is a standard exam trap. Selection bias is about which model you picked; training-size bias is about how much data the scored model got.

Lesson 7

Leave-one-out cross-validation

Push k all the way to the number of data points. Each model trains on everything but one observation — which removes almost all of the training-size bias and all of the randomness, at a price.

The extreme case of k-fold

LOOCV

Hold out a single observation. Train on the other n − 1. Record the error on that one point. Repeat for every observation in turn, then average the n errors.

CV(n)  =  (1/n) · Σni=1 MSEi    where   MSEi = ( yi − ŷi )²

for classification:   CV(n)  =  (1/n) · Σni=1 Erri,   Erri = 1 if misclassified, 0 otherwise Each ŷi comes from a model that never saw observation i. Only the thing being averaged changes between the regression and classification versions.
AdvantageWhy
Almost no training-size biasEach model is trained on n − 1 observations — essentially the full dataset — so it is about as strong as the model you will deploy. This directly fixes Lesson 6’s second drawback
Completely deterministicThere is no random split to make. Run it twice and you get the identical number, every time — which fixes the seed-sensitivity of Lesson 6’s first drawback, though not the variance of the estimate itself
Uses every observation for evaluationEvery point is held out exactly once, so nothing is permanently excluded from either role

The price

The model has to be fitted n times. With 50,000 observations and a model taking ten seconds to fit, that is close to six days. For most methods LOOCV is simply unaffordable, which is the first and most obvious reason people use k = 5 or k = 10 instead.

Exam target

Give two advantages of LOOCV over a single train/validation split.

Show answer

Far less bias, because each fit uses n − 1 observations rather than roughly half. And no randomness — there is no split to choose, so repeated runs give exactly the same answer.

The shortcut that makes it free — sometimes

For a least-squares linear or polynomial regression there is a remarkable identity: the LOOCV score can be computed from a single fit, with no refitting at all.

CV(n)  =  (1/n) · Σni=1 [ ( yi − ŷi ) / ( 1 − hi ) ]² ŷi is the ordinary fitted value from the full fit, and hi is the leverage of observation i — between 1/n and 1 — measuring how much that point influences its own fitted value.

It is the ordinary mean squared error with each residual divided by 1 − hi. High-leverage points are the ones a model bends toward, so their ordinary residuals understate how badly the model would do without them; dividing by 1 − hi inflates exactly those residuals, by exactly the right amount for the identity to hold.

Read the scope carefully

This holds for least-squares linear and polynomial regression. It does not hold in general — not for SVMs, not for k-nearest-neighbours, not for trees — and there the model really must be refitted n times. An exam question that offers the shortcut for a logistic regression or an SVM is testing whether you know where the boundary is.

Lesson 8

Choosing k, and what the CV number is for

Lesson 4 said k = 10 is common without saying why. The reason is a second bias–variance trade-off — this one in the estimate rather than the model — and it lands on 5 or 10.

Bias falls as k rises

Each fold model trains on roughly (k − 1)/k of the data. So k = 2 trains on half, k = 5 on 80%, k = 10 on 90%, and LOOCV on all but one point. The larger k is, the closer each fold model is to the model you will actually deploy, and the less the training-size bias from Lesson 6 inflates the error estimate.

kEach fit trains onBias of the estimateNumber of fits
250%Large — badly pessimistic2
580%Modest5
1090%Small10
n (LOOCV)≈ 100%Nearly zeron

On bias alone, LOOCV wins outright. If that were the whole story everyone would use it whenever they could afford to — and they don’t.

Variance rises as k rises

Here is the part that surprises people. LOOCV averages n numbers, and averaging usually stabilises things — but only when the things being averaged are independent. The n LOOCV models are trained on training sets that differ by a single observation each, so they are nearly identical to one another, and their errors are therefore highly correlated.

The statistical fact underneath

The mean of many highly correlated quantities has higher variance than the mean of the same number of weakly correlated ones. Averaging buys you very little when everything you are averaging is essentially the same measurement repeated. With k = 5 or 10 the training sets overlap much less, the fold errors are less correlated, and the average is genuinely more stable.

So the two effects oppose each other: raising k lowers bias and raises variance. Empirically, k = 5 and k = 10 sit in the region where neither is bad, which is the actual justification for the convention Lesson 4 mentioned in passing.

quick checkanswered

Why does LOOCV tend to give a higher-variance estimate than 10-fold, despite averaging far more numbers?

Two different jobs for the same number

JobWhat you need from the CV curve
Model selection — which of these candidates should I use?Only the location of the minimum. The curve can be wrong about the level of error throughout and still point at the right model
Model assessment — how good is the model I chose?The value itself, and now its bias matters. Worse: if you selected using this number, it carries Lesson 2’s selection bias and is no longer an honest estimate

This matters more than it sounds. Cross-validation estimates are often noticeably off in level while still identifying the right level of flexibility — which is good news, because selection is what people mostly use them for. It is also why selecting and reporting from the same cross-validation is the error Lesson 2 warned about, wearing a different hat.

The one-standard-error rule

Compute the standard error of the cross-validation estimate across the folds. Then choose the simplest model whose score is within one standard error of the best score. The rationale: if several models are statistically indistinguishable, the simplest is the safer bet — it has less variance, is easier to explain, and is less likely to have won by luck.

This is the principled answer to Lesson 6’s wobbling curves. Rather than pretending the estimate can separate models it cannot, the rule reads the noise band honestly and breaks the tie on simplicity.

State the bias–variance trade-off in the choice of k, and say which direction each moves.

Show answer

Larger k means each fit trains on more data, so the estimate’s bias falls — LOOCV is nearly unbiased. But larger k also means the fold models are trained on more nearly identical data, so their errors are more correlated and the averaged estimate has higher variance. k = 5 or 10 balances the two.

Lesson 9

Cross-validation for classification, and nesting

Swap the quantity being averaged and everything carries over. The harder question is what to do when you are tuning and assessing with the same procedure.

Same machinery, different summand

Cross-validation was described above using mean squared error because that is the natural measure for a numeric response. For classification, replace it with the misclassification count: Erri is 1 if the held-out case was predicted wrongly and 0 otherwise, and the fold score is the fraction wrong. Nothing else about the procedure changes.

Everything Module 2 said about metrics applies inside each fold. If accuracy is the wrong criterion for your problem — and for an imbalanced one it usually is — then cross-validating accuracy gives you a stable estimate of the wrong thing. Cross-validate the quantity you actually care about: sensitivity, expected cost, or AUC.

Stratify when the classes are unbalanced

With 3% positives and ten folds, a plain random split can easily hand one fold twice the positive rate of another, and can in principle give a fold none at all. Stratified k-fold assigns folds so that each preserves the overall class proportions. It is the same instinct as Lesson 3’s fix for the weekday problem — split within each category rather than across the whole pile — applied to the response instead of an attribute.

When you tune and assess with the same data

Here is the situation that trips up real projects. You use 10-fold cross-validation to choose k for a nearest-neighbours model. The winning k scores 0.19. Is 0.19 your estimate of the model’s error?

No — for exactly the reason in Lesson 2. You selected on those scores, so the winner’s score carries selection bias. Cross-validation lowers the noise that drives that bias but does not remove it, and if you tried thirty values of k the effect is not small.

Nested cross-validation

Two loops. The inner loop cross-validates within the training portion to choose the parameter. The outer loop holds out a fold that the inner loop never touched, and scores the whole tuning-plus-fitting procedure on it. The outer number estimates the quality of the procedure, which is what you would actually rerun on new data.

The simpler alternative, and the one this module has been describing all along, is to keep a genuine test set out of the cross-validation entirely: cross-validate on the training-and-validation portion to choose, refit the winner on all of it, and score once on the reserved test set. Nesting is the same idea done repeatedly so that every observation gets a turn in the outer role.

Lesson 10

Where cross-validation quietly breaks

Every method in this module assumes the held-out data is genuinely independent of the training data. Four common situations where it isn’t — and in all four, the failure shows up as an excellent score.

One: the preprocessing was done first

Lesson 2 establishes that any set you make a choice on stops being clean. The same applies to any set that contributed to a calculation applied to the training data — and preprocessing is full of such calculations.

Step done on the whole dataset before splittingWhat leaks
Scaling or standardizing using the overall min, max, mean or standard deviationSummary statistics of the held-out rows enter the training pipeline
Selecting the attributes most correlated with the responseThe held-out responses helped decide which attributes the model may use — often the largest leak of the four
Imputing missing values from column meansHeld-out values contribute to the numbers filled into training rows
Balancing classes by oversampling, then splittingCopies of the same observation land in both training and held-out folds

The rule that covers all of them

Every step that learns anything from data belongs inside the cross-validation loop, not before it. Fit the scaler on the training folds, apply it to the held-out fold. Select attributes using the training folds only, and re-select from scratch on each fold. If the choice of attributes changes between folds, that is not a bug — it is the procedure telling you how unstable the selection really is, which is information the leaky version hides completely.

The tell is always the same: performance that looks too good and does not survive contact with production. Attribute selection done once on all the data before cross-validating can produce a strong-looking score on data that contains no real signal at all, because the selection step has already found the attributes that happen to correlate with the held-out responses.

Two: the data is ordered in time

Random folds scatter observations across time, so a model is routinely trained on next month and evaluated on last month. That is not a mistake a deployed model can make, so the estimate is measuring something that will never happen. Any trend, seasonality or drift makes the score optimistic.

Three: the rows aren’t independent

Cross-validation assumes each observation is a separate draw. It often isn’t: forty readings from the same patient, six months of transactions from the same customer, repeated measurements on the same machine. Split those at random and near-duplicates of the same underlying unit end up on both sides. The model can then succeed by recognising the unit rather than learning anything generalisable, and the estimate silently answers “how well does this work on a patient I have already seen?” instead of the question you asked.

The fix is grouped splitting: assign whole patients, customers or machines to folds rather than individual rows. Expect the honest score to be worse. Lesson 3’s weekday example is the same principle seen from the other direction — there the worry was categories missing from a set; here it is the same unit appearing in two sets at once.

Four: the future isn’t like the past

Every estimate in this module assumes new data comes from the same source as the old. When the world shifts — a policy change, a new competitor, a pandemic — a perfectly executed cross-validation still gives an over-confident number, because the assumption it rests on has stopped being true. Nothing in the arithmetic can detect this. It is a judgement about the domain, and it belongs in the memo alongside the number.

Lesson 11

The bootstrap

Cross-validation answers “how good is this model?”. The bootstrap answers a different question — “how much would this number move if I had collected different data?” — by resampling the data you have, with replacement.

The question it answers

Any quantity computed from data is itself uncertain: a regression coefficient, a median, a ratio, the optimal allocation between two investments. Ideally you would measure that uncertainty by collecting many fresh datasets from the population and watching the estimate vary. You cannot. The bootstrap emulates that process on a computer using the single dataset you have.

The procedure

From a dataset of n observations, draw n observations at random with replacement to form a bootstrap sample. Compute your statistic on it. Repeat B times — B is typically in the hundreds or thousands. The spread of those B values estimates the sampling variability of the statistic, and its standard deviation estimates the standard error.

The number worth memorising

The chance a given observation appears in a particular bootstrap sample is 1 − (1 − 1/n)n, which converges to 1 − 1/e ≈ 63.2%. So each bootstrap sample contains about two-thirds of the distinct observations, and roughly 36.8% are left out — the “out-of-bag” observations, which some methods reuse as a free validation set.

Exam target

What fraction of the original observations appears in a typical bootstrap sample, and where does the number come from?

Show answer

About 63.2%. Each draw misses a given observation with probability (1 − 1/n); n independent draws miss it with probability (1 − 1/n)n → 1/e ≈ 0.368, so it appears with probability ≈ 0.632.

Bootstrap or cross-validation?

Cross-validationBootstrap
Question answeredHow well does this model predict new data?How uncertain is this estimated quantity?
How data is reusedPartitioned — each observation is held out exactly onceResampled with replacement — observations repeat and are omitted
Typical outputAn estimated test errorA standard error, or a confidence interval
Typical useChoosing between models; estimating qualityPutting an error bar on a coefficient, a ratio, or any statistic with no clean formula

They are complementary rather than competing. A common pairing: cross-validate to choose the model, then bootstrap to attach uncertainty to the quantity you report from it.

Why you cannot simply bootstrap the test error

It is tempting to treat the out-of-bag observations as a validation set and stop there — but the bootstrap sample and the original dataset overlap heavily by construction, with about two-thirds of observations in common. Naively scoring a bootstrapped model against the full original data therefore gives an optimistic answer, because most of the “held-out” rows were in training. Cross-validation partitions and so has no overlap at all, which is why it remains the right tool for estimating test error.

The bootstrap’s real strength is that it needs no formula. Standard errors for a regression coefficient come from textbook theory that assumes the model is correct; bootstrap standard errors make no such assumption, which is why they are often the more trustworthy of the two when the model is shaky. And for statistics with no theory at all — the ratio of two medians, an optimal allocation, the difference between two AUCs — it may be the only option.

Technique

Most validation questions reduce to one diagnostic question. Which?

Show answer

“Did the held-out data influence anything the model saw?” — through fitting, through selection, through preprocessing, through time, or through a repeated unit. If yes, the score is optimistic. If no, ask instead how much data the scored model got, which tells you whether it is pessimistic.

Section B — Short answer and discussion, final style

Roughly 4–8 marks each in a typical paper. Attempt each before opening it.

B1 · Explain why test error decomposes into three parts, and use the decomposition to explain the U-shaped relationship between flexibility and test error.

Model answer

Expected test error = variance of the fit + squared bias of the fit + irreducible error. Variance is how much the fitted model would change if trained on a different sample from the same source. Bias is the error from approximating a complicated reality with a simpler form. Irreducible error is randomness in the world — unmeasured causes and measurement noise — and is a property of the problem, not the model.

As flexibility rises, bias falls (the model can represent more shapes) and variance rises (the model chases the particular sample). Early on, bias falls faster than variance rises, so total error falls. Beyond some point bias has little left to give while variance keeps climbing, so total error rises again. The sum is therefore U-shaped, with a minimum at the flexibility where the two rates cross over.

Since all three terms are non-negative, expected test error can never fall below the irreducible error — there is a floor, and it is above zero.

Marking notes. Marks are for the mechanism, not the equation: the crossover of rates is what produces the U. A strong answer adds that training error has no such U — it declines monotonically with flexibility — which is precisely why it cannot be used to locate the minimum, and why validation exists.

B2 · State the two drawbacks of the single train/validation split and explain how k-fold cross-validation addresses each.

Model answer

High variance of the estimate. The number depends on which observations happened to land in the validation set; repeat the split with a different seed and both the estimate and often the chosen model change. k-fold addresses this by averaging k held-out estimates, each using a different portion of the data, so the result depends far less on any one partition.

Upward bias on error. The scored model was trained on only part of the data — half, under a 50/50 split — and methods perform worse with fewer observations, so the estimate overstates the error of the model that will actually be deployed on everything. k-fold addresses this by training each fold model on (k − 1)/k of the data: 90% at k = 10, much closer to the full dataset.

Marking notes. Both drawbacks must be named with direction — “biased” without saying which way typically scores half. The strongest answers note that k-fold reduces rather than eliminates both, and that raising k to reduce bias raises the variance of the estimate for a separate reason, which is the subject of the next question.

B3 · Explain the bias–variance trade-off in the choice of k, and justify the common recommendation of k = 5 or 10.

Model answer

Each fold model trains on about (k − 1)/k of the data, so larger k means more training data per fit and less upward bias in the error estimate. At k = n (LOOCV) each fit uses n − 1 observations and the estimate is nearly unbiased.

But larger k also means the k training sets overlap more heavily. At k = n they differ by a single observation, so the n fitted models are near-copies and their errors are highly correlated. The mean of many highly correlated quantities has higher variance than the mean of the same number of weakly correlated ones, so LOOCV’s average is less stable than a 10-fold average despite averaging more terms.

k = 5 or 10 sits where neither effect is severe: enough training data per fold to keep bias modest, enough independence between folds to keep variance modest, and a computational cost of 5 or 10 fits instead of n.

Marking notes. The correlation argument is the whole question. Answers that say “LOOCV has higher variance because each error is based on one point” have named a real fact that is not the reason — the averaging is meant to absorb it, and the reason it absorbs it poorly is correlation between the fits.

B4 · Describe k-fold cross-validation end to end for a project with a reserved test set, and state precisely what goes into production.

Model answer

First, before anything else touches the data, set aside a test set. Split the remaining training-and-validation data into k roughly equal folds. For each fold in turn: train each candidate model on the other k − 1 folds and score it on the held-out fold. Average each candidate’s k scores. Compare the averages and choose a candidate.

Then refit the chosen candidate on all k folds together — the deliverable is none of the k fold models, each of which saw only (k − 1)/k of the available data. Averaging their coefficients is also not an option; there is no guarantee that the average of several good models is a good model. Finally, score the refitted model once on the reserved test set and report that number.

Marking notes. The two ends are where marks are lost: the test set must be reserved before the folds are made, and the winner must be refitted on everything after the choice. The middle of the procedure is what everyone remembers and is usually worth the fewest marks.

B5 · Distinguish selection bias from training-size bias. Give the direction of each and an example where both are present.

Model answer

Selection bias comes from choosing. Each candidate’s held-out score is unbiased on its own, but the maximum of several noisy scores favours whichever candidate got a favourable draw, so the winner’s score is optimistic. It grows with the number of candidates compared.

Training-size bias comes from data volume. The scored model was fitted on less data than the deployed model will be, so it is weaker, and its error estimate is pessimistic.

Both are present in a routine tuning exercise: 10-fold cross-validating twenty values of a parameter. Each fold model trains on 90% of the data, so every candidate’s average is slightly pessimistic; and the winning average is then selected as the best of twenty, which is optimistic. The two do not cancel in any predictable way — their sizes are unrelated — which is why the honest figure still has to come from a set that took part in neither the fitting nor the choosing.

Marking notes. Naming both directions correctly is most of the marks. Explicitly rejecting the “they cancel out” intuition is worth an extra one, because it is a plausible-sounding conclusion that examiners plant in distractors.

B6 · An analyst standardizes all the data, then selects the 100 attributes most correlated with the response, then runs 10-fold cross-validation on those 100. Identify what is wrong and state the general rule.

Model answer

Both preprocessing steps used the whole dataset, including every fold that will later be held out. The standardization means each fold’s own values contributed to the mean and standard deviation applied to the training data. The attribute screening is worse: it used the held-out responses to decide which attributes the model is allowed to use, so every fold model has already been given information about the observations it will be scored on. With a large candidate pool, this can produce a respectable cross-validated score on data containing no real signal whatsoever.

The general rule: any step that learns something from the data must sit inside the cross-validation loop. Fit the scaler on the training folds and apply it to the held-out fold; re-run the attribute screening from scratch within each fold. If the selected attributes differ between folds, that instability is real information about how reliable the selection is — information the leaky version conceals.

Marking notes. Both leaks should be named, and the attribute-selection one identified as the more serious. The best answers point out the diagnostic symptom: a good cross-validated score that collapses in production, with nothing in the validation arithmetic able to detect the problem.

B7 · Why is random k-fold inappropriate for time-ordered data, and what should be used instead?

Model answer

Random folds place observations from after the held-out period into the training set, so the model is evaluated on the past having been trained on the future. A deployed model can never do this. Where there is trend, seasonality or drift, knowing the surrounding period makes the held-out points far easier than they will be in reality, so the estimate is optimistic — and the closer the correlation between adjacent periods, the larger the effect.

Use rolling-origin validation instead: train on an initial stretch of history, evaluate on the period immediately following, then extend the training window forward and repeat. Each round uses only information that would have been available at the time. It uses less data per fit and yields a worse number than random folds — and that worse number is the honest one, because it is the only arrangement a deployed model ever faces.

Marking notes. The phrase that earns marks is training on the future. Describing rolling origin concretely — the window extends, the evaluation period is always the next one — is worth more than naming it. Noting that the estimate legitimately gets worse, and that this is not a regression in quality, is a good closing point.

B8 · Define LOOCV, give two advantages and one cost, and state exactly when the computational shortcut applies.

Model answer

LOOCV holds out a single observation, trains on the remaining n − 1, records the error on that one point, and repeats for every observation, averaging the n errors: CV(n) = (1/n)Σ MSEi, or the same with misclassification indicators for a classification problem.

Advantages: almost no training-size bias, since each fit uses n − 1 observations; and complete determinism, since there is no random split — repeated runs give identical results. Cost: n model fits, which is prohibitive for large n or slow methods.

The shortcut: for least-squares linear and polynomial regression, CV(n) = (1/n)Σ[(yi − ŷi)/(1 − hi)]², computable from a single fit, where hi is the leverage. It does not generalise — logistic regression, SVMs, nearest-neighbours and trees all require the n refits.

Marking notes. The scope restriction on the shortcut is a reliable mark and a reliable trap. An answer that adds why dividing by 1 − hi is the right correction — high-leverage points pull the fit toward themselves, so their ordinary residuals understate the error the model would make without them — shows understanding rather than recall.

B9 · State the one-standard-error rule and explain the reasoning behind it. Why not simply take the minimum?

Model answer

Compute the standard error of the cross-validation estimate across folds. Identify the best score, then choose the simplest model whose score falls within one standard error of it.

The reason not to take the minimum is that the cross-validation curve is itself an estimate with noise. When several models score within the noise band, the one that happens to be lowest is partly the luckiest, and re-running with different folds would likely promote a different one. Taking the minimum therefore commits to a distinction the data cannot support — and systematically favours the more flexible model, since flexibility adds variance and variance is what produces lucky low scores.

Choosing the simplest model in the band is defensible on several grounds at once: lower variance, easier to explain, cheaper to run, and less likely to have won by accident.

Marking notes. Watch what “simplest” means for the method in question. For subset selection it is the fewest predictors; for nearest-neighbours it is the largest k, since large k is the rigid end. Candidates who reflexively pick the smallest number lose marks here, and examiners know it.

B10 · Describe the bootstrap, explain where the figure of roughly 63% comes from, and say what it is for that cross-validation is not.

Model answer

Draw n observations from your dataset of n with replacement to form a bootstrap sample; compute the statistic of interest on it; repeat B times. The spread of the B values estimates the sampling variability of the statistic, and their standard deviation estimates its standard error. Sampling must be with replacement — without it, drawing n from n simply returns the original dataset every time and there would be no variation at all.

A given observation is missed by a single draw with probability (1 − 1/n), so it is missed by all n draws with probability (1 − 1/n)n, which tends to 1/e ≈ 0.368. It therefore appears with probability ≈ 0.632, leaving about 37% out-of-bag.

Cross-validation estimates how well a modelling procedure predicts unseen data. The bootstrap estimates the uncertainty of a computed quantity — a coefficient, a ratio, a median, an allocation — particularly where no convenient standard-error formula exists, and without assuming the model is correctly specified. It is a poor tool for estimating test error, because bootstrap samples share about two-thirds of their observations with the original data, so “held-out” evaluation against the full dataset is heavily contaminated.

Marking notes. The derivation of 0.632 is usually worth a mark on its own and is easy to produce under pressure. The clean statement of what each method estimates is the part that separates answers; the two are complementary, not competing.

B11 · Compare random, rotation and stratified splitting. Give the situation where each is the right choice and the failure mode of each.

Model answer

Random: each observation is assigned at random. Unbiased on average and always defensible. Failure mode: nothing guarantees even coverage, so one set may end up heavy on a particular period or category purely by chance — a real risk when the dataset is small.

Rotation: assign on a fixed cycle. Guarantees each part of the data is equally represented across sets, which helps when conditions drift over time. Failure mode: if the cycle length lines up with a period in the data, whole categories land in one set. A five-point rotation on five-day-a-week sales data sends every Monday, Wednesday and Friday to training and never checks them.

Stratified: split randomly within each category — 60% of the Mondays, 60% of the Tuesdays, and so on, or 60% of each class. Gets the guaranteed coverage without the periodicity trap. It is the default for imbalanced classification, where a plain random fold can contain very few positives or none. Failure mode: you have to know which variable matters enough to stratify on, and stratifying on too many at once leaves strata too small to split sensibly.

Marking notes. The weekday example is the one the examiner is looking for and should be given concretely rather than gestured at. A strong answer generalises it: the danger arises whenever the split’s period matches a period in the data, which is a statement about any cyclical structure, not about weekdays specifically.

B12 · How does cross-validation change for a classification problem, and what should you be careful to cross-validate when one class is rare?

Model answer

The procedure is unchanged; only the quantity averaged differs. Instead of mean squared error, each fold contributes its misclassification rate — the fraction of held-out cases predicted wrongly — and the fold scores are averaged as before.

With a rare class, two cautions. First, use stratified folds so each preserves the overall class proportions; plain random folds can vary substantially in positive rate and, with very rare positives and small folds, may contain none at all, in which case a model predicting the majority class scores perfectly on that fold while detecting nothing. Second, cross-validate the right quantity: accuracy is dominated by the majority class, so a stable estimate of accuracy is a stable estimate of something that does not distinguish a useful model from a useless one. Cross-validate sensitivity, expected cost, or AUC — whichever the decision actually depends on.

Marking notes. The second point is where the marks are. Cross-validation improves the precision of an estimate and has no opinion about whether the estimated quantity is the right one — stating that explicitly connects this module to the metrics material and usually reads as a strong answer.

Where the marks hide

One habit earns marks on nearly every question in this section. Which?

Show answer

Stating the direction of every bias you name, and why it runs that way. “The estimate is biased” is worth almost nothing; “biased upward, because the winner was selected as the maximum of several noisy scores” is worth the question.

Section C — Multi-part cases, final style

Each part builds on the last. Work them in order.

C1 · Audit the pipeline. A colleague describes their workflow. Identify every error, say which direction each pushes the reported number, and give the corrected procedure.

1Loaded all 12,000 rows and standardized every attribute using the overall mean and standard deviation.
2Dropped the attributes whose correlation with the response was below 0.05.
3Split 80/20 into training and test.
4On the training data, ran 10-fold cross-validation over 25 parameter settings and picked the best.
5Reported the winning cross-validated score as the model’s expected performance, and separately checked it on the test set, which came out lower — concluding the test set was unrepresentative.
Model answer

Step 1 — leakage, optimistic. The mean and standard deviation were computed over rows that later became the test set, so test information entered the training pipeline. Compute them on the training data only and apply those same numbers to test.

Step 2 — leakage, optimistic, and the more serious of the two. Attribute selection used every response, including the test responses, to decide what the model may use. This must be redone using only training data, and ideally re-run inside each cross-validation fold so its instability is visible.

Step 3 — correct, but in the wrong place. The split should be the first operation, before any step that learns from data.

Step 4 — correct in itself. Cross-validating 25 settings on the training portion is exactly right.

Step 5 — two errors. First, the winning cross-validated score was selected as the best of 25 and so is optimistic; it is a basis for choosing, not a performance estimate. Second, the conclusion is backwards: the test score being lower is what the theory predicts, and blaming the test set inverts the diagnosis. The test set is the honest number.

Corrected order: split off the test set first → within the training portion, run 10-fold cross-validation, performing standardization and attribute selection inside each fold → choose the setting → refit the winner, with its preprocessing, on the whole training portion → score once on the test set → report that.

Marking notes. Four errors, and full marks require all four with directions. Step 5’s reversed conclusion is the highest-value item: it is the same misunderstanding that makes people “fix” a disappointing test result by looking for a friendlier test set, and examiners include it deliberately.

C2 · Reading a cross-validation table. 10-fold CV of a nearest-neighbours classifier over five values of k, on 4,000 training-and-validation observations. Misclassification rate, with the standard error across folds in brackets:

k15102050
CV error0.312 (0.018)0.268 (0.015)0.251 (0.014)0.247 (0.016)0.259 (0.013)

(a) How many model fits has this table cost, and how many more before deployment? (b) Which k does the raw minimum select, and which does the one-standard-error rule select? (c) Why is k = 1 the worst row, and what would its training error have been? (d) Can 0.247 be reported to the client as the model’s expected error rate? (e) The client asks for a single accuracy figure. Describe what you would do to produce one.

Model answer

(a) 5 candidates × 10 folds = 50 fits. One more — the winner refitted on all 4,000 training-and-validation observations — so 51 in total. (Strictly, nearest-neighbours “fits” nothing; the cost is in the 50 rounds of neighbour searching, which is the same accounting.)

(b) Raw minimum: k = 20 at 0.247. One-standard-error rule: the band is 0.247 + 0.016 = 0.263, and the qualifying models are k = 10 (0.251), k = 20 (0.247) and k = 50 (0.259). The simplest of these is the least flexible, and for nearest-neighbours that is the largest k — so the rule selects k = 50. Anyone who reads “simplest” as “smallest number” selects k = 10 and has the flexibility direction backwards.

(c) k = 1 is maximally flexible: each new point copies its single nearest neighbour, so the boundary wraps around individual observations and the fit is dominated by variance. Its training error would be exactly zero, since every training point is its own nearest neighbour — which is why training error cannot be used to choose k at all.

(d) No. 0.247 is the minimum of five noisy estimates and so is optimistically biased by selection; it is also the score of fold models trained on 90% of the data rather than the deployed model trained on 100%, which pushes the other way. The two do not cancel predictably. Report the score from a test set that took no part in either the fitting or the choosing.

(e) Refit the chosen k on all 4,000 observations, score once on the reserved test set, and report that figure — with the caveat that a single number hides which errors are being made. If the classes are imbalanced or the errors have different costs, give expected cost or sensitivity alongside it, and state the null-classifier baseline so the number has something to be compared against.

Marking notes. Part (b) is the discriminating one — the flexibility direction for k is the planted trap. In (a), forgetting the final refit is both an arithmetic slip and a conceptual one, and is usually marked as the latter. In (d), naming both biases and their directions scores higher than naming either alone.

C3 · Wearable sensors. A team predicts falls in elderly patients from accelerometer data: 240 patients, each contributing about 90 days of readings, roughly 21,600 rows. Falls occur on 1.2% of patient-days. Random 10-fold cross-validation gives 98.9% accuracy and AUC 0.94.

(a) What does 98.9% accuracy tell you here? (b) Name two structural reasons the AUC of 0.94 is likely to be optimistic. (c) Design a validation scheme that fixes both. (d) After the fix, AUC falls to 0.72 and the clinical lead asks whether the project has failed. Answer them. (e) The team also wants a confidence interval on the difference in AUC between their model and a simpler baseline. Which technique from this module applies, and why not cross-validation?

Model answer

(a) Almost nothing. Predicting “no fall” on every patient-day scores 98.8%, so the model is one-tenth of a point above a classifier that detects nothing. Accuracy on a 1.2% base rate cannot distinguish a useful model from a useless one, and the null baseline should be quoted every time this figure is.

(b) First, grouped data: with 90 rows per patient and random folds, readings from the same patient sit in both training and held-out folds. The model can succeed by recognising individual patients’ movement signatures rather than learning what precedes a fall. Second, time ordering: within a patient, random folds train on days after the held-out day, so the model sees the future of the very patient it is being scored on.

(c) Fold by patient, not by row, so every reading from a patient sits entirely in one fold; stratify the patient-level folds by whether the patient ever falls, so each fold contains some positive patients; and within each fold respect time order — train on each patient’s earlier days and evaluate on later ones, or hold out whole patients and evaluate their full series, which is closer to the deployment question of “a new patient arrives”. Reserve a set of patients as a test set before any of this begins.

(d) No. The 0.72 is the first figure that answers the question the deployment poses — a new patient, going forward — and the 0.94 answered a question nobody will ever ask. What the project has avoided is promising 0.94 and delivering 0.72 in a clinical setting. Whether 0.72 is good enough is a separate question and a cost question: at a 1.2% base rate, what matters is how many falls are caught at a workable alert volume, and how the cost of a missed fall compares with the cost of a check that finds nothing. That calculation, not the AUC, should decide deployment.

(e) The bootstrap. The difference between two AUCs has no convenient standard-error formula, and the bootstrap needs none: resample — at the patient level, to respect the grouping — recompute both AUCs and their difference on each resample, and read the interval off the spread of the B differences. Cross-validation is the wrong tool because it estimates predictive error, not the uncertainty of a computed statistic; it can tell you each model’s expected performance but not how confident you should be that one exceeds the other.

Marking notes. Part (b) must name both violations to score well — most candidates find the grouping and miss the time ordering. Part (d) rewards refusing the framing: restating what each number measures, rather than defending or apologising for the drop. In (e), resampling at the patient level rather than the row level is the detail that shows the grouping point was properly understood.

Ask what the reported number would be for a model that does nothing, and ask what the held-out data was allowed to touch. Between them those two questions catch the majority of validation errors that appear on exams and in practice.

Six things worth noticing

  • Two different biases, often confused. Training bias comes from fitting; validation bias comes from selecting. Cross-validation reduces the noise behind the second one but does not remove it — you still need a test set.
  • The test set is single-use. Nothing in the lecture says this outright, but it follows directly from Lesson 2: any set you make a choice on becomes a validation set.
  • The estimate has its own bias–variance problem. Lesson 5’s trade-off is about the model; Lesson 8’s is about the number you use to judge it. Larger k gives the estimate less bias and more variance, entirely independently of whatever the model is doing. Keeping the two trade-offs apart is most of what this module asks of you.
  • Every failure mode announces itself the same way. Leakage, random folds on time-ordered data, repeated units across folds, selecting and reporting from one set — all four produce a better score than the model deserves. A result that looks too good is a diagnosis, not a celebration.
  • Cross-validation improves precision, not relevance. It has no opinion about whether the quantity being averaged is the right one. Cross-validated accuracy on a 1% base rate is a very stable estimate of something useless.
  • k appears three times in this course. k-means, k-nearest-neighbor, k-fold. The source material flags this. Expect a question that tests whether you know which is which.

Likely exam targets

  • Explain why training-set performance is optimistic, using real vs. random effects.
  • Give a small-sample example of a spurious pattern and say what it proves.
  • State the job of each of the three sets, and when the validation step is skipped.
  • Explain why the best validation score among many models is biased upward.
  • Give common split ratios and the principle behind them.
  • Contrast random and rotation splitting, with the weekday example.
  • Describe k-fold cross-validation and say what gets deployed at the end.
  • Give the three-part decomposition of expected test error and use it to explain the U-shaped flexibility curve.
  • Explain why expected test error can never reach zero.
  • State the two drawbacks of the single-split approach, with the direction of each.
  • Distinguish selection bias from training-size bias and give the direction of both.
  • Define LOOCV, give two advantages and one cost, and say exactly where the leverage shortcut applies.
  • Explain why LOOCV has higher variance than 10-fold despite averaging more terms.
  • State the one-standard-error rule, justify it, and apply it to a table — including working out which direction “simplest” runs for the method in question.
  • Explain why every data-dependent preprocessing step belongs inside the cross-validation loop.
  • Explain why random folds are wrong for time-ordered data and describe rolling-origin validation.
  • Explain why repeated measurements on the same unit require grouped folds.
  • Describe the bootstrap, derive the 63.2% figure, and contrast what it estimates with what cross-validation estimates.
  • Say how cross-validation changes for classification, and what to stratify and to cross-validate when a class is rare.
