Why a wider margin is worth having

The argument differs between hard and soft classification, and in the soft case it is explicitly a trade rather than an improvement.

What this page is

Why a wider margin is worth having

Take the two settings separately, because the argument is not the same in each.

Hard classification

A wider margin gives more surety that each point is classified correctly. It also buys tolerance for data that is not quite right: if someone reports income as $50,000 when it is really $49,872, a wider margin makes it more likely that the point still lands on the side it belongs on.

Soft classification

A wider margin — even one that induces more errors on the data you are fitting to — can give a model that is more certain about how to classify new points, and less influenced by the specific location of points that may, through an incomplete model or randomness in the data, be sitting in the “wrong” place. Against that: those specific points might carry important information, and putting less weight on them can hurt the precision of the model in close cases.

Notice that the two arguments are not the same one. In the hard case the gain is robustness to measurement error in the inputs. In the soft case the gain is robustness to the particular sample you happened to draw, and it is explicitly paid for with training errors. Neither argument says a wider margin is always better; the second says outright that it can cost you precision where it matters most.

Topic 2

Scaling versus standardization

Both put attributes on a comparable footing. Which one suits depends mostly on whether the data has bounds that mean something.

Scaling versus standardization

Both put attributes on a comparable footing. Which one suits depends mostly on whether the data has natural bounds.

  • Scaling is often more appropriate when there are bounds on the data — age, SAT score, a success probability, RGB values — and especially when there is data close to or at those bounds.
  • Standardization is often more appropriate for data that is not as bounded — distance, income — and that is a reasonable fit for a normal distribution. If the data fits a different distribution, standardizing to that distribution instead is also an option.
  • Combination cases exist. Data might sit mostly in a nice bounded distribution with a few cases far away — income is a good example — where it can be best to remove the outliers and then scale the rest.
  • Or mix the two: instead of standardizing the values, take a reasonable range of z-scores, say −3 to +3 standard deviations, and scale that window from “−3 to 3” onto “0 to 1”.

Topic 3

KNN or SVM? What actually decides it

KNN or SVM? What actually decides it

Six considerations, none of which is about which algorithm is “better”.

ConsiderationWhat it implies
More than two classesKNN is more flexible. SVM methods for multiple classes exist but are not commonly used — for example, for each colour k, run a model separating “colour k” from “not colour k”.
Pockets of one class inside anotherKNN handles meaningful pockets of one colour surrounded by the other; a single separator cannot.
Computation at prediction timeSVM is easier to compute with. Every new point KNN classifies requires finding the k nearest training points. For a model answering card-transaction queries in real time, that cost matters. Heuristics help, but are beyond this course.
Balance in the datasetWith many more “yes” points than “no”, KNN leans toward predicting “yes”, and more so as k grows. You have to even the data out first.
Probability estimatesKNN gives one for free: neighbours in a class divided by k. SVM is harder — probability might be a function of the distance from the classifier to the point.
Being deliberately conservativeBoth can do it. SVM shifts the separator; KNN shifts the threshold.
Fixing an unbalanced dataset

Two basic approaches. Scale up the rare class — duplicate each point some number of times, or create new points of that colour that are similar to the ones in the dataset. Or scale down the common class — randomly take a small sample of the full set, run your model, and repeat for many different small samples. None of these approaches, or others like them, are perfect and all have potential problems; but an unbalanced dataset is sometimes reality and we have to do the best we can.

KNN probability, and its limits

With k = 7 and five “yes” neighbours to two “no”, you can quote 5/7. The estimate is not so reliable, especially at the extremes: a point whose seven nearest neighbours are all one colour is probably not really 100% likely to be that colour. Neighbours can also be weighted by how close they are.

Conservative classification with KNN

Change the threshold rather than the method. With k = 7, instead of calling anything with 4 or more red neighbours red, you might call anything with 2 or more red neighbours red — and require 6 or 7 out of 7 before classifying a point as blue. That is the KNN equivalent of the more-conservative SVM solutions from the lessons.

One more, and it is a meta-point rather than a method choice: if the points are very mixed, no model will save you. If the predictors do a bad job of separating the classes even with nonlinear-looking separators, so that everything is thoroughly intermingled, that is a sign to go back and question the underlying assumption that these particular features are good predictors of the classification at all.

Topic 4

Why units silently weight your distances

The scale each feature happens to be recorded on is acting as a weighting scheme, whether or not anybody chose it.

Why units silently weight your distances

Each feature — predictor, variable, factor, whichever word your text uses — might naturally sit on a different numeric scale, and those scales are essentially weighting the distances.

The worked example

Suppose you use “distance from warehouse to customer” and “distance from factory to warehouse” as factors in estimating transportation cost. If one factor is measured in millimetres and the other in kilometres, the first will have numbers roughly 1,000,000 times as large. Differences in that factor will therefore look bigger, and a KNN-type algorithm will weight that factor far more heavily when deciding what counts as “nearest”.

The unsettling part is that nothing about the real world changed. The same two quantities, recorded in different units, produce a different notion of “nearest” and therefore a different model. This is why scaling is a cross-cutting concept rather than a detail: it decides what your distance metric actually means.

Why it bites KNN hardest

Which methods are most exposed to this problem?

Show answer

Anything that adds or compares attributes to compute a distance or an inner product — KNN, SVM, k-means. A decision tree, which splits one attribute at a time, is largely indifferent to units.

Topic 5

Choosing on validation, and what it costs

The model that wins on validation is the right one to pick and its winning score is the wrong one to quote — and the reason is the same in both cases.

Two kinds of pattern, in every dataset

Any set of data holds real patterns, which reflect the world and will show up again in new data, and random patterns, which are accidents of that particular sample and will not. A score measured on a dataset reflects both, and the number alone doesn’t say which did the work.

Instructor’s note

If the magnitude of the random patterns is high enough compared with the differences between models’ ability to capture real patterns, then it’s likely the best model wouldn’t be the one chosen. However, it’s still the most-likely one to be best, which is why we choose it. The model with the highest validation performance would be most likely to be best, but that probability it actually is best would still be below 50% if there are several almost-equally-good options.

Why the winner’s error rises on the test set

Instructor’s note

When you select the best model based on validation error, you are introducing a selection bias in favour of models that got a random ‘boost’ on the validation set, particularly when there is a close call for the best model. When moving to the test set, that advantage is likely to disappear, resulting in a higher than expected error.

Just to make sure it’s clear, there isn’t anything inherently better or worse between the validation data and the test data. The difference is that they contain different random patterns, and each model might be a better fit to one or the other of those random patterns (in addition to having different fits to the real patterns). So, whatever performs best on one of those data sets might not perform best on the other — but there’s no way to tell which is better.

Two things follow, and they are easy to state backwards. The winner’s validation score is optimistic, because that score is what selected it. The test score is the unbiased one, not because the test set is better data, but because the selection never touched it.

And why not use both sets to choose

Instructor’s note

The reason we generally don’t use the two data sets combined to pick the model is because then we don’t have an unbiased estimate of the model’s quality. Instead, we’d be combining the validation and test data into one large validation set (to pick the right model) and we wouldn’t have any data left over to get an unbiased quality estimate for whatever model we chose.

If you’re in a situation where you really do need to use more data to pick a model and the test data is all you have left, you can use it that way — just understand (and make sure others understand) that the test data will no longer give an unbiased estimate of model quality; it’s likely that performance on the test data will now overstate the model’s quality, and you shouldn’t expect it to do that well on new data.

Note what that last paragraph is and isn’t. It isn’t a prohibition — it is a permission with a disclosure attached. The failure mode is not using the test data for selection; it is using it and then quoting the resulting number as though nothing had changed.

Topic 6

Classification against clustering

One has a correct answer for every data point and the other has none — and every other difference between them follows from that.

Supervised and unsupervised

Instructor’s note

Classification is a “supervised” model where you know the “correct answer” for each data point. Clustering is an “unsupervised” model where you don’t know any “correct answer”. [Note: In our clustering homework I did give you correct answers for each data point just so you could see how well the algorithm performed, but in practice you usually wouldn’t have that information.]

That bracketed aside is the part worth holding on to, because the homework arrangement is the thing most likely to mislead you. Labels sitting in the file do not make a method supervised; what matters is whether the algorithm learns from them. In the homework it doesn’t — they are there so you can grade the result afterwards.

Where the categories come from

Instructor’s note

Here’s another way of making the distinction. In classification, where you do know the “answer” for each data point, the categories are a different variable from the predictors (e.g., “did or didn’t repay loan” is different from the predictors “income” and “credit score”). On the other hand, in clustering, the categories/clusters are defined by the predictors through the model.

The phrasing test

Instructor’s note

Question-wise, a classification question is usually “is each data point this or that?” or “is it, or isn’t it?” while a clustering question is often more like “which groups of these data points are similar to each other?”

This is the fastest of the three tests to apply in an exam, because it works on a sentence rather than on a dataset. If the question names the categories, they exist independently and it is classification — however many of them there are. If it asks which points resemble each other, no categories have been named yet and the model will have to invent them.

Topic 7

Which outliers are hard to find, and which are hard to judge

Detection difficulty and handling difficulty are two different rankings, and the types that top each one are not the same.

Finding them is not the same problem as deciding about them

The three types are usually introduced as a difficulty ladder, which invites the assumption that one type is hardest at everything. It is not. Sort them once by how hard they are to detect and once by how hard they are to adjudicate, and the two orderings disagree.

Point outliers — easiest to detect

A single observation that sits far from everything else. It can be identified by looking at the distance of a point from all the others — for example, calculating the distance between each point and the nearest other point to it. A point with an unusually large nearest-neighbour distance is a candidate.

Contextual outliers — often detectable the same way

A value that is unremarkable in general but wrong for its surroundings. Potential contextual outliers can often be found by the same distance reasoning, once “nearest” is taken within the relevant context. Detection is not usually the hard part here.

Collective outliers — hardest to detect

A set of points that is anomalous as a group while no individual member looks unusual. These are tougher because we might know something is missing, but it is hard to know exactly what or where. There is no single observation to flag, so the distance test that works on the other two has nothing to point at.

The other ranking: which are hardest to deal with

Contextual outliers are the difficult ones once found. It is often quite hard to work out whether such a point is bad data, data that is correct but should not be used in your model, or data that is correct and should be used. Collective outliers are hard to see; contextual outliers are hard to rule on. An exam answer that names only one type as “the hardest” without saying hardest at what has answered half the question.

Once you have decided a point is an outlier

Finding it settles nothing by itself. Every flagged point then needs one of three verdicts, and the first two look identical in the data:

  1. Bad or incorrect data — a genuine error. Throw it out.
  2. Good, correct data that is not relevant to the question at hand — real, but not about what you are modelling. It can be thrown out.
  3. Good, correct data that is relevant to the question — and therefore should be retained, however inconvenient it is.

Note that the second and third verdicts both concern correct data, so no test of data quality can separate them. Only knowing what question the model is answering does. This is why deleting everything a detector flags is not a defensible policy: it silently applies verdict 1 to points that belong in category 3.

Topic 8

What C and T actually do in a CUSUM

One parameter filters noise out of each observation; the other sets how much surviving evidence you need before calling a change. They are not interchangeable.

Notation used here

Detecting an increase, the running total is St = max(0, St−1 + (xt − μ − C)), and a change is declared when St ≥ T. For a decrease, reverse the subtraction: St = max(0, St−1 + (μ − xt − C)). Everything below is written for the increase case; the argument reverses unchanged for decreases. Outside this course you will meet C under the names reference value, allowance or slack (often written k), and T as the threshold or decision interval (often h).

T — how much evidence before you call it

When T is higher it takes longer for CUSUM to detect a change, because the running total has to get larger before it trips. In exchange, a higher T makes it less likely that the model declares a change that has not happened — that the total is only high through randomness. Lower T detects changes faster and falsely declares changes more often. So T is the parameter governing how sensitive the model is to changes.

C — how much variation you agree to ignore

When adding to the running total, C is subtracted from the deviation. So even when an observation comes in above the mean, the total will not increase unless the observation is more than C above the mean. C removes the effect of ordinary variability.

  • Lots of natural random variation in the data → a higher C helps you ignore the occasions when randomness alone pushes an observation above the mean.
  • Little variability → C can be lower, because randomness on its own will not push observations very far above the mean.
  • C at or near zero, with noisy data → several random above-mean observations in a row are enough to run the sum past the threshold, and the model reports a change that never happened.

The question everyone asks: why not set C = 0 and raise T instead?

If C only absorbs noise, and a large T also stops noise from triggering an alarm, it looks as though one parameter would do. It will not, and the reason is worth being able to reproduce.

The argument

Observations will sometimes be randomly above the mean for several periods in a row — not unlikely at all when you are taking many observations. With C = 0, every one of those excursions is added to the total in full, and they accumulate. To avoid declaring a change on that basis, you would have to set T high. And now a real change — a fast one, where the data suddenly sits far above the mean — takes longer to detect, because T has been raised artificially to cope with noise. Use C to handle the variability instead, and T can stay low, so the genuine fast change is caught sooner.

The two parameters act at different stages, which is why one cannot stand in for the other. C filters each observation before it enters the total, so ordinary wobble never accumulates at all. T sets the bar on the evidence that survives the filter. Collapse them into T alone and you are asking a single number to both tolerate noise and measure evidence — and it can only do that by being large, which is precisely what costs you detection speed.

Do not update the mean as new data arrives

A second common question: should μ be recalculated as each new observation comes in? No. To detect whether a change has occurred you have to compare against the old mean. Updating μ as new data arrives masks exactly the change you are looking for.

The salary example

Take average yearly salary data from 1970 to the present. Salaries have risen a great deal — there has plainly been a change. But if μ is recalculated every time a new data point arrives, μ rises along with the data, each new observation sits close to the freshly updated mean, the running total never builds, and the method reports no change at all. The detector has been tuned to agree with whatever is happening.

Worth separating from a legitimate neighbour: a baseline with known, predictable structure — strong seasonality, say — does need handling, because a chart run against a flat μ will accumulate every summer and alarm on the calendar. The fix there is to remove the known pattern before monitoring, or to use a seasonally appropriate baseline. That is not the same as letting μ drift toward the data, which is what destroys the test.

Where this gets used

CUSUM is often helpful for tracking the performance of models you have put into production. Model performance usually does change over time, because the situation or the underlying data changes, and a sustained drop is the signal that maintenance is due: retrain on newer data, or reconsider whether a different type of model now suits the data better. This is a common use in practice — residuals or an accuracy metric are monitored per period, and a sustained shift triggers the retraining pipeline rather than a fixed calendar schedule. The same machinery is used in public-health outbreak surveillance, in manufacturing quality control, and in tracking defect rates in software builds.

Applications fall into rough categories, and which one you are in should set the parameters:

  • Early detection of big long-term changes — the change is large and you want warning as early as possible.
  • Detecting very short-term changes — the change may not persist, so a slow detector misses it entirely.
  • Distinguishing small changes from random noise — the hardest case, and the one where the C-versus-T argument matters most.

There are cases in between, but naming the category first is what makes a parameter choice defensible rather than arbitrary.

Conventions you will meet outside this course

Standard practice in statistical process control expresses both parameters in standard deviations and gives starting values: set C to roughly half the shift you want to catch — C ≈ 0.5σ if a 1σ shift is what matters — and T to about 4σ or 5σ, then tune. The trade is usually quantified as average run length: how many observations pass before an alarm. ARL with no change present is the false-alarm rate; ARL once a change has occurred is the detection delay. Raising T improves the first and worsens the second, which is the same trade described above with a number attached. Two further practical notes: the sum is normally reset after an alarm has been investigated, so old evidence does not carry into the next decision; and CUSUM earns its keep on small sustained shifts — for a very large sudden jump, a simple “is this observation extreme?” chart signals just as fast.

You are monitoring a fraud model in production. Weekly precision is noisy, and the failure you fear is a slow drift that costs money for months before anyone notices. A colleague proposes C = 0 with a high T, and recomputing μ each week from the last twelve weeks so the baseline “stays current”. Respond to both halves.

Your answerclick to type; saves automatically
Show a strong answer

On C = 0 with a high T: it will control false alarms, so it is not absurd — but it is the wrong trade here. With weekly precision noisy, runs of random above-mean weeks will accumulate in full, so T has to be set high to survive them. That inflated T then has to be climbed by the real drift as well, and since the drift is slow the total builds slowly, so detection arrives months late — the exact failure we are trying to avoid. Use C to absorb the weekly noise, set it around half the drop that would actually matter to the business, and T can then be low enough to catch the drift while it is still small.

On the rolling twelve-week μ: this does not trade anything off, it disables the detector. A slow drift is precisely a change the rolling mean will follow: each week's precision sits close to a baseline computed from the recently degraded weeks, the deviation stays near zero, the total never builds, and the chart certifies that nothing is happening while performance decays. This is the salary case — an enormous real change made invisible by a mean that chases the data. μ should be fixed at the performance level established when the model was validated, because that is the thing we want to know we have departed from.

The distinction worth making explicit: if weekly precision has genuine known seasonality — holiday fraud patterns, say — that is a real objection to a flat baseline, and the answer is to remove the known seasonal pattern or use a season-appropriate baseline. It is not to let μ follow whatever the data happens to be doing.

Two knobs, two jobs

In one line each: what does C do, and what does T do?

Show answer

C is subtracted from every deviation, so ordinary variation never enters the running total — it sets how much noise you agree to ignore. T is the level the total must reach before you declare a change — it sets how much accumulated evidence you demand, and so how sensitive the detector is. C filters; T judges. And μ stays fixed, or there is nothing to detect a departure from.

Worked examples for every topic

Each topic on this page is a judgement call, and judgement is easier to reproduce from cases than from definitions. These are concrete instances of each one, including the cases that cut the other way.

How to use this

Read a case, decide before reading the verdict, and pay particular attention to the ones marked cuts the other way — they are the ones that separate an answer that reproduces a rule from an answer that understands it. Sources beyond the instructor notes are general reference material, not lecture content.

Topic 1 — Margin width

  • Self-reported income on a loan application. An applicant writes “$50,000” when the true figure is $49,872. A boundary drawn tightly through the training points may put that applicant on the wrong side purely because of the rounding. A wider margin absorbs the displacement. Hard-classification argument: robustness to measurement error.
  • A lab assay with ±2% instrument error. If the separating boundary sits inside the instrument's own error band, the classification is partly a readout of the instrument's noise. The margin should exceed the known measurement error before the decision means anything.
  • A spam filter trained on one month of mail. Borderline messages reflect that particular month. Widening the margin reduces the influence of those specific points, at the price of training errors. Soft-classification argument: robustness to the sample you drew.
  • Cuts the other way: a filter separating aggressive marketing from actual spam. The borderline cases are the commercially important ones, and they may carry real information about where the line belongs. Widening the margin here can cost precision exactly where the model earns its money.

Topic 2 — Scaling against standardization

  • Scale: exam score out of 100, RGB channel 0–255, humidity as a percentage, battery charge, an estimated probability, age. All have a real floor and ceiling, and real data sits at them.
  • Standardize: household income, commute distance, transaction value, wind speed. No natural ceiling, and often a reasonable fit to a normal distribution. A different distribution? Standardize to that one instead.
  • Combination case — income with a handful of extreme earners. Scaling to 0–1 against a billionaire crushes everyone else into the bottom sliver of the range. Remove the far outliers and scale the rest, or clip at ±3 standard deviations and map that window onto 0–1.
  • Cuts the other way: columns that are already indicator variables taking values 0 and 1. They are on a common footing already; standardizing them rescales each by how rare its category is, which is a weighting decision you probably did not intend to make.

Topic 3 — KNN against SVM

  • Ten classes, modest data, no time pressure — handwritten digit recognition on a research dataset. KNN handles many classes without any extra machinery.
  • Binary decision, millions of rows, an answer owed in milliseconds — card authorisation at the point of sale. Training an SVM is expensive but happens once; prediction is a single arithmetic comparison. KNN inverts that cost profile, since every prediction searches the training data.
  • Severe imbalance — fraud at 0.2% of transactions. A new point's nearest neighbours are almost all legitimate whatever the truth, so the raw vote is near-useless without weighting or resampling. No balancing approach is perfect; say so.
  • Cuts the other way: if you need a probability rather than a label, note that the KNN fraction is coarse — with k = 5 the only available answers are 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1 — and least reliable at exactly the extremes where a confident answer would matter most.

Topic 4 — Units and distance

  • The headline case: one feature recorded in millimetres, another in kilometres. The millimetre numbers are around a million times larger, so differences in that feature swamp the distance calculation and the model is effectively deciding on one feature.
  • The quiet everyday case: age in years (roughly 18–80) alongside annual income in dollars (roughly 20,000–200,000). Income differences are thousands of times larger. Nothing errors out; the model simply ignores age.
  • House prices: bedrooms (1–6) against floor area in square feet (500–5,000). Choosing to record area in square metres instead changes which neighbours are “nearest”, and so changes the prediction.
  • Scope: this is not a KNN quirk. Anything deciding by distance inherits it — k-means, SVMs with a distance-based kernel, PCA, anomaly scores built on nearest-neighbour distance. Choosing units is choosing weights.

Topic 5 — Validation bias

  • Thirty near-tied candidates. The winner scores 94% on validation and 91% on test. Nothing broke: you selected the model that caught a favourable roll on that particular set, and the favour did not travel.
  • The public-leaderboard effect. In competitions where a public score is computed on one slice and the final standing on another, rankings routinely rearrange at the reveal. That is this phenomenon at scale, with a paper trail.
  • Slow leakage. Fifty rounds of “tweak, re-score on validation, keep if better” is selection against that set even without a formal model comparison. The validation figure stops being an estimate long before anyone notices.
  • Cuts the other way: the test set is not better data. It contains its own random patterns. Its value comes entirely from having been untouched while you were choosing — spend it and it becomes another validation set.

Topic 6 — Classification against clustering

  • Classification: is this email spam; will this customer churn within 90 days; which digit is this. A correct answer exists for each data point, and someone can in principle check it.
  • Clustering: what natural groupings exist among our customers. There is no correct answer per customer, so the output is a proposal about structure, not a set of facts about individuals.
  • The phrasing test: “which of these categories does it belong to” supplies the categories — supervised. “What categories are there” asks for them to be discovered — unsupervised.
  • Cuts the other way: naming your clusters afterwards (“bargain hunters”, “loyalists”) does not convert them into labels. The names are your interpretation of the grouping, and the grouping would change with the distance measure, the scaling, or k. Claims your method cannot support are exactly what this topic is about.

Topic 7 — Kinds of outlier

  • Point: a single $40,000 charge on a card that normally sees $80 purchases. A sensor reporting 300° in a system that runs between 60° and 80°. Easy to detect — the nearest-neighbour distance is enormous.
  • Contextual: 20°C is a pleasant summer reading and a remarkable one on Christmas Day in a cold climate; the value is ordinary, its position is not. A $5,000 transaction is routine on a corporate account and striking on a personal one. Detectable by the same distance reasoning applied within context.
  • Collective: fifty failed logins from different addresses against one account — each attempt is unremarkable, the set is an attack. Every car on a motorway changing to the left lane at the same moment; lane changes are ordinary, simultaneity is not. A stretch of an ECG holding a value that is itself perfectly normal for far too long. Nothing to circle, which is the difficulty.
  • The three verdicts, worked on one flagged transaction: if the amount was mis-keyed by a data-entry error, it is bad data — drop it. If it is a genuine corporate bulk purchase sitting in a model of consumer spending, it is correct but irrelevant — it can be dropped. If it is a genuine fraudulent charge in a model built to catch fraud, it is correct and relevant — and dropping it removes the very thing you are modelling.
  • Cuts the other way: note that the last two cases are indistinguishable by any test of the data itself. Only the question the model answers separates them.

Topic 8 — CUSUM parameters

  • Small change against noise — weekly precision of a deployed fraud model. Week-to-week variation is large relative to the drift you fear, so C does the heavy lifting: set it near half the drop that would actually matter, then keep T low enough to catch the drift while it is still small.
  • Early detection of a large long-term change — daily emergency-department visits during outbreak surveillance. A missed outbreak costs far more than an investigated false alarm, so T is set low and the false-alarm rate is accepted as the price.
  • Short-lived changes — a transient equipment fault that lasts hours. A slow detector does not report it late; it never reports it at all, because the total decays back to zero before reaching T.
  • Manufacturing and software — tablet weight or coating thickness on a production line, defect counts per software build. Both are the classic small-sustained-shift case that CUSUM was designed for.
  • Cuts the other way: for a very large sudden jump, CUSUM offers little over simply asking whether the latest observation is extreme. Its advantage is on small shifts that accumulate, which is also why the C-against-T argument matters most there.

Each counter-case is the point where the rule stops being a rule and turns back into a question about the data or the purpose. Widening the margin is right until the boundary points are the informative ones. Standardizing is right until the column was already on a common footing. The test set is cleaner only until you spend it. Dropping outliers is right until the outlier is the phenomenon. CUSUM beats a simple extremeness check only while the shift is small.

What they share is that the deciding fact is never in the technique — it is in what the data is like and what question is being asked. That is a strong hint about examination: a question that supplies a context is usually testing whether you noticed which side of the counter-case it falls on, and an answer that states the general rule without engaging with the supplied context will miss the mark even when the rule is stated correctly.

What these have in common

The through-line across the discussion topics so far

  • Topic 1Margin width is a bet about how much you trust the position of the points nearest the boundary.
  • Topic 2Scaling or standardizing is a bet about whether the bounds of an attribute mean anything.
  • Topic 3KNN against SVM is a bet about the shape of the classes, the balance of the data and how fast you owe an answer.
  • Topic 4Units decide what “nearest” means, so an arbitrary recording choice becomes a modelling choice.
  • Topic 5The score you selected with is a bet you already cashed, which is why it can’t also serve as the estimate.
  • Topic 6Whether a correct answer exists at all decides which family of method you are even in.
  • Topic 7Hardest to find and hardest to judge are different rankings, and finding an outlier settles nothing on its own.
  • Topic 8C decides how much noise you ignore, T decides how much evidence you need, and a mean that chases the data detects nothing.

Things worth noticing

  • None of these has a default answer. Every one resolves from a property of the data — measurement error, bounds, balance, throughput. Anyone who answers without asking about the data is guessing.
  • Two of them are really the same problem. Scaling and the units example are one issue seen from opposite ends: units weight distances, and scaling is how you take that weighting back under control.
  • The honest verdicts are part of the material. No balancing approach is perfect. The KNN probability estimate is unreliable at the extremes. A wider margin can cost precision. Reproducing the caveat is usually worth as much as reproducing the technique.
  • One finding overrides the method choice entirely. If the classes stay mixed under every separator, the problem is the predictors, not the model.
  • Two of them are really about honesty rather than technique. Topic 5 is a rule about which number you are allowed to quote, and Topic 6 is a rule about which claims your method can support at all. Neither changes a line of code, and both change what you are entitled to say.
  • Judgement and detection are separable skills. Topic 7 makes the point explicitly — the type that is hardest to find is not the type that is hardest to rule on — but it applies further back too. Spotting that a validation score is inflated is easy; deciding what to report instead is not.
  • Parameters encode the same trades as model choices. Topic 8 is Topic 1 in a different costume: C buys tolerance for noise the way a wide margin buys tolerance for misplaced points, and both are paid for in sensitivity to real signal. Anyone asking for faster detection and fewer false alarms at once is asking for the trade to go away.
  • This list is not closed. Every topic here arrived because the instructor thought the lecture version was too clean. More will arrive on the same terms, and the pattern of what gets singled out — judgement calls the lectures state as rules — is itself a hint about what gets examined.
