Topic 1

What a model estimates

Every method on this page estimates one unknown function linking predictors to a response, and what separates them is whether you want to predict the response or understand the function.

The one equation underneath everything

Y  =  f( X )  +  ε X is the set of predictors, Y the response, f the systematic information X carries about Y, and ε a random error term independent of X with mean zero. Every method here is an attempt to estimate f.

Because ε cannot be predicted from X by construction, the error of any model splits in two:

Reducible error

The part caused by your estimate f̂ differing from the true f. Better methods and more data shrink it. This is the part you are paid to reduce.

Irreducible error

The part caused by ε — unmeasured variables and genuine randomness. No model, however good, gets below it. On the chart at the top of this page it is the dashed floor.

Why this distinction is examinable

It tells you when to stop. A model whose error has flattened just above the noise floor is finished, and further complexity will make it worse rather than better. A model far above the floor still has recoverable structure. Being unable to tell these apart is how people over-fit while believing they are improving.

Prediction or inference

Two reasons to estimate f, and they pull in opposite directions.

GoalWhat you needWhat you tolerate
PredictionAccurate Ŷ for new Xf̂ can be a black box. Nobody has to understand it if it is right.
InferenceTo know which predictors matter, in which direction, and how stronglySome accuracy, in exchange for a form you can read and defend.

This is the trade behind almost every method comparison later: a flexible method usually predicts better and explains worse. Linear regression sits at one extreme — restrictive, but every coefficient means something. A boosted ensemble sits at the other.

Parametric methods

Assume a form for f — a line, say — and then estimate a fixed, small number of parameters. Reduces the problem to estimating a handful of numbers, at the cost of being wrong if the assumed form is wrong.

Non-parametric methods

Assume no particular form and let the data shape f̂. Avoids the risk of a badly chosen form, but needs far more observations to pin down a shape that a parametric method gets from an assumption.

Topic 2

Measuring model quality

Error on the data a model learned from always understates error on new data, and the gap is not noise — it is a systematic trade between bias and variance whose position you choose.

Training error is not an estimate of anything useful

MSE  =  (1/n)   Σᵢ   ( yᵢ − f̂(xᵢ) )² Computed on the training data this is training MSE; computed on data the model never saw it is test MSE. Only the second answers the question you care about.

A flexible enough method drives training MSE toward zero by chasing the noise in the training set. That noise will not repeat in new data, so the same wiggles that lowered training error raise test error. This is overfitting: not a moral failure, just the point past which extra flexibility is buying pattern that isn’t there.

The shape to memorise

Training error falls monotonically with flexibility. Test error falls, bottoms out, and rises — the U in the chart at the top of this page. A minimum always exists somewhere, and finding it is what Topic 3 is for.

Why the U exists

E( y₀ − f̂(x₀) )²  =  Var( f̂(x₀) )  +  [ Bias( f̂(x₀) ) ]²  +  Var( ε ) Expected test error at a point splits into three non-negative pieces, so minimising it means making the first two small at once. The third is the floor.
Variance

How much f̂ would change if you refitted on a different training set from the same population. Flexible methods have high variance — move one point and the fit moves with it.

Bias

Error from approximating a complicated reality with a simpler model. Restrictive methods have high bias — a straight line fitted to a curve is wrong in the same way every time, no matter how much data you give it.

More flexibility lowers bias and raises variance. The test-error minimum is where the two rates of change cancel, and it sits at different places for different problems — the more non-linear the truth, the further right the minimum lies.

Measuring quality for classification

For a categorical response, squared error makes no sense; count mistakes instead.

error rate  =  (1/n)   Σᵢ   I( yᵢ ≠ ŷᵢ ) I is 1 when the prediction is wrong and 0 when it is right. As with MSE, only the test version is informative.

A single error rate hides which mistakes are being made, which matters when the two errors cost differently. A confusion matrix separates them, and two rates come out of it:

Sensitivity (true positive rate)Of the cases that really are positive, the fraction the model catches
Specificity (true negative rate)Of the cases that really are negative, the fraction the model leaves alone. 1 − specificity is the false positive rate

Moving the probability threshold trades one against the other. The ROC curve plots every trade at once, and the area under it summarises the classifier independently of any single threshold.

Topic 3

Validation and resampling

Since test error cannot be observed, you manufacture it by holding data out — and k-fold cross-validation is the arrangement that wastes the least data while staying nearly unbiased.

The simplest version, and what is wrong with it

Split the data in two: fit on one half, measure error on the other. This validation set approach is easy and has two real defects.

  • It is highly variable. Repeat the split with a different random half and the error estimate moves substantially. An estimate that depends on which coin you flipped is a weak basis for choosing a model.
  • It overestimates error. The model is fitted on half the data, and methods generally do worse with less. So the estimate is pessimistic about the model you would actually deploy, which is fitted on everything.

k-fold cross-validation

  1. Divide the observations at random into k groups of roughly equal size, called folds.
  2. Hold out fold 1, fit the model on the remaining k − 1 folds, and record the error on fold 1.
  3. Repeat, holding out each fold in turn, so every observation is used for validation exactly once.
  4. Average the k error estimates. That average is the cross-validation estimate of test error.
CV(k)  =  (1/k)   Σki=1   MSEi MSEᵢ is the error on fold i from a model fitted without it. For classification, substitute the error rate.
Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV)

The extreme case k = n: each fit leaves out a single observation. Almost unbiased, because each model sees nearly all the data — but it requires n fits, and the n estimates are highly correlated with each other because the training sets overlap almost completely. Averaging highly correlated quantities reduces variance very little.

Why k = 5 or 10 is the standard answer

LOOCV wins on bias and loses on variance and cost; a single validation split wins on cost and loses on both. Five or ten folds sit near the best of the three: little bias, because 80% or 90% of the data is used for each fit, and much less variance than LOOCV because the training sets overlap less and the estimates are therefore less correlated.

Topic 4

Classification

Each classifier is a different bet about the shape of the boundary, and the right bet depends on how much data you have relative to how complicated the truth is.

Why not just run a regression?

Code the response 0 and 1 and fit a line, and two things go wrong. The fitted values run below 0 and above 1, so they cannot be read as probabilities. And with three or more categories, any numeric coding imposes an ordering and equal spacing that the categories do not have.

Logistic regression

p(X)  =  e^(β₀ + β₁X) / ( 1 + e^(β₀ + β₁X) )

log ( p(X) / (1 − p(X)) )  =  β₀ + β₁X The second line is the first rearranged. The quantity p/(1−p) is the odds; its logarithm is the log-odds, or logit, and it is linear in X. So the model is linear — just not in p.

Reading the coefficient correctly is a standard exam trap. A one-unit increase in X changes the log-odds by β₁, and multiplies the odds by e^β₁. It does not change p by a fixed amount: the effect on the probability depends on where you start, being largest near p = 0.5 and vanishing at the extremes. Coefficients are fitted by maximum likelihood rather than least squares.

The other classifiers, and what each assumes

MethodThe bet it makes
Logistic regressionThe log-odds are linear in the predictors. Boundary is linear. Robust when classes overlap heavily
Linear discriminant analysisEach class is normal with a shared covariance matrix. Also gives a linear boundary, and is more stable than logistic regression when classes are well separated or the sample is small
Quadratic discriminant analysisNormal classes, but each with its own covariance. Boundary becomes quadratic — more flexible, more parameters, needs more data
Naive BayesWithin a class, the predictors are independent of each other. Almost always false, and often works anyway, because it buys an enormous reduction in variance
k-nearest neighboursNothing about shape at all. Fully non-parametric, so it can approximate any boundary — and needs a lot of data to do it, with no coefficients to interpret at the end
Support vector machinesA boundary with the widest margin, optionally bent by a kernel. Determined only by the points near the boundary

The rule that ties the table together

When the true boundary really is close to linear, the linear methods win, because they estimate fewer things and estimate them more precisely. When it is strongly non-linear, the flexible methods win, provided the sample is big enough to support them. There is no method that is best everywhere — that is the bias–variance trade of Topic 2 wearing different clothes.

Topic 5

Regression

Linear regression is the baseline every other method is measured against, and most of its practical difficulty lies in diagnosing when its assumptions have failed rather than in fitting it.

The model and what it is fitted by

Y  =  β₀  +  β₁X₁  +  β₂X₂  +  …  +  βpXp  +  ε Coefficients are chosen to minimise the residual sum of squares, Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² — the vertical distances from the points to the fitted surface, squared and added up.
How to read a coefficient in multiple regression

βj is the average change in Y for a one-unit change in Xj with every other predictor held fixed. That last clause is the whole difficulty: if the predictors move together in the real world, holding one fixed describes a situation that may never occur, and the coefficient becomes hard to interpret even though it is correctly estimated.

The questions a regression output is answering

QuestionWhat answers it
Is there any relationship at all?The F-statistic, testing all coefficients jointly against zero. Ask this before looking at individual predictors
Which predictors matter?Individual t-statistics and p-values — but with many predictors, some will look significant by chance alone
How well does the model fit?R², the proportion of variance explained, and the residual standard error in the units of Y
How accurate is a prediction?A confidence interval for the average response, or a prediction interval for one new observation. The prediction interval is always wider, because it must also absorb the irreducible error

R² never falls when you add a predictor

Adding any predictor, even a column of random numbers, can only leave R² unchanged or raise it — so R² cannot be used to compare models with different numbers of predictors. This is exactly the training-error problem from Topic 2 in a different costume, and it is what motivates the adjusted criteria in Topic 6.

When the assumptions fail

Fitting is automatic; noticing that the fit is inappropriate is not. The standard failures, and the symptom each leaves behind:

ProblemWhat you see
Non-linearityA curved pattern in the residuals plotted against fitted values. The single most important diagnostic — if the relationship is not linear, every conclusion below is unreliable
Correlated errorsResiduals that track each other in sequence, common in time-ordered data. Standard errors come out too small, so predictors look more significant than they are
Non-constant varianceA funnel shape in the residual plot — spread growing with the fitted value. Often fixed by transforming the response with a log or a square root
OutliersAn unusual value of the response. Inflates the residual standard error and R² while often barely moving the line itself
High-leverage pointsAn unusual value of the predictors. These do move the line, sometimes a great deal, and are more dangerous than outliers for exactly that reason
CollinearityPredictors carrying nearly the same information. Coefficients become unstable with inflated standard errors, so a genuinely useful predictor can appear insignificant
Extending beyond a straight line

Interaction terms let one predictor’s effect depend on another’s. Polynomial terms bend the fit. Splines fit separate low-degree polynomials on intervals of the predictor and join them smoothly, which usually behaves better than a single high-degree polynomial. All of these are still linear models — linear in the coefficients, not in the predictors.

Trap to avoid

Outlier or high-leverage point — which does more damage?

Show answer

The high-leverage point, usually. An outlier is unusual in the response and mostly inflates the residual standard error and depresses R², often leaving the line roughly where it was. A high-leverage point is unusual in the predictors and can drag the whole fit, so it changes conclusions rather than just confidence in them.

Topic 6

Variable selection and regularization

Shrinking coefficients toward zero trades a little bias for a large drop in variance, and whether the shrinkage can reach exactly zero decides whether you also get variable selection for free.

Why not keep every predictor?

Because each additional predictor adds a coefficient that must be estimated, and every estimate carries variance. Predictors with no real relationship to the response contribute variance and no bias reduction, which is a losing trade. When the number of predictors approaches the number of observations, least squares becomes so unstable as to be useless; beyond that it has no unique solution at all.

Approach one: choose a subset

Best subset selectionFit every possible combination of predictors and pick the best. Exhaustive and honest, but the number of models is 2^p, so it becomes impossible past around forty predictors — and searching that many models makes it easy to find one that looks good by chance
Forward stepwiseStart with nothing and add the predictor that helps most, one at a time. Far cheaper, and usable when p exceeds n, but it can miss the best subset because an early choice is never revisited
Backward stepwiseStart with everything and drop the least useful predictor, one at a time. Requires n larger than p, since it has to fit the full model first

How the winner is chosen matters

Comparing models of different sizes cannot be done with training error or R², for the reason in Topic 5. Either adjust for size with a criterion that penalises the number of predictors — Cp, AIC, BIC, adjusted R² — or estimate test error directly by cross-validation. The second is more general and makes fewer assumptions, and is the approach the course leans on.

Approach two: shrink instead of choose

Rather than including a predictor or excluding it, fit all of them and penalise large coefficients. Both methods below minimise the residual sum of squares plus a penalty, and λ controls how hard the penalty bites.

ridge:   minimise   RSS  +  λ Σⱼ βⱼ²
lasso:   minimise   RSS  +  λ Σⱼ | βⱼ | At λ = 0 both reduce to least squares. As λ → ∞ both drive coefficients toward zero. The only difference is squared versus absolute value — and that difference changes everything.

The consequence: ridge shrinks coefficients smoothly toward zero but never reaches it, so every predictor stays in the model. The lasso can set a coefficient to exactly zero, which removes that predictor entirely — so the lasso performs variable selection as a side effect of shrinking, and produces a model you can read.

Scale the predictors first

Both penalties add up coefficients, and a coefficient’s size depends on the units of its predictor. Measure one variable in dollars and another in thousands of dollars and the penalty will fall on them completely differently, for no reason connected to the data. Standardizing the predictors before fitting is not optional here — unlike in least squares, where rescaling a predictor simply rescales its coefficient and changes nothing.

Approach three: reduce the dimensions

Instead of selecting among the predictors, construct a small number of new ones as combinations of the originals, then regress on those. Principal components regression uses the directions of greatest variance in the predictors, which are Topic 8’s principal components. It is a purely unsupervised construction: the response never influences which directions are chosen, which is both its safety and its weakness — the highest-variance direction is not guaranteed to be the one that predicts Y. Partial least squares fixes that by letting the response guide the construction.

In one sentence, why can lasso zero a coefficient when ridge cannot?

Show answer

The absolute-value penalty keeps applying constant pressure as a coefficient approaches zero, so it can be pushed all the way there and held; the squared penalty’s pressure fades as the coefficient shrinks, so it approaches zero without arriving.

Topic 7

Tree-based models

A single tree is readable and unstable, and every improvement on it — bagging, random forests, boosting — buys accuracy by growing many trees and giving up the picture.

One tree

A decision tree splits the predictor space into rectangles by asking one yes-or-no question at a time, and predicts the same value everywhere inside a rectangle — the average response there for regression, the most common class for classification. Splits are chosen greedily: at each step, take the split that most reduces error right now, without looking ahead.

Grow then prune, don’t stop early

Stopping as soon as a split fails to help is short-sighted: a weak split can be followed by a very good one. The standard remedy is to grow a large tree deliberately, then prune it back, using cross-validation to choose how far. A tree left unpruned fits the training data beautifully and generalises badly — Topic 2 again.

Many trees

One tree has high variance: refit it on a different sample and you can get a visibly different tree. Averaging fixes that, and the three standard ways of averaging differ in how they make the trees disagree.

MethodHow it works, and what it fixes
BaggingGrow many deep trees on bootstrap resamples of the data and average their predictions. Averaging reduces variance, so the individual trees are left unpruned on purpose. The problem: if one predictor is dominant, every tree splits on it first and the trees are highly correlated, which limits how much averaging helps
Random forestsBagging, plus one change — at each split, only a random subset of predictors is considered. This forces trees to use other predictors and decorrelates them, so averaging works much better. The subset size is the tuning parameter
BoostingGrow small trees in sequence, each fitted to what the current ensemble still gets wrong, and add it in with a small learning rate. Nothing is resampled and nothing is averaged; the model is built up slowly. Usually the most accurate, and the only one of the three that can overfit if grown too long
What you lose, and the partial compensation

The moment there is more than one tree there is no diagram to show anyone. Variable importance measures — how much each predictor reduced error across all the trees — recover a ranking of predictors, but not a rule you can follow or a coefficient you can sign. If interpretation is the goal, a single pruned tree may still be the right answer despite being less accurate.

put in ordersolved

Drag the reasoning from one tree to a forest into sequence — or use the arrows.

  1. Grow a single tree by splitting greedily, then prune it back by cross-validation
  2. Notice that the tree is unstable — a different sample gives a visibly different tree
  3. Grow many unpruned trees on bootstrap resamples and average them, to cut variance
  4. Notice that a dominant predictor makes every tree split the same way, so they stay correlated
  5. Restrict each split to a random subset of predictors, forcing the trees to differ
  6. Average the now-decorrelated trees, and accept that the picture is gone

Exam target

What single change turns bagging into a random forest, and why does it help?

Show answer

Only a random subset of predictors is considered at each split. This stops a dominant predictor being chosen first in every tree, so the trees are less correlated — and averaging correlated quantities reduces variance far less than averaging uncorrelated ones.

Topic 8

Unsupervised methods

With no response to predict there is nothing to validate against, so principal components and clusters are judged by whether their output is useful rather than by whether it is right.

The difficulty that defines the whole topic

Supervised methods have an answer key: hold data out, compare predictions to truth, get a number. Unsupervised methods have none. There is no cross-validation for a clustering, because there is nothing to be wrong about. This makes the analysis more subjective and the results harder to defend — which is why it usually appears as one step in a larger pipeline rather than as the deliverable.

Principal component analysis

Principal component

A normalised linear combination of the predictors. The first principal component is the direction along which the data varies most; the second is the direction of greatest remaining variance among those perpendicular to the first, and so on. Each successive component is uncorrelated with all the ones before it.

Two equivalent readings, both worth having: the first component is the direction of maximum variance, and it is also the line closest to the data in squared-distance terms. PCA is therefore a way of representing many correlated predictors with a few uncorrelated ones — which is why it appears both as a visualisation tool and as the front end of principal components regression in Topic 6.

Scaling decides the answer

PCA maximises variance, and variance depends on units. A predictor measured in a large unit dominates the first component for no substantive reason. Unless every predictor is already in the same unit, standardize before running it — otherwise the components describe your measuring choices rather than your data. A scree plot of the proportion of variance explained is the usual way to choose how many components to keep, with the elbow taken as the cut-off.

Clustering

MethodWhat you must decide first
k-meansThe number of clusters, K, before you start. The algorithm assigns points to the nearest centre, recomputes the centres, and repeats until nothing moves. It finds a local optimum that depends on the random initialisation, so it should be run several times from different starts and the best result kept
Hierarchical clusteringNothing up front — it builds a dendrogram of nested groupings, and you choose where to cut afterwards. But you must choose a dissimilarity measure and a linkage rule, and those choices change the answer as much as K does
Why clustering results are fragile

Whether the predictors were standardized, which dissimilarity measure was used, which linkage, what K is, and where the dendrogram is cut all change the output — and none of them can be settled by an error measure, because there is none. The honest practice is to try several settings, report what is stable across them, and treat clusters as a hypothesis rather than a finding.

How the topics fit together

The through-line across all eight

  • Topic 1Establishes that every method estimates one unknown function, and that some error can never be removed.
  • Topic 2Shows why more flexibility eventually makes things worse, and names the two forces behind it.
  • Topic 3Supplies the only honest way to find the turning point, by holding data out.
  • Topic 4Applies the trade to boundaries: each classifier is a different assumption about shape.
  • Topic 5Sets the low-variance, high-bias baseline, and catalogues how its assumptions fail.
  • Topic 6Buys variance reduction with deliberate bias, and gets variable selection thrown in.
  • Topic 7Takes the opposite route — high-variance models averaged until the variance goes away.
  • Topic 8Removes the response, and with it every method of checking whether you were right.

Things worth noticing

  • Topic 2 is the whole page in one sentence. Regularization adds bias to remove variance. Random forests remove variance by averaging. Pruning removes variance by simplifying. Cross-validation locates the point where the trade turns. If you can only remember one thing, remember the U.
  • Two opposite strategies, same target. Topic 6 takes a low-variance method and deliberately makes it more biased; Topic 7 takes high-variance trees and averages the variance away. They meet in the middle from opposite sides, and neither is universally better.
  • Standardization is load-bearing in three separate places. Ridge and lasso penalties depend on units; PCA maximises variance, which depends on units; distance-based methods add up squared differences, which depend on units. It never affects ordinary least squares, which is why people forget it matters elsewhere.
  • Training error is untrustworthy in the same way three times. Training MSE falls with flexibility, R² never falls when a predictor is added, and an unpruned tree fits perfectly. Three faces of one fact.
  • Interpretability is a constraint, not a bonus. Logistic regression over a black box, a pruned tree over a forest, lasso over ridge — each is a case where the readable answer is chosen despite being less accurate. Knowing when that trade is right is what the course actually asks for.
