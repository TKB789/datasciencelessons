Lesson 1

Preparing data

Three things can quietly wreck a model before it is even fitted — mismatched scales, a handful of extreme points, and columns carrying no information — and all three are fixable in advance.

The gap between how we talk and what we do

Throughout this course, data is described as though it went straight into the model: a predictor in a regression, a factor in a classification model. Credit score. Average daily temperature. Units sold at each event. The value of a share at five-second intervals. The concentration of a chemical in drinking water.

The notation encourages this. Writing xij for attribute j of data point i makes it sound as though xij is simply the number that was measured — the exact count of units sold, the exact share price.

Data preparation

Everything done to a dataset between collecting it and handing it to a model. When a course says “assume the data is given”, this is the work it is assuming you already did.

Three problems make that assumption unsafe.

Why scale is a problem and not just an inconvenience

Put average household income and credit score side by side. Income runs into the hundreds of thousands; a credit score runs to a few hundred. Nothing about that is wrong — the two quantities simply measure different things in different units.

The trouble is that many algorithms add up contributions from every attribute, and an attribute measured in larger numbers contributes larger numbers. The optimizer then spends its effort where the numbers are big, which is a fact about the units rather than about the world. That is what “leading our algorithms down the wrong path” means concretely.

Scale and extreme points are properties of the numbers in a column. Extraneous information is a property of which columns you kept at all — so it is fixed by removing or combining columns rather than by transforming values. That is why it needs a different family of methods.

Lesson 2

Finding outliers

An outlier is a point that doesn’t fit its context — which is why there are several kinds of them, and no single test that finds them all.

The definition, and immediately a complication

Outlier

A data point that is very different from the rest of the dataset.

The obvious case is a single value far from everything else. That is what most people picture when they hear the word, and it has a name: a point outlier.

But suppose that alongside one far-flung value there is also a small huddle of two or three points, well away from the mass of the data though not as extreme as the single one. Are those outliers too? There are only a few of them, and they are clearly different from the overwhelming majority — which is the definition. Yet they might have a perfectly good reason for being there.

Why the answer is “it depends”, and more so here than usual

Outliers carry philosophical questions as well as statistical ones. Deciding that a point “doesn’t belong” is a claim about what the dataset is supposed to represent, and no amount of arithmetic settles that. Lesson 3 is entirely about this problem; the rest of this lesson only covers spotting candidates.

Two further kinds show up once the data has a time attribute.

Vocabulary

Why is a contextual outlier called contextual?

Show answer

Because the surrounding points supply the context that makes it anomalous. Its own value is unremarkable; only its position relative to the rest of the series — the time at which it occurs — makes it stand out.

Finding them automatically: one dimension

Eyeballing pictures is the easy part. For a single quantitative attribute there is a standard tool.

Box and whisker plot

The top and bottom of the box are the 75th and 25th percentiles; the line through the box is the median, the 50th. The whiskers run out to whatever you consider a reasonable range — the 10th and 90th percentiles, say, or the 5th and 95th. Every value beyond the whiskers is plotted individually, and those points are the possible outliers.

A convention this lesson doesn’t mention

Most statistical software does not use percentile whiskers by default. It extends them to the furthest point within 1.5 × IQR of the box, where IQR is the 75th minus the 25th percentile. For the data above that gives 44.75 − 1.5 × 8.75 = 31.6 and 53.5 + 1.5 × 8.75 = 66.6, so only 71 and 96 get flagged — half as many points as the percentile rule. Same data, same plot type, different answer. Know which convention your software used before you report a count of outliers.

Exam target

Name every element of a box and whisker plot and say what each shows.

Show answer

Box edges: the 25th and 75th percentiles. Line inside the box: the median, the 50th percentile. Whiskers: out to a chosen reasonable range, such as the 10th and 90th or 5th and 95th percentiles. Individual dots beyond the whiskers: the possible outliers.

Finding them automatically: more than one dimension

There is no good all-purpose method for detecting multi-dimensional outliers, or the other types, before modelling. That is a genuine gap rather than an omission from the lesson.

What you can do is turn the problem around: fit a model first, then look at where it is most wrong. Fit an exponential smoothing model to a time series, and in most periods the difference between the actual value and the model’s estimate will be small, because the fitted function is smooth. At an outlier it will not be. The model expects a point sitting on the smooth curve, and the actual value is nowhere near it.

Only half the battle

Everything in this lesson produces candidates. Not one of these methods tells you whether a flagged point is a mistake, a rare but genuine event, or the most informative observation in the dataset. That question is Lesson 3’s, and it cannot be answered from inside the dataset.

Worth noticing

Why does the model-error approach catch contextual outliers when a box plot can’t?

Show answer

A box plot sees only the distribution of values, so a mid-winter reading equal to a normal summer one sits comfortably inside the box. A fitted time-series model expects a particular value at that time, so the same reading produces a large error. The model supplies exactly the context the word “contextual” refers to.

Lesson 3

Dealing with outliers

Finding an outlier is the easy half — deciding whether it is an error, a real rarity worth modelling, or a real event you can’t model, takes investigation outside the dataset.

First question: what caused it?

Everything else follows from this. Often an outlier is simply bad data:

  • A sensor malfunctioned
  • A wireless transmission between sensor and receiver failed
  • An experiment was contaminated — bacteria accidentally introduced into a medical test, say
  • A person entered the wrong figure, or copied the wrong value across

But sometimes an outlier reflects something real in the system. Then you need to find out what caused it and decide whether the model should account for it.

The uncomfortable part

Answering either question — is it real or an error, and if real what to do — usually requires investigation. Where did the data come from? How was it compiled? What was unusual about the circumstances of that particular point? This means looking beyond a dataset and a model. It is also a fast way to make your results actually worth something to whoever asked for them.

  1. Flag the candidate, by any of the methods in Lesson 2.
  2. Trace it: where the value came from, how it was recorded, and what was going on at the time.
  3. Decide whether it is an error or real. This step happens outside the dataset.
  4. If it is an error, omit the point — or, if you need a value in that slot, impute one.
  5. If it is real, ask whether the thing it represents can be predicted at all.
  6. If it can, decide whether your model should represent it, or whether a second model should.
  7. If it can’t, you may still have to remove it — but now you know what you are removing.

Exam target

Give four causes of an outlier that is simply bad data.

Show answer

A malfunctioning sensor; a failed transmission between sensor and receiver; a contaminated experiment; and human error in entering or copying a value.

If it is real: expect some of them

Some outliers are nothing but true randomness. With enough data, extreme values are not merely possible — they are guaranteed.

normally distributed noise:
P( |x − μ| > 2σ )  =  4.55%
P( |x − μ| > 3σ )  =  0.270%  →  about 2,700 points in a million Both figures are two-sided — they count values that are extreme in either direction. Rounding the second to “about 2,000” undercounts it; if you want the one-sided figure, values more than 3σ above the mean, that is roughly 1,350 in a million.

So in a dataset of a million points, thousands of them being far from expectation is not evidence of anything wrong. It is what the distribution says should happen. The question then is not whether these points are legitimate but whether they matter to the model.

The test to apply

If the magnitude of your model’s error is part of how its value is judged, then rare large values may belong in the dataset. Throw them out and the model becomes too optimistic, and reality will supply some bad surprises.

Take a supply chain moving perishable medicine from a manufacturing site to clinics in a remote region. Most shipments take a predictable time. Occasionally severe weather or a border disruption makes one take far longer. Those points are real, they recur, and the cost of underestimating them lands on patients. Deleting them would produce a model that is right about the ordinary case and silent about the case that matters.

Splitting one model into two

You do not have to choose between including outliers and discarding them. A common alternative is to model the two regimes separately:

  1. Build one model — logistic regression, for example — estimating the probability that an outlying event occurs under given conditions.
  2. Build a second model estimating the outcome under normal conditions, fitted to data with the outliers removed.
  3. Combine them when forecasting: how likely is the disruption, and how long does each case take.

Why this often beats a single model

A single model fitted to both regimes is pulled between them and describes neither well. Splitting lets each model see a coherent population. The price is that you now have two models to validate, and a probability estimate that is itself uncertain.

Check

In a million normally distributed observations, roughly how many sit more than three standard deviations from the mean?

Show answer

About 2,700, counting both tails — 0.27% of the data. Roughly 1,350 of those are on the high side. Either way, the point is that thousands of extreme values in a large dataset are expected rather than suspicious.

Real, and still unusable

The last case is the awkward one: an outlier that is genuine data but describes something that will not happen again in any predictable way. Sometimes such points have to be removed even though they are correct.

What makes this case dangerous is that it looks exactly like bad data from the outside. The scenario below is one version of how that goes wrong.

A teammate: “That’s obviously a data error — probably a duplicated import. Drop it and refit; we’re already behind.”

What should Priya do?

The point is confirmed as real. It was driven by a one-off news cycle. Nothing in Priya’s available data — price, promotion, season, store count — would have predicted it, and nobody expects it to recur.

The lesson underneath the story

Even when you are confident you know what an odd data point is, it is worth asking. The cost of one conversation is small; the cost of deleting the most informative row in a dataset is not. And note that checking changed the reason for removing the point without changing the decision — which is still a better position to be in.

Worth noticing

Two of the three destinations for an outlier end in removing it. What distinguishes them?

Show answer

The reason, and therefore what you write down. An erroneous point is removed because it never happened. A verified one-off is removed because it happened but cannot be predicted from the data you have. Only the second obliges you to record the event somewhere, in case it stops being a one-off.

Things worth noticing

  • The module narrows as it goes. Lesson 1 names three problems, Lessons 2 and 3 pursue exactly one of them, and by the end you are looking at a single data point and asking a question no algorithm can settle. That funnel is the shape of the topic.
  • Detection and judgement are strictly separate. Nothing in Lesson 2 tells you whether a flagged point is an error. Nothing in Lesson 3 helps you find one. Confusing the two is how people end up deleting real data because software drew a dot outside a whisker.
  • Every threshold here is chosen, not derived. Where the whiskers go, what counts as “a lot of error” in a fitted model — both are settings. Module 3’s validation material is what turns such choices into defensible ones, and no lesson here says so out loud.
  • Lesson 2’s model-error trick reappears in Module 6. Fitting a model of what the data should look like and then watching the residual is exactly what CUSUM does, except that CUSUM accumulates the residual instead of examining each one alone.
