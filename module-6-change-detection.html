Lesson 1

Change detection

When the unusual points stop being unusual, the question changes from Module 5’s “is this point wrong?” to “has the whole process moved?” — and the answer has to arrive early enough to act on.

What it is

Change detection

Determining whether something has changed. Because change is the subject, the data is nearly always a time series, or at least carries some time attribute.

The questions it answers fall into three families.

FamilyExamples
Do we need to do something?Does a machine showing variation in performance need maintenance, or is this just random variation? Has global temperature changed? Have sales of a product fallen because a new competitor arrived?
Did the action we took have an effect?Did a policy change produce the intended result — did lowering business tax rates lead firms to hire more people? Did a discount promotion actually move sales?
What has drifted over time?Have voting patterns shifted? Anything where the point is to understand the change in order to plan or analyse for the future.

The first family is the demanding one, because acting late is expensive. The other two can usually be answered at leisure with all the data in hand.

Detecting a problem that isn’t a problem yet

One class of application is harder than it looks: you need to detect a change now that predicts trouble later, even though nothing is wrong at the moment.

Traffic on a busy stretch of motorway is the standard illustration. Loop sensors in the road report vehicle counts and average speeds every few seconds. A jam does not appear from nowhere — density builds, speeds start to vary more, and gaps between vehicles shorten, for several minutes before flow actually collapses. By the time speed has dropped, the queue exists and the options for preventing it are gone. The useful question is therefore not “is traffic bad now?” but “is what I am seeing the beginning of a build-up that will be bad in five minutes?”

Why this is a change-detection problem rather than a prediction problem

You are not forecasting a number. You are asking whether the current stream of readings still comes from the same regime it did an hour ago. Once you accept that framing, the method has to work on a running basis, with the data that has arrived so far and no more.

The noise problem, and why a hypothesis test isn’t enough

Consider condition monitoring on a rotating machine — a large pump or motor, with a temperature probe on the bearing housing. Bearings usually fail gradually, and temperature rises before they seize. If the rise can be caught early enough, the machine can be stopped and the bearing replaced during planned downtime instead of failing in service and taking the line with it.

In the ideal case the first high reading would be a reliable warning: the temperature is genuinely elevated, the machine comes out of service, and the failure is prevented.

Real sensor readings do not behave like that. There is a great deal of random fluctuation between readings — ambient temperature, load, probe contact, plain measurement noise. Individual high readings appear routinely and mean nothing. The difficulty is symmetrical: react to every high reading and you stop a healthy machine repeatedly, but wait until you are really sure and the bearing may already have failed.

A natural first attempt is a hypothesis test: has the mean temperature risen, at the usual level of significance? The trouble is timing. Waiting until a test meets a standard threshold means waiting for enough evidence to be confident, and with data this noisy that can take longer than the component has left. The test gives the right answer too late to be worth anything.

The trade that defines the topic

What is needed is a method that identifies potential problems sooner, on the explicit understanding that some of its alarms will be false. Accepting false positives is not a weakness of the approach — it is the price of an answer arriving in time.

Lesson 2

CUSUM

CUSUM accumulates how far above expectation the data has been running, with C setting how much evidence is dismissed as noise and T setting how much is needed before you call it a change.

The question it answers

CUSUM — cumulative sum

A running-total method that answers one question: has the mean of the observed distribution gone beyond a critical level? It can detect a move to a higher level, a move to a lower level, or both at once.

Set up the notation. Let xt be the observed value at time t — a share price on day t, a bearing temperature at reading t, a reaction time on trial t. Let μ be the expected value of those observations if nothing has changed. Then xt − μ is how far above expectation the process ran at time t.

St  =  max { 0 ,   St−1 + ( xt − μ − C ) }    with S0 = 0

declare a change when   St ≥ T Three moving parts. The running total carries evidence forward; C subtracts a little each period so ordinary noise doesn’t accumulate; the max with zero throws the total away whenever it would go negative.

Why each piece is there

  1. The running total. A single high reading proves nothing; several in a row is evidence. Adding each period’s excess to the previous total is what turns a sequence of weak signals into a strong one.
  2. The reset at zero. If the total would go below zero, set it to zero instead. A run of lower-than-expected observations is irrelevant to the question of whether an increase happens later, and letting it bank negative credit would delay the eventual detection. This is what lets CUSUM react quickly.
  3. The constant C. Roughly half the time, an observation will exceed the expectation purely at random. Without something pulling the total down, that noise alone would eventually push St past any threshold. C is the amount of excess dismissed as noise each period.

How the two parameters behave

The bigger C is, the harder it is for St to get large, and the less sensitive the method. The smaller C is, the more sensitive, because St grows faster. T works the same way in the other place: a high threshold means more evidence before an alarm, so later detection and fewer false ones. Neither has a formula — they are model parameters you fit to data.

Choosing C and T is a cost question

There is no simple answer for what C and T should be. Part of the decision is statistical, and part of it is about which mistake hurts more.

Take a chip manufacturing process where a machine has begun placing a component slightly too far to the left. Every chip produced between the moment the drift starts and the moment it is detected has to be scrapped, so late detection costs material and time. But if CUSUM calls a change when the machine is fine, production stops while a crew inspects and adjusts equipment that was working. Setting C and T is trading those two costs against each other.

If you…Then…
Lower C or lower TChanges are detected sooner, and false alarms become more frequent. Right when late detection is the expensive mistake.
Raise C or raise TFalse alarms become rarer, and real changes take longer to surface. Right when interrupting a healthy process is the expensive mistake.

Detecting decreases, and both at once

Nothing about the method is specific to increases. Flip the sign of the deviation inside the sum and the same machinery looks for a drop:

increase  (upper CUSUM):   S+t  =  max { 0 ,   S+t−1 + ( xt − μ − C ) }

decrease  (lower CUSUM):   S−t  =  max { 0 ,   S−t−1 + ( μ − xt − C ) } Both start at zero. Both raise the alarm on the same test, St ≥ T. To watch both directions, run the two recursions side by side on the same observations and alarm if either reaches its threshold.

What actually differs between the two equations

Almost nothing — and the parts that look like they might differ do not. Going through them one at a time is the fastest way to stop making sign errors.

PieceIncreaseDecreaseWhy it is that way
The deviation xt − μ μ − xt This is the only real difference. Each version measures distance in the direction that counts as evidence. A reading of 460 against μ = 500 contributes −40 to the increase sum and +40 to the decrease sum.
The allowance C subtracted subtracted C is not a direction, it is a handicap. It always works against accumulation, so it is subtracted in both. Adding it in the decrease version is the single most common error — see below.
The floor at zero max{0, …} max{0, …} Identical, and it means the same thing in both: evidence pointing the wrong way is thrown away rather than banked.
The alarm test S+t ≥ T S−t ≥ T Both statistics are non-negative by construction, so both are compared against a positive threshold. There is no −T anywhere.
What a large value means the process ran high the process ran low S− going up means the process went down. The statistic and the thing it measures move in opposite directions, which is why the notation trips people up.

An equivalent way to write the second one, which some texts prefer because it makes the symmetry obvious:

S−t  =  max { 0 ,   S−t−1 − ( xt − μ ) − C } Same algebra, rearranged. Both forms compute the same number; pick whichever you are less likely to mistype under exam pressure.

Three mistakes that account for most wrong answers

1. Writing μ − xt + C. Adding C makes every period contribute more, so the total climbs on ordinary noise and alarms when nothing has happened. On the sales data below this version fires at week 8 — three weeks before the change exists.

2. Expecting S− to go negative and comparing it against −T. It cannot go negative; the max with zero forbids it. A downward change makes S− climb toward a positive T.

3. Running only the increase recursion on a process that fell. S+ sits at zero forever and reports "no change" while the process quietly collapses. The direction you monitor is a design decision, not something the method works out for you.

Running both sides is not free

Each period now gives the scheme two chances to alarm instead of one, so at the same T a two-sided scheme raises roughly twice as many false alarms as a one-sided one. If the false-alarm rate is what you were tuning, raise T a little when you add the second side. The two sides do not have to be symmetric either — if an increase is dangerous and a decrease is merely interesting, give them different C and T.

Which to run is a decision about consequences, not about statistics:

SituationRun
Bearing temperature, contamination level, server latency, defect rate — only one direction is a problemOne-sided. Monitoring the harmless direction buys nothing but false alarms.
A machined dimension, a chemical concentration, sales, a calibration reading — either direction signals something worth knowingTwo-sided. Both recursions, same data, alarm on either.

One practical detail that is easy to forget: once the alarm fires and the cause has been dealt with, reset the statistic to zero. Otherwise it stays above T forever and alarms every period from then on.

The same equations in industrial notation

Quality-control software, textbooks and published tuning tables all write CUSUM with different letters. It is worth being able to read them, because that is where the ready-made parameter values live.

C+i  =  max { 0 ,   C+i−1 + ( xi − μ0 ) − K }
C−i  =  max { 0 ,   C−i−1 − ( xi − μ0 ) − K }

signal when   C+i > H   or   C−i > H Identical to the two recursions above. μ0 is μ, K is C, H is T. This is the "tabular CUSUM"; the older graphical "V-mask" version is the same decision drawn as a sideways V laid over the plot.
Course notationIndustrial notationUsual name
μμ0target, or in-control mean
CK = k·σreference value, allowance, or slack
TH = h·σdecision interval

The useful part is the σ. Expressing the two dials as multiples of the process standard deviation makes them portable: k and h mean the same thing on a temperature probe and on a share price, so published tables of good settings apply to both. The standard starting points:

  • k = δ/2 — half the shift, measured in standard deviations, that you most want to catch quickly. Aiming at a 1σ shift gives k = 0.5, which is the default in most software. This is not a rule of thumb: k = δ/2 is the value that makes each period's contribution the log-likelihood ratio between "unchanged" and "shifted by δ", which is what makes CUSUM the fastest detector for that particular shift size.
  • h = 4 or 5 — with k = 0.5, h = 4 gives about 168 periods between false alarms and h = 5 about 465. Larger h, rarer false alarms, later detection: the same trade as always, now in numbers you can look up rather than guess.
  • Fast initial response — start the statistic at H/2 instead of 0 if the process might already be out of control when monitoring begins. Detection is much faster at startup, at the cost of some extra early false alarms.

The cost of that precision is narrowness: k is tuned to one shift size, and performance falls away for shifts much larger or smaller than δ. If you have no particular δ in mind, EWMA (in the extra material below) is the more forgiving choice.

The two numbers that price the two mistakes

Average run length (ARL)

The average number of periods a scheme runs before it raises an alarm. Quoted twice: ARL0, measured while the process is healthy, is the average gap between false alarms and should be large. ARL1, measured after a change of a stated size, is the average detection delay and should be small.

This is Lesson 1's trade-off turned into two numbers you can actually compute and compare. It also makes the case for CUSUM concrete. Take a plain Shewhart chart, which alarms whenever a single observation falls outside ±3σ: false alarms arrive every 370 periods on average, which sounds excellent, but a sustained 1σ shift takes about 44 periods to catch because a 1σ shift only pushes about 2% of readings past the limit. A CUSUM with k = 0.5 and h = 4 catches the same 1σ shift in about 8 periods.

SchemeARL0 — periods between false alarmsARL1 at a 1σ shift
Shewhart chart, ±3σ limits≈ 370≈ 44
CUSUM, k = 0.5, h = 4≈ 168≈ 8
CUSUM, k = 0.5, h = 5≈ 465≈ 11

Read the last row against the first: CUSUM at h = 5 raises fewer false alarms than the Shewhart chart and still detects the shift four times faster. Accumulating evidence is not a compromise here, it is a straight improvement — for small shifts. For a large sudden jump the ordering reverses, because a 4σ jump puts the very first observation outside the Shewhart limits while CUSUM still needs a period or two to accumulate. That is the whole reason both charts are still in use, and why some schemes run both.

What the equations quietly assume

CUSUM as written assumes observations are independent and identically distributed around μ when nothing has changed, with a stable spread. Three consequences worth knowing: autocorrelation inflates false alarms badly, because runs of similar values look like accumulating evidence; trend or seasonality must be modelled first, with CUSUM then run on the residuals rather than the raw series; and μ and σ have to be estimated from a clean stretch of data — a "Phase I" period known to be in control. Estimating μ from data that already contains the change is a good way to detect nothing.

Why quote ARL0 and ARL1 rather than a single accuracy number?

Show answer

Because the two mistakes have different costs and no single number can express the trade between them. ARL0 prices crying wolf, ARL1 prices reacting late, and tuning C and T just slides you along the curve between them. Which point on the curve is right comes from the costs, not from the data.

What to take from this lesson

CUSUM is one method among several, and the course does not attempt to cover the rest. What generalises is the underlying idea — accumulate evidence over time rather than judging each observation alone — and the trade-off between early detection and false detection, which every change-detection method has to make somewhere. If you want to know what the other methods are, the additional resources below map them out; none of that is class material.

Things worth noticing

  • This is Module 5’s question at a different time scale. An outlier is one point that doesn’t fit its context; a change is every subsequent point failing to fit the old context. Module 5’s contextual outlier and this module’s level shift differ mainly in how many points are involved.
  • CUSUM is the residual trick with a memory. Module 5 found outliers by fitting a model and looking at where the error was large. CUSUM does the same thing — μ is the model, xt − μ is the residual — except that it accumulates residuals instead of judging each one alone. That is the entire difference, and it is what buys early detection.
  • The two recursions are one recursion. Increase and decrease differ only in the sign of the deviation term — C is subtracted in both, the floor at zero applies to both, both compare against the same positive threshold. Almost every error people make here comes from assuming more differs than actually does, usually by flipping the sign of C as well or expecting the lower statistic to go negative.
  • The reset at zero is the whole idea, compressed. Evidence for an increase is worth keeping; evidence against one is thrown away, because the question is only ever “has it gone up?”. That asymmetry is what makes CUSUM fast, and it is also why C has to exist.
  • Neither lesson tells you how to set anything. C, T and μ are all left to you. Module 3’s validation material is the missing piece, with one twist: “performs well” here has two components — detection delay and false-alarm rate — whose relative weight comes from costs rather than from data.
  • Statistical correctness is not the goal. Lesson 1 rejects hypothesis testing even though it gives the right answer. That is unusual enough to be worth remembering: a method can be correct and still useless, if the deadline is part of the problem.

The full map of methods

Every method here accumulates or compares evidence about whether the data-generating process has moved. They differ in when you need the answer, what kind of change you are looking for, and how much you are willing to assume.

The one distinction that organises everything

Before the taxonomy, the split that decides which half of the literature applies to you — and it is Lesson 1's three families of question, in disguise.

CUSUM sits squarely in the online column, which is exactly why the lectures introduce it right after arguing that the answer has to arrive in time to act.

Family 1 — Sequential monitoring (control charts)

All three of the classical charts monitor a stream and signal when a statistic crosses a limit. What separates them is how much memory the statistic has.

MethodStatisticMemoryBest at
Shewhart chart
Shewhart, 1925/1931		 The observation itself, against μ ± 3σ None — each point judged alone Large sudden shifts, roughly 2σ and up. Fastest possible response to a big jump; nearly blind to small sustained drift.
CUSUM
Page, 1954		 Running total of deviations, discounted by C and floored at zero Full, but reset to zero whenever evidence runs out Small sustained shifts of a size you can name in advance. Optimal for that size; performance drops off either side of it.
EWMA

EWMA:   zt  =  λ xt + ( 1 − λ ) zt−1 ,    z0 = μ

Small λ means a long memory and sensitivity to tiny shifts; large λ approaches a Shewhart chart. λ is EWMA's version of C — the single dial that trades speed against false alarms.

Two more members of this family worth knowing by name:

  • Page–Hinkley test. A CUSUM variant that tracks the gap between the running mean and the current observation and signals when the cumulative deviation exceeds a threshold. Essentially the same idea with the baseline estimated on the fly rather than fixed — which is why it is the one that reappears in machine-learning drift libraries.
  • Shiryaev–Roberts procedure. Sums likelihood ratios over all possible change points instead of taking the maximum, giving a Bayesian-flavoured alternative to CUSUM with comparable performance and sometimes better properties for detecting a change that happened long ago.
  • Combined Shewhart–CUSUM. Run both, alarm on either. Catches large jumps immediately and small drift eventually, at the cost of a somewhat higher false-alarm rate. Widely used where both failure modes are real.

Family 2 — Offline segmentation: finding change points after the fact

When the series is complete and you want to know where the breaks were — possibly several of them — the problem becomes an optimisation: partition the series so that the data within each segment fits a simple model well, without inventing so many breaks that you are just fitting noise. Every method below is a different way of searching that space.

The current default for offline work; its assumption is that breaks grow in number with the length of the series rather than clustering in one stretch.

Bottom-up / window slidingStart over-segmented and merge, or slide a window and score the discrepancy either side of it.Cheap and intuitive; approximate. Window methods are a natural bridge between offline and online.

The search method is not the interesting choice — the cost function is

All of these algorithms take a cost function that says how badly a stretch of data fits a single regime, and that is what decides what kind of change you can find. An L2 cost detects shifts in the mean. A normal cost detects shifts in mean or variance. A linear cost detects changes in a regression relationship between variables. An autoregressive cost detects changes in the dynamics. A kernel or RBF cost detects a change in the whole distribution, without assuming its shape. Choosing PELT over binary segmentation changes how thoroughly you search; choosing the cost changes what you are searching for.

Family 3 — Bayesian change point detection

Bayesian online change point detection (BOCPD, Adams & MacKay 2007) maintains a posterior distribution over the run length — how long it has been since the last change — and updates it with every new observation. Instead of a binary alarm it gives you a probability that a change has just occurred, which is exactly what you want when the decision downstream is itself probabilistic, when the sample is small, or when you need to express uncertainty about where the change was rather than just that there was one. The cost is that you must specify prior beliefs about how often changes happen and what the data looks like within a regime.

Family 4 — Structural break tests in fitted models

Econometrics asks a narrower version of the question: not "did the series change?" but "did the relationship I fitted change?" — did the coefficients of a regression shift partway through the sample. This is the natural tool for Lesson 1's second family, "did the action we took have an effect".

Family 5 — Non-parametric and distribution-change methods

Everything so far assumes you can name the distribution, or at least that the change shows up in the mean. When neither holds — heavy tails, high-dimensional data, a change in shape rather than location — these apply instead.

  • Two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov. Compare the empirical distributions of two windows. Simple, assumption-light, and the basis of the KSWIN streaming detector.
  • Kernel methods / maximum mean discrepancy. Map the data into a high-dimensional space through a kernel and look for a mean shift there, which corresponds to a change in the full distribution. Kernel CUSUM and kernel change-point detection are the sequential and offline versions.
  • Rank and energy statistics. Work on the ordering of the data rather than its values, so they are robust to outliers and need no distributional assumption at all.

Family 6 — Drift detection in machine-learning systems

The version of this problem you are most likely to meet in practice: a deployed model that was accurate at launch and is quietly getting worse. Two things can be moving, and the distinction matters because it changes what you monitor.

These all inherit Lesson 1's trade-off exactly. Error-rate detectors like DDM and EDDM are cheap in memory because they hold a few summary numbers; window-based detectors like ADWIN and KSWIN hold data and cost more, but detect changes the summary methods miss. And all of them are sensitive to their threshold parameters in the same way C and T are.

Family 7 — Model-based monitoring

  • Kalman filter innovations. If you already have a state-space model of the process, its one-step-ahead prediction errors should be white noise with known variance. Running CUSUM or a Shewhart chart on those innovations detects changes in a system that is supposed to be moving — which the raw-value charts cannot handle.
  • Generalised likelihood ratio (GLR). CUSUM needs you to name the post-change parameter. GLR estimates it from the data instead, maximising the likelihood ratio over both the change point and the size of the change. More flexible, considerably more computation, and the standard answer when you cannot specify δ.

Two more that answer a different question entirely

Interrupted time series and difference-in-differences are worth knowing because they are the correct tools for Lesson 1's second family. There you already know when the intervention happened, and the question is whether the change that followed can be attributed to it rather than to whatever else was going on. Detection is trivial; attribution is the hard part. Reaching for CUSUM there answers a question nobody asked.

What do all seven families have in common?

Each one compares the evidence for "same regime" against the evidence for "different regime", and each one needs a threshold to turn that comparison into a decision. Choosing and comparing

Four questions narrow the field to one or two candidates, and two numbers tell you whether the one you picked is tuned properly.

Four questions that pick the method

  1. When do you need the answer? Immediately, as data arrives → a sequential method: Shewhart, CUSUM, EWMA, or a drift detector. After the fact, with everything in hand → offline segmentation or a structural break test. This question alone removes about half the options.
  2. What is changing? The mean is the easy case and what most methods assume. A change in variance needs a normal cost, CUSUM-of-squares, or a chart built for dispersion. A change in the relationship between variables needs a structural break test or a linear cost. A change in distribution shape with the mean unmoved needs a kernel or rank method.
  3. Do you know how big a shift you care about? Yes → CUSUM with k = δ/2, which is optimal for that δ. No → EWMA, which spreads its sensitivity across shift sizes, or GLR, which estimates the shift from the data.
  4. How much can you assume? A known distribution and independent observations allow the parametric methods and their published ARL tables. Autocorrelation, heavy tails or high dimensions push you toward non-parametric and kernel methods, or toward modelling the structure first and monitoring the residuals.

How to tell whether a detector is any good

Lesson 2 leaves C and T to you and points at Module 3's validation material. This is what "performs well" actually means once you get there — and it is never a single accuracy number, because there are two mistakes with different prices.

MetricMeasuresWant
ARL0 — in-control average run lengthAverage number of periods between false alarms on a healthy processLarge. 370 and 500 are conventional targets, chosen to match a Shewhart chart.
ARL1 — out-of-control ARLAverage number of periods from a change of stated size to the alarmSmall. Always quoted with the shift size, since it is meaningless without one.
EDD — expected detection delayThe same idea stated as a delay; the standard metric in the sequential-detection literatureSmall. Usually plotted against log ARL0, which is close to a straight line for CUSUM.
False alarm rateAlarms per period on a healthy process — roughly 1/ARL0Small. The more intuitive form when you are explaining the cost to someone.
Detection rate / F1For offline work: how many true change points were found, against how many were inventedHigh. Needs a tolerance window, since "found at t = 41 when the truth was 40" should usually count as a hit.
Annotation error, Hausdorff distanceFor segmentation: difference in the number of change points, and the worst location errorSmall. The standard pair for comparing segmentations.

Why ARL0 alone is misleading

An average of 370 periods between false alarms does not mean a false alarm arrives on schedule every 370 periods. Run lengths are roughly geometric, so the distribution is heavily skewed: with ARL0 = 370 you will see gaps of 20 periods and gaps of 1,200, and roughly a quarter of the intervals will be shorter than 110. Quote the ARL, but do not promise it as a rhythm.

How to actually pick C and T

Lesson 2's answer is Module 3's answer, and it holds. Concretely:

  1. Get a clean stretch. Find a period where the process was demonstrably in control and estimate μ and σ from it. Everything downstream depends on this being uncontaminated.
  2. Name the shift that matters. The smallest change you would actually act on, in σ units. That is δ, and k = δ/2 follows.
  3. Pick h from what a false alarm costs. Work out how often you can tolerate stopping a healthy process, convert it to a target ARL0, and read h off a table — h = 4 and h = 5 with k = 0.5 give roughly 168 and 465.
  4. Simulate. With μ and σ estimated, generate healthy data, inject a shift of size δ at a known point, and measure the delay and the false-alarm rate you actually get. Repeat over a grid of (k, h). This is the validation step, and it costs almost nothing because the data is synthetic.
  5. Backtest on real history. Run the chosen settings over past data containing known incidents. Count what you would have caught, how late, and how often you would have stopped production for nothing.
  6. Re-estimate periodically. μ and σ drift for legitimate reasons — new equipment, new suppliers, seasonality. A scheme tuned to a baseline that no longer exists is worse than no scheme.

Practice and self-test

Eight worked problems and a mixed self-test. Do the arithmetic by hand at least once — sign errors are invisible until you make one.

Part A — computing CUSUM by hand

Problem 1 — the increase recursion

A process has μ = 20. You are monitoring for an increase with C = 2 and T = 10. The first five observations are 23, 19, 24, 26, 22. Compute St for each period and say whether an alarm fires.

Show worked solution

t = 1: 23 − 20 − 2 = 1; plus S0 = 0 gives 1. S1 = 1.
t = 2: 19 − 20 − 2 = −3; plus 1 gives −2, which is negative, so the floor applies. S2 = 0.
t = 3: 24 − 20 − 2 = 2; plus 0 gives 2. S3 = 2.
t = 4: 26 − 20 − 2 = 4; plus 2 gives 6. S4 = 6.
t = 5: 22 − 20 − 2 = 0; plus 6 gives 6. S5 = 6.

No alarm — the maximum is 6 against T = 10. Notice t = 5: an observation genuinely above μ contributed exactly nothing, because it exceeded expectation by precisely C. That is C doing its job.

Problem 2 — the decrease recursion

Same five observations, same μ = 20. Now compute the decrease statistic with C = 2. What do you get, and what does it tell you?

Show worked solution

Every period: μ − xt is 20 − 23 = −3, 20 − 19 = +1, 20 − 24 = −4, 20 − 26 = −6, 20 − 22 = −2. Subtract C = 2 from each and the largest is −1. The running total never becomes positive, so S− = 0 in every period.

The interpretation is not "the method failed" — it is the correct answer. This process has not gone down. A lower statistic pinned at zero is exactly what a healthy or rising process should produce, and is the same behaviour you saw from S+ in the sales example in Lesson 2.

Problem 3 — a decrease that does fire

A machine's output has μ = 100. Monitoring for a decrease with C = 3 and T = 12, the observations are 98, 94, 101, 92, 95, 90. When does the alarm fire?

Show worked solution

t = 1: 100 − 98 − 3 = −1; floored. S− = 0.
t = 2: 100 − 94 − 3 = 3; plus 0. S− = 3.
t = 3: 100 − 101 − 3 = −4; plus 3 gives −1; floored. S− = 0.
t = 4: 100 − 92 − 3 = 5. S− = 5.
t = 5: 100 − 95 − 3 = 2; plus 5. S− = 7.
t = 6: 100 − 90 − 3 = 7; plus 7 gives 14 ≥ 12. Alarm at t = 6.

Note t = 3. A single reading above μ wiped out the accumulated evidence entirely, and the eventual detection was delayed by it. That is the price of the floor — and it is the right price, because the alternative is banking credit from quiet periods.

Problem 4 — diagnosing a broken implementation

A colleague's decrease detector alarms within about ten periods on every process they point it at, healthy or not. Their code computes s = max(0, s + (mu - x + C)). Explain the behaviour and give the fix.

Show worked solution

C is added rather than subtracted, so every period contributes an extra +C regardless of the data. On a healthy process the deviations μ − xt average zero, leaving a steady upward drift of C per period. The total therefore reaches T after roughly T/C periods no matter what the process is doing — which is why the alarms arrive on a schedule rather than in response to anything.

Fix: s = max(0, s + (mu - x - C)). A quick sanity test that catches this class of bug: feed the detector data generated with no change at all and confirm the statistic stays near zero. If it climbs steadily, a sign is wrong.

Part B — parameters, tuning and interpretation

Problem 5 — converting to industrial notation

A process has σ = 4. You want to catch a shift of 1.5σ as quickly as possible and can tolerate a false alarm roughly every 465 periods. Give k, h, K, H and the resulting recursion.

Show worked solution

k = δ/2 = 0.75, so K = kσ = 0.75 × 4 = 3.
An ARL0 near 465 corresponds to h = 5, so H = hσ = 5 × 4 = 20.
The recursions are S+t = max{0, S+t−1 + (xt − μ − 3)} and S−t = max{0, S−t−1 + (μ − xt − 3)}, alarming at 20.

Caveat worth stating: the ARL0 ≈ 465 figure is for k = 0.5, not 0.75. Changing k moves the ARL, so in practice you would read h off a table for your actual k, or simulate. Quoting a table value for the wrong k is a common slip.

Problem 6 — reading a tuning decision

A hospital monitors post-operative infection rates with CUSUM. The team proposes halving T to catch outbreaks sooner. What do they need to know before agreeing, and what is the alternative?

Show worked solution

They need the cost of a false alarm in this setting — an investigation, staff time, possibly suspending a surgeon's list or closing a ward — against the cost of a week's delay in catching a real outbreak. Halving T will roughly halve the detection delay and increase false alarms substantially; whether that is right is a judgement about consequences, not statistics.

The alternative worth raising: tuning C instead. In Lesson 2's reaction-time example, lowering C bought most of the speed while leaving the false-alarm margin nearly intact. C and T both move you along the same curve, but not at the same rate, and it is worth checking both before changing either.

One more thing to check: whether μ is still right. Infection rates have seasonal and case-mix variation, and a scheme tuned against a stale baseline will look badly calibrated whatever you do to T.

Problem 7 — picking a method

For each scenario, name a suitable method and say why.
(a) A fraud model deployed six months ago; you have labels arriving with a two-week lag.
(b) Twenty years of quarterly GDP; you want to know whether the relationship between GDP and interest rates broke, and when.
(c) A vibration sensor sampled at 1 kHz where failures announce themselves as a change in the shape of the distribution, not its mean.
(d) A filling machine where over-filling wastes product and under-filling breaks a regulation.

Show worked solution

(a) A drift detector on the error stream — DDM or EDDM if you want a cheap error-rate monitor, ADWIN if you can afford to hold a window. The two-week label lag is the real constraint: it sets a floor on detection delay no method can beat, and it is worth also monitoring the input features with KSWIN so data drift is visible before labels arrive.
(b) A structural break test, not a control chart — the question is about a fitted relationship. Bai–Perron if multiple breaks are plausible over twenty years, which they are; Quandt–Andrews if you are confident there is at most one.
(c) A non-parametric method: kernel change-point detection or kernel CUSUM offline, KSWIN online. Mean-based methods are blind to a change that leaves the mean where it was.
(d) Two-sided CUSUM, with asymmetric parameters. Under-filling is a regulatory breach and over-filling is a cost, so the two directions deserve different thresholds — probably a tighter T on the under-fill side.

Problem 8 — why the method failed

A CUSUM on daily website response times alarms two or three times a week, and every investigation finds nothing. σ and μ were estimated from a month of clean data and C = 0.5σ, T = 5σ are standard settings. What is the most likely explanation?

Show worked solution

Autocorrelation and daily seasonality. Response times are not independent from one reading to the next, and they follow a weekday/weekend and time-of-day pattern. CUSUM's whole design assumes independent observations scattered around a fixed μ; runs of similar values are exactly the pattern it is built to treat as evidence, so predictable structure reads as change. Published ARL tables silently assume independence, which is why settings that should give a false alarm every 465 periods give one every two days.

The fix is to model the structure first — a seasonal model, or an ARIMA fit — and run CUSUM on the residuals, which are what should be independent and stationary. This is the same "residual trick" noted in the module summary, applied to fix the assumption rather than to find the change.

Part D — written questions

No multiple choice. Write the answer out before opening it — recognising a correct answer and producing one are different skills, and only the second is tested.

Six to write out

1. State both CUSUM recursions and explain, in one sentence each, what the deviation term, the constant C, the max with zero, and the threshold T are for.

Show a model answer

S+t = max{0, S+t−1 + (xt − μ − C)} and S−t = max{0, S−t−1 + (μ − xt − C)}, both from zero, both alarming at St ≥ T. The deviation measures how far the process ran from expectation in the direction being monitored. C is the amount of that deviation dismissed as noise each period, so ordinary fluctuation cannot accumulate. The max with zero discards evidence pointing the wrong way, so a quiet stretch does not delay the next detection. T is how much accumulated evidence is required before a change is declared.

2. Explain why a statistically correct hypothesis test can be the wrong tool for change detection.

Show a model answer

Because reaching a conventional significance threshold requires enough evidence to be confident, and on noisy data that can take longer than the deadline allows — longer than the bearing survives, or longer than the scrap costs justify. The verdict is right and arrives too late to act on. Change detection deliberately trades confidence for speed, accepting false alarms as the price of an answer in time.

3. Describe what happens to detection delay and false-alarm rate when C rises, and when T rises. Why are these not the same dial?

Show a model answer

Both raise the delay and reduce false alarms, but they act differently. C is subtracted every period, so it suppresses accumulation continuously — it stops noise building up at all, which flattens the statistic during healthy stretches. T only decides how much accumulated evidence is required at the end, and does nothing to stop the total climbing beforehand. That is why, in the reaction-time example, raising C from 0 to 15 kept nearly all the detection speed while cutting the pre-change peak from 119 to 23; raising T instead would have cost several periods of delay to achieve the same margin.

4. Why does the statistic reset to zero rather than being allowed to go negative?

Show a model answer

Because the question is only ever "has it moved in this direction?". A run of lower-than-expected readings is no evidence against a later increase, and letting the total bank negative credit would mean the method had to climb out of a hole before it could report one — delaying every subsequent detection by however long the quiet stretch lasted. The floor makes each new stretch of data start from a clean slate, which is what makes CUSUM fast.

5. A colleague says CUSUM is strictly better than a Shewhart chart. Respond.

Show a model answer

Only for small sustained shifts. CUSUM with k = 0.5, h = 5 detects a 1σ shift about four times faster than a ±3σ Shewhart chart while raising fewer false alarms, so for that regime it genuinely dominates. But a 4σ jump puts the very next observation outside the Shewhart limits, while CUSUM still needs a period or two to accumulate — so for large sudden changes Shewhart wins. The crossover sits somewhere around 1.5σ, which is why combined Shewhart–CUSUM schemes exist.

6. Give three assumptions CUSUM makes and say what goes wrong when each is violated.

Show a model answer

Independence: autocorrelated data contains runs of similar values, which look exactly like accumulating evidence, so false alarms rise far above the rate the tables promise. Stationarity around a fixed μ: trend or seasonality produces long predictable runs above expectation and the method alarms on every seasonal peak; fit the structure first and monitor the residuals. μ and σ estimated from clean data: if the baseline period already contains the change, μ is pulled toward the post-change level and the method may never detect anything.

The following are additions rather than lecture content: the eighteen-trial reaction-time dataset in Lesson 2 and every number computed from it, including the whole C = 15 comparison and the control chart; the eighteen-week sales dataset and its two-sided CUSUM table, chart and C-versus-sign-error comparison; the ideal-versus-real sensor figure; all cross-references to Modules 3 and 5; and the whole of “Things worth noticing”. All quiz feedback, margin questions and exam targets are written here rather than drawn from the recordings.

Everything under “Additional resources” is outside material. Extras 1 to 4 — the map of methods, the industrial k and h notation, ARL figures, the Shewhart/CUSUM/EWMA comparison, the practice problems and the reading list — were assembled from published sources, principally the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook, Montgomery’s Introduction to Statistical Quality Control, the original method papers cited in Extra 4, and the documentation of the ruptures and river libraries. None of it was taught in this module. Quantitative claims (ARL values, parameter conventions) are standard in those sources but assume independent, normally distributed observations; check them against a primary source before relying on them, and treat the lectures as authoritative wherever the two differ.
