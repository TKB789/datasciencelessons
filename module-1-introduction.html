Lesson 1

What analytics answers

Analytics answers three kinds of question — what happened, what will happen, and what to do about it — and modeling is the act of turning a situation into math and the math back into a recommendation.

The three parts of analytics modeling

Across almost any applied project, the critical work breaks into three pieces:

  1. Selecting and specifying the right model or models to combine into a solution.
  2. Compiling, building and/or forecasting the necessary data sets.
  3. Interpreting the model’s output to make suggestions that match the organization’s needs, priorities and structure.

Notice that only the middle third is what most people picture as “doing analytics.” The first and last thirds are judgement about the organization, and the whole subject is shaped around that.

Frame for the term

Which of the three parts of analytics modeling does an algorithm actually do for you?

Show answer

Only part of the middle one. Choosing the model and interpreting its output for a specific organization are both yours — which is why Module 4 later calls this “the art of analytics.”

Given an unfamiliar business question, how do you decide which of the three it is?

Show answer

Ask what the answer would be. An explanation of something past → descriptive. A value for something not yet observed → predictive. A course of action, usually with an objective to optimize → prescriptive.

What “modeling” means here

Modeling, in analytics

Taking a real-life situation and expressing the key parts of that situation in terms of math — so we can analyze the math, and then turn the mathematical analysis back into a real-life answer, solution or recommendation.

Read that as a round trip with three legs: situation → math, then analysis, then math → recommendation. Most teaching covers the middle leg, but the two translations at either end are where analytics work actually succeeds or fails. The word “key” is doing real work too — a model expresses the key parts, not all of them.

Lesson 2

How the topics fit together

Two parallel streams — the models themselves, and the concepts that cut across all of them — followed by the harder work of integrating both into one recommendation.

The map

The models stream is a run of mathematical approaches to solving analytics problems — machine learning, regression, optimization and many others, each paired with situations where it is valuable.

The cross-cutting stream holds concepts that matter for using and understanding many different models: preparing data, measuring the quality of a model’s output, imputing missing data. Each arrives the first time it’s needed, and then applies to every model you learn afterwards.

Integration is the last stage and the hardest: combining data decisions with modeling decisions, and combining several models with each other, to deliver one complete recommendation.

Orientation

Which stream does validation belong to, and why does that matter?

Show answer

Cross-cutting. It isn’t a model — it’s a way of judging any model, so it arrives once and then applies to everything afterwards.

The depth tradeoff

What an introduction deliberately does not do

It gives enough mathematical intuition to understand what’s going on and what each model’s benefits and drawbacks are — without going far down into the theory. And it gives a broad toolbox rather than every advanced variation of one method: you see the most commonly used version of each model. Depth on any single method is what specialist study is for.

What is a model?

“Model” means at least three different things in analytics, often within a single paragraph — so learn to read which one is meant from the level of detail on the table.

Three zoom levels, one word

Why this matters later

Module 3 says “pick the best of ten models, then retrain the model on all the data.” How many senses of the word are in that sentence?

Show answer

Two. The ten you compare are specifications; the thing you retrain is a specification that then produces a fitted equation. Reading it as one sense is exactly how people end up thinking they should deploy one of the fold models.

How the module fits together

The through-line across the three lessons

  • Lesson 1Sets the scope: three question types, and modeling as a round trip from situation to math and back.
  • Lesson 2Maps the route: models and cross-cutting concepts in parallel, then integration.
  • Lesson 3Clears one piece of vocabulary that would otherwise confuse every later topic.

Three things worth noticing

  • The two ends of the round trip are the hard parts. Situation → math and math → recommendation are where the value of an analytics professional sits. The middle leg is what a computer does. The same argument returns in Module 4 as “the art of analytics.”
  • The streams explain the ordering. Once you know Module 3 is cross-cutting and Module 4 is models, the apparent zig-zag between topics stops looking random — cross-cutting concepts land the first time they’re needed.
  • The three senses of “model” resolve a real later confusion. “Which model do we deploy after cross-validation?” only sounds paradoxical if you collapse the specification and the fitted equation into one word.

Likely exam targets

  • Classify a business question as descriptive, predictive or prescriptive, with a reason.
  • Give the definition of modeling and name the three legs of the round trip.
  • Name the three parts of analytics modeling from Lesson 1.
  • Describe the two streams and give examples of each.
  • Give the three senses of the word “model” with an example of each.
