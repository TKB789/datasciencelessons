Lesson 1

What analytics answers

Analytics answers three kinds of question — what happened, what will happen, and what to do about it — and modeling is the act of turning a situation into math and the math back into a recommendation.

The three parts of analytics modeling

Across almost any applied project, the critical work breaks into three pieces:

  1. Selecting and specifying the right model or models to combine into a solution.
  2. Compiling, building and/or forecasting the necessary data sets.
  3. Interpreting the model’s output to make suggestions that match the organization’s needs, priorities and structure.

Notice that only the middle third is what most people picture as “doing analytics.” The first and last thirds are judgement about the organization, and the whole subject is shaped around that.

Frame for the term

Which of the three parts does an algorithm actually do for you?

Show answer

Only part of the middle one. Choosing the model and interpreting its output for a specific organization are both yours — which is why Module 4 later calls this “the art of analytics.”

Two failure modes to name:

Exam target

Show answer

Ask what the answer would be. An explanation of something past → descriptive. A value for something not yet observed → predictive. A course of action, usually with an objective to optimize → prescriptive.

What “modeling” means here

Modeling, in analytics

Taking a real-life situation and expressing the key parts of that situation in terms of math — so we can analyze the math, and then turn the mathematical analysis back into a real-life answer, solution or recommendation.

Read that as a round trip with three legs: situation → math, then analysis, then math → recommendation. Most teaching covers the middle leg, but the two translations at either end are where analytics work actually succeeds or fails. The word “key” is doing real work too — a model expresses the key parts, not all of them.

Lesson 2

The map of methods

A survey course gives you a wide, shallow toolbox on purpose — enough of each method to recognise when it fits, not enough to derive it. Knowing the shape of the toolbox is the point.

About this lesson

The source lectures cover course logistics here rather than analytics content. That material has been left out, and this lesson is orientation written for these notes instead — a survey of the field so the later modules have somewhere to sit. It is not lecture content, and your syllabus is the authority on what is actually examinable.

What a survey course is

A survey course trades depth for breadth deliberately. You get enough mathematical intuition about each method to understand what it does, what it assumes, and where it breaks — but not the full theory, and not every advanced variant. You will see the most commonly used version of each model and then move on.

That sounds like a limitation. It is actually the skill being taught: most real analytics failures are choosing the wrong method, not implementing the right one badly. Recognising that a question is prescriptive and needs optimization rather than a classifier is worth more than knowing one algorithm perfectly.

What breadth buys you

The ability to look at an unfamiliar problem and say: this is a classification question, the data is unlabelled so it might be clustering first, the costs are asymmetric so accuracy is the wrong metric, and the attributes are on wildly different scales so it needs standardizing. None of that requires deep theory. All of it requires knowing the map.

Set expectations

If you finish a course like this and don’t feel expert in any single method, has something gone wrong?

Show answer

No — that’s the intended outcome. Depth in one method is what specialist study is for. What you should have is the judgement to pick a method and the vocabulary to go and learn it properly.

Someone asks: “after ten-fold cross-validation, which of the ten models do we put into production?” Explain why the question is malformed and what the right answer is.

Your answerclick to type; saves automatically
Show a strong answer

The question collapses level 2 and level 3. Cross-validation is a procedure for estimating how well a specification performs; the fitted equations produced on each fold are by-products, each trained on a fraction of the data.

So the answer is: none of the ten. You use the fold results to choose a specification, then refit that specification on all the available training data, and the resulting fitted equation is what goes into production. It will have different coefficients from all ten fold models, and that is expected, not a problem.

About this lesson

These are the instructor’s own summaries from the discussion forums, added here in full. They anticipate Module 2 material — support vector machines, k-nearest neighbours, scaling — but the reasoning is judgement rather than mechanics, which is why it belongs alongside the foundations.

Why a wider margin is worth having

Take the two settings separately, because the argument is not the same in each.

Hard classification

A wider margin gives more surety that each point is classified correctly. It also buys tolerance for data that is not quite right: if someone reports income as $50,000 when it is really $49,872, a wider margin makes it more likely that the point still lands on the side it belongs on.

Soft classification

A wider margin — even one that induces more errors on the data you are fitting to — can give a model that is more certain about how to classify new points, and less influenced by the specific location of points that may, through an incomplete model or randomness in the data, be sitting in the “wrong” place. Against that: those specific points might carry important information, and putting less weight on them can hurt the precision of the model in close cases.

Notice that the two arguments are not the same one. In the hard case the gain is robustness to measurement error in the inputs. In the soft case the gain is robustness to the particular sample you happened to draw, and it is explicitly paid for with training errors. Neither argument says a wider margin is always better; the second says outright that it can cost you precision where it matters most.

The core trade

What exactly are you giving up when you widen the margin in a soft-classification setting?

Show answer

Fit to the data in front of you — more points end up misclassified during training — and with it, whatever real information those points carried. If they were genuine signal about how close cases behave, precision on close cases falls.

Scaling versus standardization

Both put attributes on a comparable footing. Which one suits depends mostly on whether the data has natural bounds.

  • Scaling is often more appropriate when there are bounds on the data — age, SAT score, a success probability, RGB values — and especially when there is data close to or at those bounds.
  • Standardization is often more appropriate for data that is not as bounded — distance, income — and that is a reasonable fit for a normal distribution. If the data fits a different distribution, standardizing to that distribution instead is also an option.
  • Combination cases exist. Data might sit mostly in a nice bounded distribution with a few cases far away — income is a good example — where it can be best to remove the outliers and then scale the rest.
  • Or mix the two: instead of standardizing the values, take a reasonable range of z-scores, say −3 to +3 standard deviations, and scale that window from “−3 to 3” onto “0 to 1”.
Exam target

Given an unfamiliar attribute, how do you choose?

Show answer

Ask whether meaningful bounds exist and whether data sits near them — if so, scale. Ask whether it is unbounded and roughly normal — if so, standardize. If it is bounded-ish with a long tail, consider handling the outliers first, or clipping to a z-score window and scaling that.

KNN or SVM? What actually decides it

Six considerations, none of which is about which algorithm is “better”.

ConsiderationWhat it implies
More than two classesKNN is more flexible. SVM methods for multiple classes exist but are not commonly used — for example, for each colour k, run a model separating “colour k” from “not colour k”.
Pockets of one class inside anotherKNN handles meaningful pockets of one colour surrounded by the other; a single separator cannot.
Computation at prediction timeSVM is easier to compute with. Every new point KNN classifies requires finding the k nearest training points. For a model answering card-transaction queries in real time, that cost matters. Heuristics help, but are beyond this course.
Balance in the datasetWith many more “yes” points than “no”, KNN leans toward predicting “yes”, and more so as k grows. You have to even the data out first.
Probability estimatesKNN gives one for free: neighbours in a class divided by k. SVM is harder — probability might be a function of the distance from the classifier to the point.
Being deliberately conservativeBoth can do it. SVM shifts the separator; KNN shifts the threshold.
Fixing an unbalanced dataset

Two basic approaches. Scale up the rare class — duplicate each point some number of times, or create new points of that colour that are similar to the ones in the dataset. Or scale down the common class — randomly take a small sample of the full set, run your model, and repeat for many different small samples. None of these approaches, or others like them, are perfect and all have potential problems; but an unbalanced dataset is sometimes reality and we have to do the best we can.

KNN probability, and its limits

With k = 7 and five “yes” neighbours to two “no”, you can quote 5/7. The estimate is not so reliable, especially at the extremes: a point whose seven nearest neighbours are all one colour is probably not really 100% likely to be that colour. Neighbours can also be weighted by how close they are.

Conservative classification with KNN

Change the threshold rather than the method. With k = 7, instead of calling anything with 4 or more red neighbours red, you might call anything with 2 or more red neighbours red — and require 6 or 7 out of 7 before classifying a point as blue. That is the KNN equivalent of the more-conservative SVM solutions from the lessons.

One more, and it is a meta-point rather than a method choice: if the points are very mixed, no model will save you. If the predictors do a bad job of separating the classes even with nonlinear-looking separators, so that everything is thoroughly intermingled, that is a sign to go back and question the underlying assumption that these particular features are good predictors of the classification at all.

Diagnosis

Your classes are hopelessly mixed whatever separator you try. What is the correct conclusion?

Show answer

Not that you need a fancier model. It is evidence against the meta-model — the assumption that these features predict this classification. Go back and look for better predictors.

Why units silently weight your distances

Each feature — predictor, variable, factor, whichever word your text uses — might naturally sit on a different numeric scale, and those scales are essentially weighting the distances.

The worked example

Suppose you use “distance from warehouse to customer” and “distance from factory to warehouse” as factors in estimating transportation cost. If one factor is measured in millimetres and the other in kilometres, the first will have numbers roughly 1,000,000 times as large. Differences in that factor will therefore look bigger, and a KNN-type algorithm will weight that factor far more heavily when deciding what counts as “nearest”.

The unsettling part is that nothing about the real world changed. The same two quantities, recorded in different units, produce a different notion of “nearest” and therefore a different model. This is why scaling is a cross-cutting concept rather than a detail: it decides what your distance metric actually means.

The defensible answer is SVM, but only if it engages with what is being given up.

The training figure is the wrong evidence. Inducing more errors on the data you are fitting to is the expected consequence of widening the margin, not a symptom of failure — the whole argument is that you accept those errors in exchange for a model less influenced by points that may be in the wrong place through randomness or an incomplete model.

The right evidence is performance on data used for neither fitting nor choosing. If that improves, the wider margin was correct. If it falls, the colleague may be right for a different reason than the one they gave: those points may have carried genuine information about close cases, and down-weighting them cost precision exactly where it mattered.

Your KNN model on an imbalanced dataset predicts the majority class almost always. Walk through your options and the specific risk each one carries.

Your answerclick to type; saves automatically
Show a strong answer
  • Duplicate the rare points exactly. Simple, but every duplicate is the same point — you have not added information, and a single unrepresentative rare point now has several votes.
  • Create similar new rare points. Better coverage, but you are inventing data, and any structure your invention assumes gets baked into the model as if it were observed.
  • Subsample the common class repeatedly. Keeps all real data honest but throws away information in each run, so results vary between samples — which is why you repeat across many samples.
  • Shift the threshold instead. With k = 7, require far fewer rare-class neighbours to call the rare class. Cheap and reversible, but it does not fix the underlying neighbourhood composition.

The correct framing to close on: none of these is perfect and all have potential problems. An unbalanced dataset is sometimes just reality.

Explain to a non-technical manager why recording one column in millimetres instead of metres changed which customers the model flags. Then say what you would do about it.

Your answerclick to type; saves automatically
Show a strong answer

The explanation should avoid jargon and land the mechanism: the model decides who is “similar” by adding up differences across columns. A column recorded in millimetres produces numbers a million times larger than the same quantity in kilometres, so its differences swamp everything else in that sum. The model is not weighting that factor because it matters — it is weighting it because of the unit somebody chose.

The fix is to put every attribute on a comparable footing before computing distances: scale the bounded ones onto a common range, standardize the unbounded roughly-normal ones, and handle the awkward ones with a combination approach. Worth adding that the choice of scaling technique is itself a modelling decision, not a formatting step.

Four things worth noticing

  • Classify the question before reaching for a method. Descriptive, predictive or prescriptive narrows the toolbox immediately. Starting from the method you happen to know is how people end up forcing a classifier onto an optimization problem.
  • The two ends of the round trip are the hard parts. Situation → math and math → recommendation are where the value of an analytics professional sits. The middle leg is what a computer does. The same argument returns in Module 4 as “the art of analytics.”
  • Cross-cutting beats method-specific. Validation, scaling and variable selection get used against every model you ever build. That’s where the compounding return is.
  • The three senses of “model” resolve a real later confusion. “Which model do we deploy after cross-validation?” only sounds paradoxical if you collapse the specification and the fitted equation into one word.
  • Method choices are contingent, not ranked. Margin width, scaling technique and KNN-versus-SVM all resolve differently depending on bounds, balance, measurement error and how many predictions per second you owe someone. Anyone who answers these without asking about the data is guessing.
  • Units are a weighting scheme. Recording one factor in millimetres and another in kilometres changes what counts as “nearest”, and therefore changes the model — without anything in the real world having changed.

A hospital wants to know which patients to prioritise for a follow-up call after discharge. Take the problem from question type through to a specific method, naming every cross-cutting decision you would have to make and why.

Your answerclick to type; saves automatically
Show a strong answer

Type. Ambiguous, and saying so earns credit. “Who is likely to be readmitted?” is predictive; “whom should we call, given ten nurse-hours a week?” is prescriptive and needs the prediction as an input. Settle this before touching data.

Family. Classification for the predictive half, since past readmissions give labels and the response is a category.

Cross-cutting decisions. Class imbalance — readmission is rare, so accuracy is the wrong metric and a KNN would lean to “no”; scaling — age is bounded, income and distance-from-hospital are not, so they need different treatment; missing data — comorbidity fields are patchy; validation — records are time-ordered as protocols change, so a random split leaks the future; asymmetric costs — missing a readmission is worse than an unnecessary call, so the threshold should be deliberately conservative.

Method. Either is defensible with reasons. KNN gives a natural probability for ranking the call list and handles pockets, but needs rebalancing. SVM is cheap at prediction time, though scoring is only weekly so that hardly matters, and probabilities are harder to extract.

Closing. The output is a ranked call list with a stated threshold, not a yes/no label — that is the math → recommendation leg.

discussionexam-style long answer

Write the strongest possible case against automatically preferring wider margins, then say under what conditions you would still widen.

Your answerclick to type; saves automatically
Show a strong answer

Against. Widening deliberately down-weights the points nearest the boundary — and in many applications those are the most informative records you have, because they describe exactly the close cases the model exists to adjudicate. If the data is accurately measured and the sample is large and representative, a point near the boundary is probably telling you something true about where the boundary is. Pushing it aside trades real information for a comfort that the sample does not require.

Still widen when: inputs are known to carry measurement or rounding error, as with self-reported income; the sample is small enough that the boundary points may be accidents of sampling; the model is acknowledged to be incomplete, so points in the “wrong” place are expected; or the deployment conditions differ enough from training that robustness beats precision.
