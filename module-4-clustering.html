Lesson 1

Introduction to clustering

Clustering groups points that are similar — and it finds groups you didn’t know were there, not just the ones you went looking for.

The definition

Clustering

Taking a set of data points and dividing them into groups so that each group contains points that are close to each other, or similar.

Learning typeUnsupervised — nothing is being predicted (formalised in Lesson 6)
GoalPoints inside a cluster are near each other
OutputGroup assignments, not a numeric prediction
Grouping basisDistance between data points

Clustering has two payoffs, not one. It segments data into groups you expected, and it can also reveal groups you didn’t know were in the dataset.

Check 1

Why is clustering called unsupervised learning, when regression is not?

Show answer

There is no response variable being predicted and no correct answer to train against. The algorithm only sees the attributes and groups points by how close they are to each other.

Where it gets used

Targeted marketing. Segment potential customers and give each group a different message, because different people respond to different message types. For a consumer durable — say a family car — the four angles might be capacity, price, versatility and status. Data produces the clusters; marketing experts then decide what material each cluster sees.

The discovery payoff shows up here: inspecting a cluster might reveal a segment you never planned for, such as buyers who care about gas mileage.

Personalized medicine. An ideal cluster is a set of people who would react similarly to a treatment, while different clusters react differently.

Facility location. Here closeness is literal physical distance: cluster people by where they live, then put one facility in each cluster. Civic examples are police stations, fire stations, medical clinics and libraries; the same approach works for gas stations and fast food restaurants.

Image analysis. Re-coloring pictures, recognizing faces and other objects. This is why handwritten CAPTCHAs keep getting more ambiguous — clustering algorithms keep getting better at reading them.

Initial data investigation. Find obvious clusters, then analyze each one separately in case they have different properties. Predicting how much a buyer will spend might need a different model per cluster.

Lesson 2

Distance and norms

Euclidean, Manhattan and infinity distance are one formula with three values of p — and the right p comes from how the real process works.

The same two points, three answers

Take X = (0, 0) and Y = (3, 4). Every metric below measures the same gap and gets a different number:

d = √( (x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)² )Straight-line, or Euclidean, distance.
d = |x₁ − y₁| + |x₂ − y₂|Rectilinear distance. Used for driving on a city street grid, which is why it is also called Manhattan distance.
Check 4

Why can the rectilinear distance never be smaller than the Euclidean distance?

Show answer

A straight line is the shortest path, so any route made of axis-aligned legs can only be longer or equal. They are equal only when the points already line up on one axis.

Generalizing: the p-norm

d = ( Σⁿᵢ₌₁ |xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵖ )1/pThe p-norm distance, also called the Minkowski distance of order p. Written for n dimensions.

Any value of p is available in theory — 3, 10, 1000. In practice the third most common choice is p = ∞.

Why the infinity norm is just “the biggest”

  1. Raise every coordinate difference to the infinity power.
  2. The largest term dominates completely. 8∞ swamps 7∞, and their sum is so close to 8∞ that the second term is irrelevant.
  3. So the sum is essentially (largest difference)∞.
  4. You then take the ∞th root of an ∞ power. Root and power cancel.
  5. What’s left is the largest difference.
Infinity norm

The largest of a set of absolute differences. Nothing more. The treatment here is deliberately informal with the infinities here because the intuition comes across better; a formal treatment would use the limit as p goes to infinity.

Check 5

Two points differ by 2 in one dimension and 9 in another. Give the 1-, 2- and ∞-norm distances.

Show answer

1-norm: 11. 2-norm: √85 ≈ 9.22. ∞-norm: 9.

Note the ordering ∞ ≤ 2 ≤ 1. The larger p gets, the more the single biggest gap dominates.

When you’d actually want it: the warehouse

A warehouse has an automated storage and retrieval system. In each aisle a machine moves horizontally along the aisle, and also stretches vertically to reach the right height. If it moves, stops, then stretches, the total is the sum of the two times — a 1-norm. If it stretches while it moves, the total is whichever takes longer — an ∞-norm.

Lesson 3

The k-means algorithm

Alternate two cheap steps — reassign points, recompute centers — until nothing moves. It skips an optimization problem that would be far too slow to solve exactly.

Setup and notation

The running example is car buyers over the past eight years, with age horizontal and average daily temperature where they live vertical. With n data points and m attributes:

xijValue of data point i, attribute j — the data itself
yik1 if data point i is in cluster k, 0 if not — cluster membership
zkjThe jth dimension coordinate of cluster center k
minimize   Σᵢ Σₖ   yik · d( xi , zk )

subject to   Σₖ yik = 1   for every point i The first part adds up distances from points to centers, but only where the point is in that cluster — the job the 0/1 y term does. The constraint forces every point into exactly one cluster.

This is a hard optimization problem, covered properly in the optimization topic later in the course. So instead of solving it, we use k-means.

Check 7

Which of x, y, z is given to you, and which two are you solving for?

Show answer

x is observed and fixed. Both y (membership) and z (center locations) are unknowns — and they depend on each other, which is why k-means alternates between them.

The algorithm as a loop

put in ordersolved

Drag the steps of k-means into sequence — or use the arrows if you prefer the keyboard.

  1. Decide how many clusters you want
  2. Pick k points inside the range of the data as starting centers
  3. Assign each data point to the center it is closest to
  4. Replace each center with the centroid of the points assigned to it
  5. Repeat the assign-and-recompute pair until no point changes cluster
  6. Report the final clusters

Check 8

Why is “no point changed clusters” a safe place to stop?

Show answer

Centers are computed purely from membership. If membership didn’t change, the centroids are identical to last round, so the next assignment reproduces the same result. The algorithm is at a fixed point.

Jargon break

Heuristic

An algorithm not guaranteed to find the absolute best solution, but which in many cases — including this one — usually gets very close and gets there quickly.

Expectation-maximization (EM)

Calculating cluster centers takes the mean of the points in a cluster, similar to an expectation. Reassigning points strictly minimizes distance, but you can view it as maximizing the negative of the distance. k-means alternates between the two.

Lesson 4

k-means in practice

Outliers, restarts and the choice of k each get a quantitative default and a qualitative override — and the qualitative one comes first every time.

Outliers

An outlier here means a point that isn’t close to any cluster center. k-means has no concept of “not in a cluster” — it assigns the outlier to whichever center is nearest, even though the point may not belong there at all.

The default move: remove the point and rerun, so it doesn’t drag its cluster center artificially to one side. The lecturer’s position: that’s not always right. Better to ask what the outlier is, what makes its attributes so different, and what the implication is of putting a point like this in the nearest cluster.

The soapbox — it recurs all course

Answering those questions takes time and effort, and it is part of what separates a top-notch analytics professional from someone who blindly runs algorithms. In many situations the algorithm is really just a guide. As much as this gets called data science, the best professionals also practise the art of analytics: looking at the combination of the data, the algorithm and the real situation to find the right answer.

Check 10

Why does an outlier distort a k-means solution rather than sitting harmlessly in the nearest cluster?

Show answer

The center is the centroid — the mean of its members. A far-off point pulls that mean toward itself, and the shifted center can then change which cluster other borderline points get assigned to.

Restarts, and choosing k

k-means is fast, and that speed is a resource. Run it several times from different initial centers and keep the best result. Run it across a range of different k rather than guessing. But there’s a trap:

Why total distance alone can’t choose k

Start with a k-cluster solution and add one more cluster — even one holding a single data point — and the total distance to centers gets smaller. So the clustering always appears better with more clusters. At the extreme, the best-looking solution is one cluster per data point.

Qualitative answer. The best solution is the one that fits the situation. If your city only has budget for four new fire stations, a solution giving six new areas isn’t much help.

Quantitative answer — the elbow diagram.

Check 11

A classmate picks k by finding the value with the lowest total distance to cluster centers. What goes wrong?

Show answer

Total distance falls monotonically as k rises, so that rule always picks the largest k tested — in the limit, one cluster per point.

The elbow looks at where the rate of improvement drops off. And a practical constraint can override it entirely.

Lesson 5

Clustering for prediction

Send a new point to the nearest center. Do that for every possible location and the whole space divides into regions — a Voronoi diagram, an idea centuries older than analytics.

Predicting the cluster of a new point

Take a finished solution — five clusters, triangles marking the centers. If a new point lands inside a cluster, the answer is easy. If it doesn’t land inside any cluster, choose whichever center is closest; that’s as reasonable a choice as any. Do this for every possible location and you get regions.

Voronoi diagram

The partition of a space into one region per center, where each region holds every location nearer that center than any other. Named for a mathematician who lived more than 100 years ago.

More than 150 years agoA diagram like it was used to analyze the source of a cholera outbreak in London
Early-to-mid 1600sRené Descartes used something like it in his work

The point: not all the good ideas in analytics are new and cutting-edge. Sometimes the best ideas have been around a long time and just need dusting off.

Check 12

Assigning a new point to the nearest cluster center looks a lot like KNN. What is different?

Show answer

KNN compares the new point to actual labelled training points and votes among the k nearest. Here you compare to k computed centers, and there are no labels anywhere — the groups were invented by the algorithm.

The k also means different things: number of clusters vs. number of neighbours consulted.

Lesson 6

Clustering vs. classification

Both split points into groups. The only difference is whether you already knew the right answer going in — and that difference has a name.

The difference is what we know about the data points

Not the shape of the output. Both produce groups. What differs is whether you already had the right answer going in.

ClassificationClustering
Attributes known?YesYes
Correct group known?Yes — response already observedNo
Model usesAttributes and responsesAttributes only
JargonSupervised learningUnsupervised learning

The loan example. We already know each point’s classification: blue repaid in full, red did not. That known response helps us classify a new applicant. In clustering no such labels exist.

Check 13

Define supervised and unsupervised learning.

Show answer

Supervised: we have observed the right answer for each data point, and use that in addition to attributes. Classification is the example.

Unsupervised: we have not observed the response, and use only attribute information. Clustering is the example.

How the module fits together

The through-line across all six lessons

  • Lesson 1Defines clustering as grouping points that are close — and leaves “close” undefined.
  • Lesson 2Supplies the definition. Closeness is a p-norm, and which p you pick should follow from how the real process behaves.
  • Lesson 3Puts it to work. Every time k-means asks “which center is this closest to,” it calls a metric from Lesson 2.
  • Lesson 4Handles what the clean version ignored: outliers, unlucky starting points, and how many clusters to ask for.
  • Lesson 5Extends the finished solution to new data, and carves the whole space into nearest-center regions.
  • Lesson 6Names what has been true all along — no responses were ever observed, so this was unsupervised learning.

Four things worth noticing

  • The axes change between lessons. Lesson 1 plots age against miles driven per year; Lesson 3 plots age against average daily temperature. Same buyer story, different attributes.
  • The qualitative answer comes first, twice. On outliers and on choosing k, the material leads with “understand the situation” and only then gives the technique.
  • Speed is the resource being spent. Restarts and the elbow diagram are both purchased with the same property: k-means is fast enough to run many times.
  • k-means pairs naturally with the 2-norm. The centroid minimizes total squared Euclidean distance, which is one reason variants like k-medians exist.
