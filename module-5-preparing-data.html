Lesson 1

Preparing data

Three things can quietly wreck a model before it is even fitted — mismatched scales, a handful of extreme points, and columns carrying no information — and all three are fixable in advance.

What this lesson is, and isn’t

The source lecture for this lesson is a short preview: it names the three problems, says each can be dealt with before modelling, and hands off to the lessons that follow. It contains no procedures. Everything below that goes past naming the three problems — the worked scale comparison, the pipeline diagram, and the pointers to where each problem is actually solved — is orientation material written for these notes, not lecture content. The margin questions and both activities in this lesson are likewise written here rather than drawn from the recording.

The gap between how we talk and what we do

Throughout this course, data is described as though it went straight into the model: a predictor in a regression, a factor in a classification model. Credit score. Average daily temperature. Units sold at each event. The value of a share at five-second intervals. The concentration of a chemical in drinking water.

The notation encourages this. Writing xij for attribute j of data point i makes it sound as though xij is simply the number that was measured — the exact count of units sold, the exact share price.

Data preparation

Everything done to a dataset between collecting it and handing it to a model. When a course says “assume the data is given”, this is the work it is assuming you already did.

Three problems make that assumption unsafe.

Orientation

Which of the three problems is actually taught inside this module?

Show answer

Only the second. Scaling belongs to Module 2, Lesson 7; variable selection and dimensionality reduction are named but not covered anywhere in these recordings. Lessons 2 and 3 here handle extreme points. Module 6 then changes the question entirely, from one odd point to a shift in the whole process.

Why scale is a problem and not just an inconvenience

Put average household income and credit score side by side. Income runs into the hundreds of thousands; a credit score runs to a few hundred. Nothing about that is wrong — the two quantities simply measure different things in different units.

The trouble is that many algorithms add up contributions from every attribute, and an attribute measured in larger numbers contributes larger numbers. The optimizer then spends its effort where the numbers are big, which is a fact about the units rather than about the world. That is what “leading our algorithms down the wrong path” means concretely.

A cross-reference, not a repeat

Module 2, Lesson 7 works this through with an SVM: coefficients of 5 and 700 on two attributes, and a 1% nudge to the larger one moving the objective as much as a 600% change to the smaller. Rather than restate it here, treat that lesson as the answer to the first box in the diagram above.

Lesson 2

Finding outliers

An outlier is a point that doesn’t fit its context — which is why there are several kinds of them, and no single test that finds them all.

The definition, and immediately a complication

Outlier

A data point that is very different from the rest of the dataset.

The obvious case is a single value far from everything else. That is what most people picture when they hear the word, and it has a name: a point outlier.

But suppose that alongside one far-flung value there is also a small huddle of two or three points, well away from the mass of the data though not as extreme as the single one. Are those outliers too? There are only a few of them, and they are clearly different from the overwhelming majority — which is the definition. Yet they might have a perfectly good reason for being there.

Why the answer is “it depends”, and more so here than usual

Outliers carry philosophical questions as well as statistical ones. Deciding that a point “doesn’t belong” is a claim about what the dataset is supposed to represent, and no amount of arithmetic settles that. Lesson 3 is entirely about this problem; the rest of this lesson only covers spotting candidates.

Two further kinds show up once the data has a time attribute.

Vocabulary

Why is a contextual outlier called contextual?

Show answer

Because the surrounding points supply the context that makes it anomalous. Its own value is unremarkable; only its position relative to the rest of the series — the time at which it occurs — makes it stand out.

Finding them automatically: one dimension

Eyeballing pictures is the easy part. For a single quantitative attribute there is a standard tool.

Box and whisker plot

The top and bottom of the box are the 75th and 25th percentiles; the line through the box is the median, the 50th. The whiskers run out to whatever you consider a reasonable range — the 10th and 90th percentiles, say, or the 5th and 95th. Every value beyond the whiskers is plotted individually, and those points are the possible outliers.

A convention this lesson doesn’t mention

Most statistical software does not use percentile whiskers by default. It extends them to the furthest point within 1.5 × IQR of the box, where IQR is the 75th minus the 25th percentile. For the data above that gives 44.75 − 1.5 × 8.75 = 31.6 and 53.5 + 1.5 × 8.75 = 66.6, so only 71 and 96 get flagged — half as many points as the percentile rule. Same data, same plot type, different answer. Know which convention your software used before you report a count of outliers.

quick checkanswered

A colleague reports that a box plot “found four outliers” in the order-time data. What is the first thing to ask?

Exam target

Name every element of a box and whisker plot and say what each shows.

Show answer

Box edges: the 25th and 75th percentiles. Line inside the box: the median, the 50th percentile. Whiskers: out to a chosen reasonable range, such as the 10th and 90th or 5th and 95th percentiles. Individual dots beyond the whiskers: the possible outliers.

Finding them automatically: more than one dimension

There is no good all-purpose method for detecting multi-dimensional outliers, or the other types, before modelling. That is a genuine gap rather than an omission from the lesson.

What you can do is turn the problem around: fit a model first, then look at where it is most wrong. Fit an exponential smoothing model to a time series, and in most periods the difference between the actual value and the model’s estimate will be small, because the fitted function is smooth. At an outlier it will not be. The model expects a point sitting on the smooth curve, and the actual value is nowhere near it.

Only half the battle

Everything in this lesson produces candidates. Not one of these methods tells you whether a flagged point is a mistake, a rare but genuine event, or the most informative observation in the dataset. That question is Lesson 3’s, and it cannot be answered from inside the dataset.

Worth noticing

Why does the model-error approach catch contextual outliers when a box plot can’t?

Show answer

A box plot sees only the distribution of values, so a mid-winter reading equal to a normal summer one sits comfortably inside the box. A fitted time-series model expects a particular value at that time, so the same reading produces a large error. The model supplies exactly the context the word “contextual” refers to.

Lesson 3

Dealing with outliers

Finding an outlier is the easy half — deciding whether it is an error, a real rarity worth modelling, or a real event you can’t model, takes investigation outside the dataset.

First question: what caused it?

Everything else follows from this. Often an outlier is simply bad data:

  • A sensor malfunctioned
  • A wireless transmission between sensor and receiver failed
  • An experiment was contaminated — bacteria accidentally introduced into a medical test, say
  • A person entered the wrong figure, or copied the wrong value across

But sometimes an outlier reflects something real in the system. Then you need to find out what caused it and decide whether the model should account for it.

The uncomfortable part

Answering either question — is it real or an error, and if real what to do — usually requires investigation. Where did the data come from? How was it compiled? What was unusual about the circumstances of that particular point? This means looking beyond a dataset and a model. It is also a fast way to make your results actually worth something to whoever asked for them.

  1. Flag the candidate, by any of the methods in Lesson 2.
  2. Trace it: where the value came from, how it was recorded, and what was going on at the time.
  3. Decide whether it is an error or real. This step happens outside the dataset.
  4. If it is an error, omit the point — or, if you need a value in that slot, impute one.
  5. If it is real, ask whether the thing it represents can be predicted at all.
  6. If it can, decide whether your model should represent it, or whether a second model should.
  7. If it can’t, you may still have to remove it — but now you know what you are removing.

Exam target

Give four causes of an outlier that is simply bad data.

Show answer

A malfunctioning sensor; a failed transmission between sensor and receiver; a contaminated experiment; and human error in entering or copying a value.

If it is real: expect some of them

Some outliers are nothing but true randomness. With enough data, extreme values are not merely possible — they are guaranteed.

normally distributed noise:
P( |x − μ| > 2σ )  =  4.55%
P( |x − μ| > 3σ )  =  0.270%  →  about 2,700 points in a million Both figures are two-sided — they count values that are extreme in either direction. Rounding the second to “about 2,000” undercounts it; if you want the one-sided figure, values more than 3σ above the mean, that is roughly 1,350 in a million.

So in a dataset of a million points, thousands of them being far from expectation is not evidence of anything wrong. It is what the distribution says should happen. The question then is not whether these points are legitimate but whether they matter to the model.

The test to apply

If the magnitude of your model’s error is part of how its value is judged, then rare large values may belong in the dataset. Throw them out and the model becomes too optimistic, and reality will supply some bad surprises.

Take a supply chain moving perishable medicine from a manufacturing site to clinics in a remote region. Most shipments take a predictable time. Occasionally severe weather or a border disruption makes one take far longer. Those points are real, they recur, and the cost of underestimating them lands on patients. Deleting them would produce a model that is right about the ordinary case and silent about the case that matters.

Splitting one model into two

You do not have to choose between including outliers and discarding them. A common alternative is to model the two regimes separately:

  1. Build one model — logistic regression, for example — estimating the probability that an outlying event occurs under given conditions.
  2. Build a second model estimating the outcome under normal conditions, fitted to data with the outliers removed.
  3. Combine them when forecasting: how likely is the disruption, and how long does each case take.

Why this often beats a single model

A single model fitted to both regimes is pulled between them and describes neither well. Splitting lets each model see a coherent population. The price is that you now have two models to validate, and a probability estimate that is itself uncertain.

Check

In a million normally distributed observations, roughly how many sit more than three standard deviations from the mean?

Show answer

About 2,700, counting both tails — 0.27% of the data. Roughly 1,350 of those are on the high side. Either way, the point is that thousands of extreme values in a large dataset are expected rather than suspicious.

Real, and still unusable

The last case is the awkward one: an outlier that is genuine data but describes something that will not happen again in any predictable way. Sometimes such points have to be removed even though they are correct.

What makes this case dangerous is that it looks exactly like bad data from the outside. The scenario below is one version of how that goes wrong.

Priya is fitting a weekly sales model for a single product line. Three years of data look clean except for one week, where sales spike to roughly four times anything else in the series. Every other column that week looks normal.

A teammate: “That’s obviously a data error — probably a duplicated import. Drop it and refit; we’re already behind.”

What should Priya do?

The point is confirmed as real. It was driven by a one-off news cycle. Nothing in Priya’s available data — price, promotion, season, store count — would have predicted it, and nobody expects it to recur.

The lesson underneath the story

Even when you are confident you know what an odd data point is, it is worth asking. The cost of one conversation is small; the cost of deleting the most informative row in a dataset is not. And note that checking changed the reason for removing the point without changing the decision — which is still a better position to be in.

Worth noticing

Two of the three destinations for an outlier end in removing it. What distinguishes them?

Show answer

The reason, and therefore what you write down. An erroneous point is removed because it never happened. A verified one-off is removed because it happened but cannot be predicted from the data you have. Only the second obliges you to record the event somewhere, in case it stops being a one-off.

Extra 1 · not class material

The full detection toolbox

Lesson 2's box plot is one of about a dozen standard methods. They disagree with each other routinely, and knowing why is more useful than knowing any one of them.

Two failure modes every method has to handle

Before the catalogue, the two problems that separate the good rules from the naive ones. Both arise because most rules estimate "normal" from the same data that contains the abnormality.

Masking

Outliers inflate the estimate of spread, which makes them look less extreme, which can push them back inside the threshold. Two outliers hide each other; one large one can hide a smaller one. This is why a rule built on the mean and standard deviation is fragile.

Swamping

The reverse: an extreme point drags the centre toward itself, so perfectly ordinary points on the opposite side start to look far from the mean and get flagged. The method reports outliers that are artefacts of the outlier it should have found.

Worked demonstration — the same twenty values, three rules

Order-fulfilment times, in hours: 12, 14, 14, 15, 15, 16, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 28, 31, 88, 96. Two values are clearly wrong. Mean = 26.80, standard deviation = 22.90, median = 19.5, MAD = 4.5, Q1 = 15.5, Q3 = 25.5, IQR = 10.

These give a p-value rather than a flag, which matters when you have to justify the decision to someone. All of them assume the data is approximately normal apart from the outliers, and all of them are misleading if it is not — a skewed distribution produces "significant" outliers in its long tail forever.

On the fulfilment data above

Grubbs computes the largest z, which is 3.02 for 96 against a 5% critical value of about 2.71 at n = 20 — significant, so 96 is declared an outlier. Then you stop, because Grubbs tests one point. Iterating it naively would next test 88 against a recomputed mean and standard deviation, and while here it would in fact be caught, the significance level of that second test is not the one you think it is. If you suspect two, the correct tool is generalised ESD with an upper bound of two or three, which handles the sequencing properly.

Family 3 — More than one dimension at a time

Lesson 2 raises this: a point can be entirely ordinary on every attribute separately and absurd in combination — a 22-year-old with 40 years of driving experience. Column-by-column rules cannot see it.

  • Mahalanobis distance. Distance from the centre measured in units of the data's own covariance, so it accounts for how attributes vary together. Under normality its square follows a chi-squared distribution with as many degrees of freedom as there are attributes, which gives a threshold with a real significance level attached. The standard multivariate rule.
  • Robust Mahalanobis (MCD). Same idea, but the centre and covariance are estimated from the most tightly packed subset of the data — the minimum covariance determinant — so the outliers cannot inflate the covariance that is meant to catch them. This is the masking fix, applied in many dimensions, and it matters more here than in one dimension because a few bad points can distort a covariance matrix badly.
  • Regression influence measures. When there is a fitted model, "outlier" splits into three distinguishable things: a large residual (the model predicts this point badly), high leverage (the point sits far out in the space of the inputs, so it has the opportunity to move the fit), and high influence (it actually does move the fit). Cook's distance measures the third by refitting without the point and seeing how much the predictions change; DFFITS and DFBETAS do the same per-prediction and per-coefficient. A high-leverage point that happens to sit on the line is harmless; a moderate residual with high leverage can be the most damaging point in the dataset. This is Lesson 2's "fit a model and look at the error" made precise.

Family 5 — Outliers in time series

Lesson 2's contextual outlier lives here, and the general recipe is the one the lesson describes: model what the value should have been, then examine the residual. What varies is the model.

  • Seasonal decomposition (STL) residuals. Strip out trend and seasonality, then apply any of the univariate rules above to what is left. A 35°C reading in July is only an outlier once July has been accounted for.
  • Forecast residuals. Fit an ARIMA or exponential-smoothing model and flag points far from their one-step-ahead prediction. Module 6's CUSUM is the same trick with the residuals accumulated rather than judged one at a time — which is precisely the difference between an outlier and a change.
  • Hampel filter. Rolling median and rolling MAD, so "normal" is defined locally and can drift.

When two methods disagree, that is the result

Running three detectors and getting three different sets of flagged points is the normal outcome, not a sign that something is broken. Each encodes a different definition of "doesn't fit", which is Lesson 2's opening complication restated: the definition was never settled in the first place. The productive response is to treat every disagreement as a point worth looking at by hand — and none of it changes Lesson 3's verdict, which still comes from knowing where the data came from rather than from any statistic.

Extra 2 · not class material

The rest of data preparation

Lesson 1 names three problems and pursues one. Here are the other two, plus the ones it does not name — and the single rule that governs how all of them must be carried out.

The rule that comes before every technique on this page

Fit every transformation on training data only

A scaler needs a mean and a standard deviation. An imputer needs a median. A target encoder needs group averages. Every one of those is a parameter estimated from data, and if you estimate it from the whole dataset before splitting, information from the validation and test sets has leaked into the training procedure. The validation score then overstates how well the model will do, in exactly the way Module 3 spends twelve lessons warning about.

The correct order is: split first, compute the statistic on the training portion, apply it unchanged to the held-out portion. Inside cross-validation this has to happen within each fold, refitting the transformation k times, which is why every serious library has a pipeline object — it exists precisely so this cannot be got wrong by accident. Standardising the full dataset and then calling train_test_split is the single most common leakage bug in practice, and it is silent: nothing errors, the score is just quietly too good.

Problem 1 — Scale, in more detail than Lesson 1 gives it

Often the single most useful transformation there is, because it turns multiplicative structure into additive structure — and it makes extreme values much less extreme without deleting anything, which is sometimes the better answer to a Lesson 3 dilemma.
Box–Coxa power transform with the exponent fittedYou want the log idea generalised, with the data choosing the power. Strictly positive values only.
Yeo–Johnsonthe same, extendedAs Box–Cox but handles zero and negative values. The safer default of the two.
Quantile transformmap to ranks, then to a uniform or normal distributionNothing else works and the model needs well-behaved inputs. It forces any distribution into the target shape — but it collapses outliers onto the boundary, making them indistinguishable from ordinary extreme points, which destroys exactly the information Lesson 3 cares about. Powerful and blunt.

Two practical notes. Tree-based models need none of this — decision trees, random forests and gradient boosting split on thresholds within each column independently, so any monotonic rescaling gives an identical tree. If you are wondering whether to scale, the first question is which model. And scaling does not remove outliers: a point three standard deviations out is still three standard deviations out afterwards. Scaling fixes the comparability problem, not the extremity problem.

Problem 2 — Missing values

Lesson 1 names this and passes it on. The essential first step is not a technique but a question: why is it missing? The answer determines whether any fix is safe.

MechanismMeaningConsequence
MCAR
missing completely at random		Whether a value is missing has nothing to do with anything — a form was lost, a sensor dropped a packet.The easy case. Deleting the rows loses power but introduces no bias.
MAR
missing at random		Missingness depends on observed data — older respondents skip a question more often, and you have their age.Fixable. Imputation that uses the other columns can recover an unbiased picture.
MNAR
missing not at random		Missingness depends on the unobserved value itself — high earners decline to state income; a sensor fails precisely when readings go out of range.The dangerous case. No imputation method fixes it, because the information needed is exactly what is absent. The fact of missingness is itself data and should be recorded as an indicator column.

The techniques, in rough order of sophistication:

  • Deletion. Drop rows (listwise) or columns. Safe under MCAR, biased otherwise, and it can throw away most of a dataset when missingness is scattered across many columns.
  • Mean / median / mode imputation. One number for every gap. Fast, and it systematically shrinks the variance of that column and weakens its correlations with everything else — the imputed points all sit exactly at the centre. Acceptable for a small percentage, misleading beyond that. Use the median for skewed columns.
  • Indicator column. Add a binary "was missing" flag alongside whatever you impute. Cheap, and it lets the model learn from the missingness itself — essential under MNAR.
  • Regression / k-NN imputation. Predict the missing value from the other columns. Preserves relationships far better; understates uncertainty, because the imputed value is a point estimate treated as though it were observed.
  • Multiple imputation (MICE). Generate several complete datasets with different plausible fills, analyse each, and combine the results so the extra uncertainty appears in the standard errors. The statistically correct answer under MAR, and the standard in fields where inference matters more than prediction.

Problem 3 — Categorical attributes

Not mentioned in Lesson 1, but it sits in the same slot: something the model cannot consume as it stands.

  • One-hot encoding. One binary column per category. The safe default; explodes in width when a column has thousands of levels.
  • Ordinal encoding. Map categories to integers. Correct only when there genuinely is an ordering (small < medium < large). Applied to unordered categories it invents a false ordering and false spacing — the same error as numbering regions and computing a distance in Module 4.
  • Target encoding. Replace each category with the mean response for that category. Handles high-cardinality columns compactly and leaks badly if computed on the full data, since each row's own response contributes to its own feature. Must be done inside the cross-validation folds, with smoothing for rare categories.
  • Frequency encoding, hashing, embeddings. Progressively more compact routes for very high cardinality.

A fourth problem: unbalanced classes

When 0.2% of transactions are fraudulent, a model that predicts "not fraud" every time is 99.8% accurate and completely useless. The standard responses are resampling the training set (oversampling the minority, undersampling the majority, or generating synthetic minority examples with SMOTE), weighting the classes in the loss function, or leaving the data alone and changing the metric — precision, recall, F1 or the area under the precision–recall curve instead of accuracy. Two cautions worth carrying: resampling belongs inside the cross-validation loop, never before the split, for the leakage reason above; and it is applied to the training folds only, never to the held-out fold, which must keep the real class balance or the estimate is meaningless.

Why does preparing data belong in the cross-validation loop?

Show answer

Because every preparation step that estimates something — a mean, a median, a category average — is part of the modelling procedure, not a fixed property of the world. Practice, reading and tools

Seven problems with the arithmetic worked out, a self-test, and where to read further. Do Problems 1 and 2 by hand — the fences and the modified z-score are the two calculations most likely to be asked for.

Part A — the arithmetic

Problem 1 — box plot by hand

Twelve values: 4, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 21, 42. Find the median, quartiles, IQR, the whisker fences, and say which points the box plot flags.

Show worked solution

Already sorted, n = 12.
Median: mean of the 6th and 7th values = (12 + 13)/2 = 12.5.
Q1: median of the lower six (4, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12) = (8 + 9)/2 = 8.5.
Q3: median of the upper six (13, 14, 16, 18, 21, 42) = (16 + 18)/2 = 17.
IQR: 17 − 8.5 = 8.5.
Fences: lower 8.5 − 1.5(8.5) = −4.25; upper 17 + 1.5(8.5) = 29.75.
Flagged: 42 only. The whiskers are drawn to the most extreme values inside the fences, so the upper whisker ends at 21, not at 29.75 — a detail people get wrong. The lower whisker ends at 4.

Note what the box plot does not say: nothing about whether 42 is a typing error, a genuine long job, or a different kind of order altogether. That is Lesson 3's question.

Problem 2 — classical versus modified z-score

Same twelve values. Compute the classical z-score of 42 (mean 14.58, sd 10.05) and the modified z-score (median 12.5, MAD 4.5). Compare.

Show worked solution

Classical: (42 − 14.58) / 10.05 = 2.73. Under the common |z| > 3 rule this does not flag.
Modified: 0.6745 × (42 − 12.5) / 4.5 = 0.6745 × 6.56 = 4.42. Against the usual cut-off of 3.5, this flags clearly.

The gap is the whole argument for robust rules. 42 has inflated both the mean it is being compared to and the standard deviation it is being divided by, so it shrinks its own score from both directions at once. The median and MAD are computed from the middle of the data and cannot be moved by one extreme point, so the modified score reports the distance honestly. To find the MAD: take |x − 12.5| for every value, giving 8.5, 5.5, 4.5, 3.5, 1.5, 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 3.5, 5.5, 8.5, 29.5, and take the median of those, which is 4.5.

Problem 3 — how many false alarms should you expect?

You apply the 1.5 × IQR rule to a clean sample of 5,000 normally distributed measurements. Roughly how many points get flagged, and what should you conclude?

Show worked solution

Under normality the Tukey fences sit at roughly ±2.7 standard deviations, and about 0.7% of a normal distribution falls beyond them — so roughly 35 points. Every one of them is a perfectly ordinary observation from the intended process.

The conclusion is Lesson 3's, arrived at from the other direction: in a large dataset, most flagged points are not errors. The flag rate is a property of the rule and the sample size, not evidence about the data. This is also why "delete everything outside the whiskers" is a bad default — on 5,000 points it discards 35 real observations, and if the distribution has any right skew at all, far more than that.

For scale, the comparable figures for standard-deviation rules: about 4.6% of normal data lies beyond two standard deviations, and about 0.27% beyond three — roughly 2,700 points per million.

Problem 4 — the multivariate blind spot

A dataset of drivers has an age column (range 17–90) and a years-of-experience column (range 0–70). One record reads age 22, experience 40. Would a box plot on either column catch it? What would?

Show worked solution

No. Age 22 is unremarkable; 40 years of experience is unremarkable. Neither column, examined alone, has anything to report. Yet the combination is impossible — the person would have started driving at two.

What catches it: Mahalanobis distance, because age and experience are strongly correlated and this record sits far from that relationship even though it is near the centre of each margin. Or, more directly, a domain rule: experience ≤ age − 17. Or a derived feature, age minus experience, which converts the two-dimensional problem into a one-dimensional one that a box plot can see.

That last option is worth remembering, because it generalises: when you know which combination is the meaningful one, computing it as a new column is usually simpler and more interpretable than reaching for a multivariate detector.

Part B — judgement

Problem 5 — four flagged points, four different answers

An A&E department's dataset of patient wait times flags four points. For each, say what you would do and why.
(a) A wait of −15 minutes.
(b) A wait of 1,440 minutes, on a day the hospital's IT system was down.
(c) A wait of 611 minutes, on the night of a major road accident.
(d) A wait of 380 minutes, on an ordinary Tuesday, with nothing unusual recorded.

Show worked solution

(a) Remove or correct. A negative wait is physically impossible, so it is an error — most likely swapped timestamps. If the underlying times are recoverable, correct it; if not, treat the value as missing rather than deleting the whole patient record, since the other columns are probably fine.
(b) Remove, with documentation. Exactly 1,440 minutes is 24 hours, which is the signature of a default or a fallback value rather than a measurement. Combined with the known IT outage, this is bad data about the recording system rather than data about the patient. Note that you are removing it because you identified the cause, not because it was extreme.
(c) Keep — and consider modelling it separately. This is real, explicable, and quite possibly the most informative row in the dataset: it is the only evidence you have about how the department performs under surge. Deleting it produces a model that is confidently wrong about exactly the situation anyone would want it to predict. Lesson 3's "split one model into two" applies if there are enough such nights to model.
(d) Keep, and investigate. Nothing justifies removal. A long wait on an ordinary day is either genuine variation — which the model should learn — or a signal of something not captured in the columns you have, which is worth finding out. The temptation to delete it because it is inconvenient is precisely the failure the module warns about.

Problem 6 — diagnosing a pipeline

A colleague's workflow is: load data → standardise all columns → remove points with |z| > 3 → split into train and test → fit → evaluate. Name three separate problems.

Show worked solution

1. Leakage. The mean and standard deviation are computed from the whole dataset including the test set, so the test score is optimistic. The split must come first and the scaler must be fitted on the training portion only.
2. The wrong scaler for the job, used to find outliers. Standardisation is computed from the mean and standard deviation, both of which the outliers themselves inflate — so this is the masking problem, and the |z| > 3 step will miss exactly the points it is meant to catch when there is more than one. A modified z-score or IQR rule belongs here.
3. Deleting without asking why. Everything beyond three standard deviations is removed automatically, which on a large dataset discards thousands of genuine observations, and on a right-skewed column discards one entire tail. This is Lesson 3's central warning, automated.

A fourth, if you want it: the outlier removal is applied to the test set as well, so the reported performance is on an artificially clean sample that does not resemble what the model will meet in production.

Problem 7 — choosing a method

Name a detection method for each and say why.
(a) One column, 40 values, roughly normal, you suspect one bad reading and need a p-value for a report.
(b) 200,000 rows, 30 mixed columns, no distributional assumptions.
(c) A fitted regression where one point seems to be bending the line.
(d) Hourly energy demand where the suspect reading is high for 3 a.m. but normal for the day overall.

Show worked solution

(a) Grubbs' test. Designed for exactly one outlier in approximately normal data, and it returns a p-value. If you suspected two or three, generalised ESD instead — never Grubbs applied repeatedly.
(b) Isolation Forest. Scales to that size, makes no distributional assumption, handles many columns, and needs almost no tuning. Local Outlier Factor if the data has clusters of clearly different density.
(c) Cook's distance. It measures influence directly — how much the fitted values change when the point is dropped — which is the actual question. Check leverage and the standardised residual alongside it to see why: high leverage plus a moderate residual is the damaging combination.
(d) Decompose first. Remove the daily and weekly seasonal pattern with STL or fit a forecasting model, then apply any univariate rule to the residuals. This is Lesson 2's contextual outlier, and the general recipe is always the same: model what the value should have been, then examine the error.

Part D — written questions

Four to write out

1. Define masking and swamping, and explain why they argue for median-based rules.

Show a model answer

Masking is when outliers inflate the estimate of spread so much that they fall back inside the threshold built from it — two extreme points hide each other, and one large one can hide a smaller one. Swamping is the reverse: an extreme point pulls the estimated centre toward itself, so ordinary points on the far side start to look distant and get flagged. Both arise because the mean and standard deviation are computed from the same data that contains the contamination, and both are unbounded — a single value can move them arbitrarily far. The median and the median absolute deviation are computed from the middle of the ordered data and cannot be moved by extreme values at all, so a rule built on them reports the distance of a suspect point honestly regardless of how many other suspects there are.

2. Explain why the 1.5 × IQR rule flags points in perfectly clean data, and what follows for large datasets.

Show a model answer

The fences sit at roughly ±2.7 standard deviations under normality, and about 0.7% of a normal distribution lies beyond them. The rule therefore has a fixed false-positive rate by construction: it is a convention about where to draw whiskers, not a test with a null hypothesis. In a sample of 5,000 clean measurements it flags around 35 points; in a million, around 7,000. It follows that in any large dataset most flagged points are ordinary, that the flag count carries no information about data quality, and that automatic deletion of everything outside the whiskers discards genuine observations at a predictable rate — worse on skewed data, where one whole tail is legitimately long.

3. A dataset has 15% missing values in one column. Describe how you would decide what to do.

Show a model answer

Start with why the values are missing, because the mechanism determines what is safe. If missingness is unrelated to anything (MCAR), deletion is unbiased but wasteful at 15%. If it depends on other observed columns (MAR), imputation using those columns — regression, k-NN, or multiple imputation — recovers an unbiased picture. If it depends on the unobserved value itself (MNAR), for example a sensor that fails when readings go out of range or high earners declining to state income, no imputation method can fix it, because every complete case you would learn from comes from the wrong part of the distribution. In that case the fact of missingness is itself information: add an indicator column and let the model use it. Whatever you choose, the imputation statistic must be estimated inside each cross-validation fold, not once on the whole dataset.

4. Why must scaling and imputation happen inside the cross-validation loop?

Show a model answer

Because both estimate parameters from data — a mean and standard deviation, a median — and those estimates are part of the modelling procedure rather than fixed facts about the world. Module 3 estimates generalisation performance by applying the whole procedure to data it has never seen; if the scaler's mean was computed from the full dataset, the held-out rows contributed to it and the score is optimistic. The fix is to refit every transformation on each fold's training portion and apply it unchanged to that fold's held-out portion, which is what a pipeline object automates. The bug is silent — nothing errors, the number is simply too good — which is why it is the most common leakage mistake in practice.
