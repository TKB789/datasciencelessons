Lesson 1

Introduction to clustering

Clustering groups points that are similar — and it finds groups you didn’t know were there, not just the ones you went looking for.

The definition

Clustering

Taking a set of data points and dividing them into groups so that each group contains points that are close to each other, or similar.

Learning typeUnsupervised — nothing is being predicted (formalised in Lesson 6)
GoalPoints inside a cluster are near each other
OutputGroup assignments, not a numeric prediction
Grouping basisDistance between data points

Clustering has two payoffs, not one. It segments data into groups you expected, and it can also reveal groups you didn’t know were in the dataset.

Check 1

Why is clustering called unsupervised learning, when regression is not?

Show answer

There is no response variable being predicted and no correct answer to train against. The algorithm only sees the attributes and groups points by how close they are to each other.

Where it gets used

Targeted marketing. Segment potential customers and give each group a different message, because different people respond to different message types. For a consumer durable — say a family car — the four angles might be capacity, price, versatility and status. Data produces the clusters; marketing experts then decide what material each cluster sees.

The discovery payoff shows up here: inspecting a cluster might reveal a segment you never planned for, such as buyers who care about gas mileage.

Personalized medicine. An ideal cluster is a set of people who would react similarly to a treatment, while different clusters react differently.

Facility location. Here closeness is literal physical distance: cluster people by where they live, then put one facility in each cluster. Civic examples are police stations, fire stations, medical clinics and libraries; the same approach works for gas stations and fast food restaurants.

Image analysis. Re-coloring pictures, recognizing faces and other objects. This is why handwritten CAPTCHAs keep getting more ambiguous — clustering algorithms keep getting better at reading them.

Initial data investigation. Find obvious clusters, then analyze each one separately in case they have different properties. Predicting how much a buyer will spend might need a different model per cluster.

Lesson 2

Distance and norms

Euclidean, Manhattan and infinity distance are one formula with three values of p — and the right p comes from how the real process works.

The same two points, three answers

Take X = (0, 0) and Y = (3, 4). Every metric below measures the same gap and gets a different number:

d = √( (x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)² )Straight-line, or Euclidean, distance.
d = |x₁ − y₁| + |x₂ − y₂|Rectilinear distance. Used for driving on a city street grid, which is why it is also called Manhattan distance.
Check 4

Why can the rectilinear distance never be smaller than the Euclidean distance?

Show answer

A straight line is the shortest path, so any route made of axis-aligned legs can only be longer or equal. They are equal only when the points already line up on one axis.

Generalizing: the p-norm

d = ( Σⁿᵢ₌₁ |xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵖ )1/pThe p-norm distance, also called the Minkowski distance of order p. Written for n dimensions.

Any value of p is available in theory — 3, 10, 1000. In practice the third most common choice is p = ∞.

Why the infinity norm is just “the biggest”

  1. Raise every coordinate difference to the infinity power.
  2. The largest term dominates completely. 8∞ swamps 7∞, and their sum is so close to 8∞ that the second term is irrelevant.
  3. So the sum is essentially (largest difference)∞.
  4. You then take the ∞th root of an ∞ power. Root and power cancel.
  5. What’s left is the largest difference.
Infinity norm

The largest of a set of absolute differences. Nothing more. The treatment here is deliberately informal with the infinities here because the intuition comes across better; a formal treatment would use the limit as p goes to infinity.

Check 5

Two points differ by 2 in one dimension and 9 in another. Give the 1-, 2- and ∞-norm distances.

Show answer

1-norm: 11. 2-norm: √85 ≈ 9.22. ∞-norm: 9.

Note the ordering ∞ ≤ 2 ≤ 1. The larger p gets, the more the single biggest gap dominates.

When you’d actually want it: the warehouse

A warehouse has an automated storage and retrieval system. In each aisle a machine moves horizontally along the aisle, and also stretches vertically to reach the right height. If it moves, stops, then stretches, the total is the sum of the two times — a 1-norm. If it stretches while it moves, the total is whichever takes longer — an ∞-norm.

Lesson 3

The k-means algorithm

Alternate two cheap steps — reassign points, recompute centers — until nothing moves. It skips an optimization problem that would be far too slow to solve exactly.

Setup and notation

The running example is car buyers over the past eight years, with age horizontal and average daily temperature where they live vertical. With n data points and m attributes:

xijValue of data point i, attribute j — the data itself
yik1 if data point i is in cluster k, 0 if not — cluster membership
zkjThe jth dimension coordinate of cluster center k
minimize   Σᵢ Σₖ   yik · d( xi , zk )

subject to   Σₖ yik = 1   for every point i The first part adds up distances from points to centers, but only where the point is in that cluster — the job the 0/1 y term does. The constraint forces every point into exactly one cluster.

This is a hard optimization problem, covered properly in the optimization topic later in the course. So instead of solving it, we use k-means.

Check 7

Which of x, y, z is given to you, and which two are you solving for?

Show answer

x is observed and fixed. Both y (membership) and z (center locations) are unknowns — and they depend on each other, which is why k-means alternates between them.

Check 8

Why is “no point changed clusters” a safe place to stop?

Show answer

Centers are computed purely from membership. If membership didn’t change, the centroids are identical to last round, so the next assignment reproduces the same result. The algorithm is at a fixed point.

Jargon break

Heuristic

An algorithm not guaranteed to find the absolute best solution, but which in many cases — including this one — usually gets very close and gets there quickly.

Expectation-maximization (EM)

Calculating cluster centers takes the mean of the points in a cluster, similar to an expectation. Reassigning points strictly minimizes distance, but you can view it as maximizing the negative of the distance. k-means alternates between the two.

Lesson 4

k-means in practice

Outliers, restarts and the choice of k each get a quantitative default and a qualitative override — and the qualitative one comes first every time.

Outliers

An outlier here means a point that isn’t close to any cluster center. k-means has no concept of “not in a cluster” — it assigns the outlier to whichever center is nearest, even though the point may not belong there at all.

The default move: remove the point and rerun, so it doesn’t drag its cluster center artificially to one side. The lecturer’s position: that’s not always right. Better to ask what the outlier is, what makes its attributes so different, and what the implication is of putting a point like this in the nearest cluster.

The soapbox — it recurs all course

Answering those questions takes time and effort, and it is part of what separates a top-notch analytics professional from someone who blindly runs algorithms. In many situations the algorithm is really just a guide. As much as this gets called data science, the best professionals also practise the art of analytics: looking at the combination of the data, the algorithm and the real situation to find the right answer.

Check 10

Why does an outlier distort a k-means solution rather than sitting harmlessly in the nearest cluster?

Show answer

The center is the centroid — the mean of its members. A far-off point pulls that mean toward itself, and the shifted center can then change which cluster other borderline points get assigned to.

Restarts, and choosing k

k-means is fast, and that speed is a resource. Run it several times from different initial centers and keep the best result. Run it across a range of different k rather than guessing. But there’s a trap:

Why total distance alone can’t choose k

Start with a k-cluster solution and add one more cluster — even one holding a single data point — and the total distance to centers gets smaller. So the clustering always appears better with more clusters. At the extreme, the best-looking solution is one cluster per data point.

Qualitative answer. The best solution is the one that fits the situation. If your city only has budget for four new fire stations, a solution giving six new areas isn’t much help.

Quantitative answer — the elbow diagram.

Check 11

A classmate picks k by finding the value with the lowest total distance to cluster centers. What goes wrong?

Show answer

Total distance falls monotonically as k rises, so that rule always picks the largest k tested — in the limit, one cluster per point.

The elbow looks at where the rate of improvement drops off. And a practical constraint can override it entirely.

Lesson 5

Clustering for prediction

Send a new point to the nearest center. Do that for every possible location and the whole space divides into regions — a Voronoi diagram, an idea centuries older than analytics.

Predicting the cluster of a new point

Take a finished solution — five clusters, triangles marking the centers. If a new point lands inside a cluster, the answer is easy. If it doesn’t land inside any cluster, choose whichever center is closest; that’s as reasonable a choice as any. Do this for every possible location and you get regions.

Voronoi diagram

The partition of a space into one region per center, where each region holds every location nearer that center than any other. Named for a mathematician who lived more than 100 years ago.

More than 150 years agoA diagram like it was used to analyze the source of a cholera outbreak in London
Early-to-mid 1600sRené Descartes used something like it in his work

The point: not all the good ideas in analytics are new and cutting-edge. Sometimes the best ideas have been around a long time and just need dusting off.

Check 12

Assigning a new point to the nearest cluster center looks a lot like KNN. What is different?

Show answer

KNN compares the new point to actual labelled training points and votes among the k nearest. Here you compare to k computed centers, and there are no labels anywhere — the groups were invented by the algorithm.

The k also means different things: number of clusters vs. number of neighbours consulted.

Lesson 6

Clustering vs. classification

Both split points into groups. The only difference is whether you already knew the right answer going in — and that difference has a name.

The difference is what we know about the data points

Not the shape of the output. Both produce groups. What differs is whether you already had the right answer going in.

Define supervised and unsupervised learning.

Show answer

Supervised: we have observed the right answer for each data point, and use that in addition to attributes. Classification is the example.

Extra 1 · not class material

The other clustering algorithms

k-means answers one specific question — split the data into k groups so that total distance to the group centre is small. Every algorithm below answers a slightly different question, and the difference shows up as a different shape of cluster it can find.

What k-means assumes, and therefore what it cannot find

Before the alternatives, it is worth being explicit about the assumptions baked into Lesson 3's loop, because each alternative exists to relax one of them.

AssumptionConsequence when it fails
You know k in advance.You have to supply it, and Lesson 4's elbow diagram is a workaround rather than an answer.
Clusters are roughly round and similarly sized.Minimising distance to a single centre point can only carve out convex, blob-shaped regions. A crescent, a ring or a long thin band gets sliced in half.
Every point belongs to some cluster.There is no “none of the above” category, so noise and outliers are forced into a group and drag its centre with them — the problem Lesson 4 addresses by hand.
Assignments are hard.A point halfway between two centres is assigned to one of them with no record that it was borderline.
The mean is meaningful.Categorical attributes have no mean, so k-means cannot be applied to them directly at all.

Family 1 — Partitioning methods (k-means and its relatives)

Same loop, different notion of a cluster's representative point, which changes what the method is robust to and what data it can handle.

MethodCentre is…Use when
k-meansthe meanThe default. Fast, scales to millions of points, minimises within-cluster squared Euclidean distance.
k-mediansthe component-wise medianYou want the 1-norm rather than the 2-norm. Far less sensitive to the outliers Lesson 4 worries about, because the median ignores how far away an extreme point is.
k-medoids / PAMan actual data pointThe centre must be a real member (a real store to site a depot at, a real patient as the archetype), or your distance measure has no meaningful average. Works with any distance matrix at all. Much slower.
k-modes / k-prototypesthe most common category / a mixThe data is categorical, or mixed numeric and categorical, where a mean does not exist.
Fuzzy c-meansthe mean, weighted by membershipYou want soft assignments — each point gets a degree of membership in every cluster rather than one label. Useful when boundary cases matter.
Bisecting k-meansthe meanYou want a hierarchy as a by-product: repeatedly split the worst cluster in two. Often better than plain k-means on document data.

k-means++ — a better answer to Lesson 4's restart problem

Lesson 4's advice is to run k-means many times from random starting points and keep the best result, because the algorithm is a heuristic that can settle into a poor local optimum. The standard improvement is to be cleverer about where the starting points go. k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) picks the first centre uniformly at random, then picks each subsequent centre with probability proportional to the squared distance from the nearest centre already chosen — so new centres tend to land far from old ones, and the initial k centres are spread across the data rather than clumped.

Two things are worth knowing. First, it comes with a guarantee: the expected cost of the initialisation alone is within a factor of 8(ln k + 2) of the optimum, which is the only bound of its kind for a method this cheap. Second, finding the true k-means optimum is NP-hard, so no practical algorithm removes the restart problem entirely — k-means++ makes each restart much more likely to be good, which means you need fewer of them. It is the default initialisation in scikit-learn and in most other implementations, which is why modern k-means feels more stable than the lecture's description suggests.

Family 2 — Hierarchical clustering

Instead of committing to k, build the whole nested family of clusterings at once and choose where to cut afterwards.

  • Agglomerative (bottom-up). Start with every point as its own cluster; repeatedly merge the two closest clusters until one remains. The merge history draws a dendrogram, a tree whose height records how far apart the things being joined were. Cut the tree at a height, and the number of branches you cross is your k — so k becomes a decision you make after seeing the structure rather than before.
  • Divisive (top-down). The reverse: start with everything together and split repeatedly. Rarer, because there are many more ways to split than to merge.

The crucial parameter is not k but the linkage — how the distance between two clusters is computed from distances between points. Different linkages produce genuinely different shapes.

LinkageCluster distance is…Tends to produce
Singlethe smallest distance between any two membersLong straggly chains. Can follow a curved band that k-means would cut, but a single bridging point can merge two real clusters — "chaining".
Completethe largest distance between any two membersCompact, roughly equal-diameter clusters. Sensitive to outliers, since one far member sets the whole distance.
Averagethe mean over all cross pairsA compromise between the two; the common default.
Wardthe increase in total within-cluster variance caused by mergingRound, similar-sized clusters — essentially the hierarchical cousin of k-means, and the one that most often agrees with it.

Hierarchical clustering is O(n²) in memory and worse in time, so it stops being practical somewhere in the tens of thousands of points. Its payoff is the dendrogram: for taxonomy-style questions — which customer types sit inside which broader types — a picture of the nesting is the answer, and no partition method gives you one.

Family 3 — Density-based clustering

A completely different definition of a cluster: not "points near a centre" but "a connected region where points are packed densely, surrounded by sparser space". Because there is no centre, there is no requirement that the region be round — which is how these methods find the rings in the figure above.

MethodHow it worksNotes
DBSCAN
Ester et al., 1996		Two parameters: a radius ε and a count minPts. A point with at least minPts neighbours within ε is a core point; core points that are within ε of each other join the same cluster; points near a cluster but not dense themselves are border points; everything else is noise.Finds arbitrary shapes, works out the number of clusters itself, and has an explicit noise label — the "none of the above" that k-means lacks. Struggles when different clusters have very different densities, because one ε cannot suit both.
OPTICSGeneralises DBSCAN by not fixing ε: it orders points by reachability and produces a plot from which clusters at many density levels can be read.Removes the hardest parameter choice, at the cost of a less direct output.
HDBSCANRuns DBSCAN across all values of ε and extracts the clusters that persist most stably.The current practical default for density clustering. Handles varying density, needs essentially one intuitive parameter (minimum cluster size), and returns a hierarchy.
Mean shiftSlide every point uphill along the estimated density until it reaches a peak; points arriving at the same peak form a cluster.Discovers the number of clusters, but the bandwidth parameter is doing the same job k did, and it is slow.

The trade you are making

Density methods swap one hard question for another. k-means asks "how many clusters?"; DBSCAN asks "how dense does dense mean?". Neither question has a data-driven answer, and both are ultimately settled by what the clusters will be used for. What changes is which question you find easier to reason about in your domain — and for spatial data, fraud, or anything with genuine noise, "how close do two events have to be to count as the same incident" is often much easier to answer than "how many kinds of incident are there".

Family 4 — Model-based clustering

Gaussian mixture models assume the data was generated by drawing from one of k Gaussian distributions, each with its own mean and its own covariance, and fit those parameters by expectation-maximisation. Lesson 3 already names EM as the general procedure k-means is a special case of — this is the general case made concrete, and the relationship is exact: a GMM with spherical, equal-sized covariances and hard assignments is k-means.

Relaxing that gives two things k-means cannot do. Clusters can be elliptical and differently sized, oriented along whatever directions the data actually varies in. And assignments are soft: each point gets a probability of belonging to each cluster, so a point sitting between two groups is reported as ambiguous rather than silently rounded off. Because a GMM is a probability model, you also get a likelihood, which means AIC and BIC can choose k for you — a principled alternative to reading an elbow off a chart.

Family 5 — Graph-based clustering

Spectral clustering builds a graph in which nearby points are connected, computes the eigenvectors of that graph's Laplacian matrix, and runs k-means in the resulting low-dimensional space. The effect is to unbend the geometry: structures that are tangled in the original space — the two rings again — become linearly separable in the embedded space, where k-means then works fine. It handles arbitrary shapes well but scales poorly (building an n × n affinity matrix), needs k as an input, and unlike the density methods has no concept of noise, so every point is assigned to something.

Affinity propagation and BIRCH round out the list — the first passes messages between points until a set of exemplars emerges (no k required, but slow), the second builds a compact summary tree in one pass and is designed for data too large to hold in memory.

Extra 2 · not class material

Distance beyond the p-norms

Lesson 2 gives the p-norm family, which covers numeric attributes measured in comparable units. Real data is often none of those things, and each departure has a standard answer.

First, the thing that breaks every distance measure

Scale decides the answer before the metric does

Suppose you cluster customers on age (18–80) and annual spend (£200–£90,000). Every distance is now essentially the difference in spend: a 40-year age gap contributes 40 to the 1-norm while a modest spend difference contributes thousands. The clustering is on spend, and age is decoration — not because you decided that, but because of the units the data happened to arrive in. Change spend to thousands of pounds and you get a different clustering from the same data.

So numeric attributes are normally standardised before any distance is computed. That is Module 5's material, and the point here is only that it is not optional for clustering. Unlike a regression, which can absorb scale differences into its coefficients, a distance-based method has no way to compensate. Lesson 2's choice of p matters much less than whether you scaled first.

When the attributes are correlated: Mahalanobis distance

Euclidean distance treats every direction as equally surprising. If height and weight are strongly correlated, a person who is tall and heavy is unremarkable, while a person equally far from the mean in the tall-and-light direction is genuinely unusual — and the 2-norm gives them the same distance. Mahalanobis distance divides out the covariance, so distance is measured in "how surprising is this, given how the data actually varies". Geometrically, it replaces circular contours of equal distance with ellipses aligned to the data's own spread. It is what makes a GMM's elliptical clusters possible, and it is also the standard multivariate outlier measure in Module 5.

When only direction matters: cosine distance

For text, recommender data, or anything represented as counts, magnitude is often an artefact. A 400-word article and a 4,000-word article on the same subject have wildly different word counts but the same profile. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their lengths entirely; cosine distance is one minus that.

cos(u, v)  =  (u · v) / ( ‖u‖ ‖v‖ )     cosine distance  =  1 − cos(u, v) Worked contrast: u = (3, 4) and v = (6, 8). Euclidean distance is 5 — they look well separated. Cosine distance is 0, because v is just u doubled: identical direction, identical profile. Which answer is right depends entirely on whether magnitude carries meaning in your data.

The standard answer to "half my columns are text". Pairs with k-medoids or hierarchical clustering, since neither needs a mean.
Sequences of different lengths

Strings

Only k-medoids and hierarchical clustering work directly with all of these, because both need nothing but a table of pairwise distances. k-means needs an average, which is why it is confined to numeric data — a point the lectures make implicitly by defining the centroid as a mean.

The problem that gets worse with every extra column

The curse of dimensionality

As the number of attributes grows, the distances between points become increasingly similar to one another: the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbour approaches 1. "Nearest" stops meaning much, and every distance-based method degrades — clustering, k-nearest neighbours, outlier detection alike.

Three standard responses, in increasing order of effort: drop irrelevant attributes, since noise dimensions do nothing but dilute the signal; reduce dimensions first with PCA or a similar method and cluster in the reduced space (this is essentially what spectral clustering does internally); or use a method built for it, such as subspace clustering, which looks for clusters that exist in some subset of the attributes rather than all of them at once. The practical warning is simply that a clustering on 200 columns is often reporting noise, and the fix is upstream of the algorithm.

Extra 3 · not class material

Choosing k, and judging the result

The elbow diagram is the first of about six standard methods, and it is the weakest of them. The rest give a number rather than a shape to squint at.

Why the elbow is unsatisfying, stated precisely

Lesson 4 makes the essential point: total within-cluster distance always falls as k rises, reaching zero when every point is its own cluster, so it cannot be minimised. The elbow heuristic looks instead for the k where the rate of improvement drops off. The trouble is that on real data there is very often no visible elbow at all — the curve bends smoothly — and where there is one, different people read it at different k. It is a diagnostic, not a decision rule.

The silhouette — the one worth learning properly

Silhouette coefficient

For each point i: let a(i) be its mean distance to the other points in its own cluster, and b(i) its mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster. Then

s(i) = ( b(i) − a(i) ) / max{ a(i), b(i) }

which runs from −1 to +1. Near +1 means the point sits comfortably inside its cluster; near 0 means it is on a boundary; negative means it is closer on average to a different cluster than to its own — it is probably in the wrong group. Average s over all points to score the clustering; compare that average across values of k and take the best.

Unlike total distance, the silhouette does not improve automatically with k, because splitting a genuine cluster in two lowers b as much as it lowers a. That is what makes it usable as a criterion instead of a curve to interpret.

Expect them to disagree — that is the finding

Run all six on the same data and it is entirely normal to get, say, gap saying 2, silhouette saying 9, Calinski–Harabasz saying 8 and the elbow showing nothing. Published comparisons find exactly this. Disagreement means the data does not have one obvious cluster count — often because it has structure at several scales, or none. The right response is to look at the candidate clusterings and ask which one is useful for the decision you are making, which is where Lesson 4's "understand the situation first" advice comes back in.

Judging a clustering when you do have labels

Everything above is an internal measure — computed from the data and the clustering alone, because in genuine unsupervised work there is nothing else. Occasionally you do have a known grouping to compare against (a benchmark dataset, or labels withheld deliberately to test the method), and then external measures apply.

  • Adjusted Rand index (ARI). Counts pairs of points that the two groupings agree about, corrected so that random labelling scores 0 and perfect agreement scores 1. The correction matters: the uncorrected Rand index rises with k for no good reason.
  • Normalised mutual information (NMI). How much knowing the cluster label tells you about the true label, scaled to [0, 1].
  • Purity / homogeneity / completeness. Simpler and more interpretable, but purity increases with k automatically — the same defect as raw total distance, and the reason ARI exists.

None of these is available in the situation Lesson 6 describes, which is the whole point of that lesson: no responses were observed, so there is nothing to score against. That is precisely why choosing k is hard in a way that choosing a model in Module 3 is not — there is no held-out truth to validate against.

Part A — distance arithmetic

Problem 1 — the three norms

Points P = (3, 1, 7) and Q = (8, 4, 5). Compute the 1-norm, 2-norm and ∞-norm distances.

Show worked solution

Component differences: |3 − 8| = 5, |1 − 4| = 3, |7 − 5| = 2.
1-norm: 5 + 3 + 2 = 10.
2-norm: √(25 + 9 + 4) = √38 ≈ 6.16.
∞-norm: max{5, 3, 2} = 5.

The ordering 1-norm ≥ 2-norm ≥ ∞-norm always holds, which is a useful arithmetic check. The reason is Lesson 2's: as p grows, the largest component increasingly dominates the sum until only it survives.

Problem 2 — when the metric changes the answer

Centre A = (0, 0), centre B = (10, 0). Point X = (6, 6). Which centre is X assigned to under the 1-norm, and under the ∞-norm?

Show worked solution

1-norm: to A, 6 + 6 = 12; to B, 4 + 6 = 10. X goes to B.
∞-norm: to A, max{6, 6} = 6; to B, max{4, 6} = 6. A tie — and note that under the 2-norm, to A is √72 ≈ 8.49 and to B is √52 ≈ 7.21, so B again.

The lesson is that Lesson 2's choice of p is not cosmetic: it changes cluster membership, and therefore the centroids, and therefore the next iteration. The ∞-norm's tie here is characteristic — it discards all information except the single worst coordinate, so ties are common.

Problem 3 — scale

You cluster on age (years, range 20–70) and income (pounds, range 20,000–200,000). Without scaling, roughly what fraction of a typical Euclidean distance comes from income?

Show worked solution

Essentially all of it. A large age difference is ~50; a large income difference is ~180,000 — about 3,600 times bigger, and squared before summing, so income contributes on the order of ten million times more to the squared distance. Age is not being down-weighted, it is being erased.

After standardising both to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, each contributes according to how much it actually varies relative to its own spread, which is what you almost always want. The exception is when the units are already comparable and meaningful — three sensor readings all in millivolts, say — where standardising would destroy real information about which sensor moves more.

Part B — algorithm behaviour

Problem 4 — one iteration of k-means by hand

Points: (1,1), (2,1), (1,2), (8,8), (9,9). Initial centres c₁ = (1,1) and c₂ = (2,1). Run one full iteration of the Lesson 3 loop using Euclidean distance, then say what happens next.

Show worked solution

Assign step. (1,1) → c₁ (distance 0 vs 1). (2,1) → c₂ (0 vs 1). (1,2) → c₁ (1 vs √2 ≈ 1.41). (8,8) → c₂ (√98 ≈ 9.90 vs √85 ≈ 9.22). (9,9) → c₂ (√128 ≈ 11.31 vs √113 ≈ 10.63).
So cluster 1 = {(1,1), (1,2)}; cluster 2 = {(2,1), (8,8), (9,9)}.
Update step. c₁ = (1, 1.5). c₂ = ((2+8+9)/3, (1+8+9)/3) = (6.33, 6).
What happens next. On the second assign step, (2,1) is now 1.12 from c₁ and 7.03 from c₂, so it switches back to cluster 1. The iteration after that gives c₁ = (1.33, 1.33) and c₂ = (8.5, 8.5), assignments stop changing, and the algorithm stops.

The point of working through this is the intermediate state: after one iteration the clustering is visibly wrong, with (2,1) grouped with two points forty units away. k-means does not converge in one pass, and a bad start costs iterations rather than causing failure — unless it costs correctness, which is the local-optimum problem Lesson 4 raises.

Problem 5 — diagnosing a bad result

You run k-means with k = 4 on customer data. One cluster contains 9,800 of the 10,000 points; the other three contain 150, 40 and 10. What are the two most likely explanations, and what would you check first?

Show worked solution

One: unscaled attributes. If one column has a far larger range than the others, the clustering is effectively one-dimensional on that column, and a few extreme values on it get picked off into tiny clusters while everything else stays together. Check first by printing the standard deviation of each column — a spread of several orders of magnitude is the tell.
Two: outliers. A handful of extreme points can capture centres entirely, which is Lesson 4's problem. The tiny clusters of 40 and 10 look exactly like this.

Both diagnoses point the same way and are not mutually exclusive. The standard workflow is: standardise, look for and deal with outliers (Module 5), rerun with k-means++ and several restarts. If the imbalance survives all of that, it may be real — some populations genuinely have one dominant group and a few small ones, in which case a density method with an explicit noise label often describes the data better than any k.

Problem 6 — choosing the method

For each, name a suitable algorithm and say why.
(a) Geo-tagged emergency call locations; you want to find hotspots and expect most calls to be scattered background.
(b) 400 patient records with 6 numeric measurements and 3 categorical fields; you want a nested picture of patient types.
(c) 5 million web sessions on 12 scaled numeric features; you want 8 segments for a marketing campaign.
(d) Overlapping customer groups where you need to know which customers are ambiguous.

Show worked solution

(a) DBSCAN or HDBSCAN. Hotspots are dense regions of arbitrary shape, the background scatter needs a noise label rather than forced membership, and you do not know how many hotspots there are. Every one of those rules out k-means.
(b) Gower distance with agglomerative hierarchical clustering. Mixed types rule out k-means (no mean for the categorical fields), 400 rows is comfortably within hierarchical clustering's limits, and "nested picture" asks for a dendrogram specifically.
(c) k-means with k-means++. Five million points rules out everything except k-means and BIRCH; k is given; the features are already scaled. This is the case k-means was built for.
(d) A Gaussian mixture model. Soft assignment is precisely the requirement — a customer with 0.55 and 0.45 membership is reported as ambiguous rather than silently rounded into one segment.

Problem 7 — computing a silhouette

Three points in cluster 1: X, Y, Z, with d(X,Y) = 2 and d(X,Z) = 4. The nearest other cluster has two points P and Q, with d(X,P) = 5 and d(X,Q) = 7. Compute s(X) and interpret it.

Show worked solution

a(X) = (2 + 4) / 2 = 3. b(X) = (5 + 7) / 2 = 6.
s(X) = (6 − 3) / max{3, 6} = 3 / 6 = 0.5.

Positive, so X is in the right cluster, but only moderately well placed — the other cluster is just twice as far as its own, which is not a wide margin. Values above about 0.7 are usually read as strong structure, 0.5 to 0.7 as reasonable, below 0.25 as essentially no structure, and anything negative as misassigned. These bands are conventions rather than tests, so treat them as rough guidance.

Part D — written questions

Four to write out

1. Explain why k-means cannot find the two rings in the figure in Extra 1, and name two algorithms that can.

Show a model answer

k-means minimises total distance to k centre points. The set of points closer to one centre than to any other is always a convex region — that is what a Voronoi cell is — so every cluster k-means can produce is convex. A ring is not convex: its own centre region belongs to the other ring. No value of k and no number of restarts changes this, because the limitation is in the objective function rather than the search. DBSCAN finds the rings by treating a cluster as a dense connected region, which follows a curve without difficulty; spectral clustering finds them by embedding the data in a space where the rings become linearly separable and then running k-means there.

2. State the k-means++ initialisation rule and explain what problem it addresses.

Show a model answer

Pick the first centre uniformly at random from the data. Then pick each remaining centre by sampling a data point with probability proportional to the squared distance from the nearest already-chosen centre, until k are chosen; then run the ordinary loop. It addresses the local-optimum problem: with uniform random starts, several centres can land in the same dense region, leaving other regions to share a centre, and the loop cannot recover. Distance-proportional sampling spreads the initial centres out, so each run is far more likely to land in a good basin — reducing how many restarts are needed rather than removing the need for them, since the underlying problem is NP-hard.

3. Give the silhouette formula, and explain why its maximum over k is meaningful while the minimum of total within-cluster distance is not.

Show a model answer

s(i) = (b(i) − a(i)) / max{a(i), b(i)}, where a(i) is the mean distance from i to the other members of its own cluster and b(i) the mean distance to the members of the nearest other cluster. Total within-cluster distance falls monotonically as k rises and hits zero when every point is its own cluster, so minimising it always selects the degenerate answer — the elbow heuristic exists only to work around that. The silhouette rewards separation as well as cohesion: splitting a real cluster in two reduces a but reduces b by a comparable amount, so the score does not improve, and the maximum lands on a genuine structure rather than on k = n.

4. Your data has eight numeric and three categorical columns. Explain what must be done before any distance-based method will give a sensible answer.

Show a model answer

Two things. The numeric columns must be put on a common scale, because otherwise the column with the largest range dominates every distance and the clustering is effectively on that column alone — the metric choice from Lesson 2 has far less influence on the result than this does. And the categorical columns need a distance definition of their own, since they have no mean and no ordering; the standard route is Gower distance, which computes a [0,1] distance per attribute in the way appropriate to its type and averages them. Gower produces a distance matrix rather than coordinates, so it pairs with k-medoids or hierarchical clustering, both of which need only pairwise distances. Encoding the categories as integers and running k-means invents an ordering and a spacing that do not exist.
