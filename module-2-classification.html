Lesson 1

What classification is

Classification means putting things into categories — and the whole problem reduces to a picture where you draw a line between two colours of dot, then argue about which line.

The definition, and it really is that simple

Classification

Putting things into categories. The term carries exactly its everyday meaning — nothing special is intended by it in analytics.

The simplest cases have two categories, and often those are just yes and no:

  • Which loan applicants will fully repay, and which won’t
  • Which email is real and which is spam
  • Which documents are relevant to a case and which are irrelevant
  • Which people passing through a screening point warrant a second look
  • Whether a donor organ is safe to transplant or carries an infectious disease

That last one shows why a model is needed at all rather than just a test. If a donor was infected very recently, a laboratory test may not come back positive yet — so an analytical approach has to fill the gap the test leaves.

More than two categories works too: sorting voters into supportive, opposed and undecided; or assigning a newly found bone to one of many species.

Worth noticing

Why can’t the transplant question be settled with a lab test alone?

Show answer

A recent infection may not have become detectable yet, so a negative test isn’t conclusive. The model uses other attributes to estimate risk the test can’t yet see.

What the data looks like

Each classification question needs data. For lending, that might be income, credit history, age, family size, assets and liabilities. Combine those attributes of previous borrowers with the observed outcome — repaid or not — and you can build a model for future applicants.

Graphically, put credit score on the horizontal axis and household income on the vertical. An applicant with a credit score of 730 and an income of $76,000 is one point. Blue if they repaid the whole loan, red if they defaulted.

Real problems have more factors — length of relationship with the bank, number of dependents, total assets, liabilities — and each is another dimension. Two dimensions are used throughout this module purely because they can be drawn.

The move that sets up everything else

Draw a line separating blue above from red below. A new applicant landing in the blue zone gets a loan; one landing in the red zone doesn’t. That works — but there are infinitely many lines that separate these points equally well. Which is best?

Lesson 2

Choosing a classifier

Prefer the line that is furthest from making a mistake — then bend that rule when one kind of mistake costs far more than the other.

Distance from error

Two candidate classifiers will agree about most applicants. It’s the ambiguous ones — sitting between the groups — where one says lend and the other says decline.

The principle: choose a line that is further from making mistakes. A line passing close to a red point and a blue point has nearly misclassified both. If the line, or the points, were slightly different, it would. And the input data is inexact — a recorded income may be a little off. Small uncertainties in the data shouldn’t flip a decision.

A line sitting well clear of every point would need a much bigger data error before it misclassified anything. Lesson 4 formalises this as maximising the margin.

Vocabulary

Hard classifier versus soft classifier — what separates them?

Show answer

A hard classifier separates the classes perfectly. A soft one accepts some misclassification and aims for the best separation available, which is what you need when no perfect boundary exists.

When one mistake costs more

So far both errors have been treated as equally bad. Often they aren’t. Lending to someone who won’t repay costs more than turning away someone who would have. Eating a poisonous plant is far worse than passing up an edible one.

The rule

The more costly one type of mistake is, the further you move the line away from it.

If a bad loan is judged twice as costly as a rejected good applicant, shift the line closer to the blue points. Now an ambiguous applicant — nearer the blue group but still in the contested zone — gets declined anyway.

With a life at stake you might go further still. Even when the data looks perfectly separable, you might deliberately misclassify some safe points in order to push the boundary further from any dangerous ones. Foragers do exactly this: anything that even resembles a poisonous species gets left alone.

The same logic runs through medical screening. Missing a case of an infectious disease is very costly, because someone may unknowingly infect others. So the initial test is set with a deliberately aggressive threshold, and anyone who tests positive takes a second, confirmatory test.

SO Sam OkaforRisk analyst, small business lending

Sam’s classifier separates repaid from defaulted loans cleanly on five years of history. Finance tells him a default costs roughly four times what a wrongly declined applicant costs in lost margin.

A colleague: “The line already gets 100% of the historical cases right. There’s nothing to change.”

What should Sam do?

Six months later. New data arrives and the classes now overlap — no line separates them. Sam’s shifted boundary now misclassifies a visible handful of applicants who did repay.

Exam target

Which way do you move the boundary when one error is more costly?

Show answer

Away from the costly side — so that ambiguous cases fall on the cheap-error side. Moving it toward the costly class would do the opposite of what you want.

A free insight from the picture

Suppose you assumed two attributes were needed, and the fitted classifier turns out to run almost parallel to the vertical axis. Then only the horizontal attribute is really doing any work — the vertical one barely affects the decision. You’ve discovered that one attribute suffices.

This observation returns in Lesson 6 as a method that works in dimensions you can’t draw.

Lesson 3

Data definitions

A table where rows are data points and columns are attributes — plus one special column, the response, that holds the answer.

The mental model

Analytics talk almost always assumes data sits in a table.

Data pointA row. One observation — one applicant, or one day of sales
AttributeA column. Also called a feature, and depending on use, a covariate or predictor
ResponseThe special column holding the answer for each data point. Also called the outcome

For loan applicants the response is whether they repaid the full loan — or it could be the fraction repaid, from 0 to 100%. For daily sales it’s the number of sales that day.

Vocabulary

Four words for a column, and one for the special column. Name them.

Show answer

Attribute, feature, covariate, predictor. The special one is the response, also called the outcome.

Types of data

Structured

Data that can be described and stored neatly. Credit score, age and number of sales are stored as numbers; a category might be stored as a single letter or one of a few fixed words.

Unstructured

Data that isn’t easily described and stored. Text is the most common example — free-written incident reports, say, where every author writes differently and the text must be parsed before any trend can be found.

Within structured data:

QuantitativeThe numbers mean something. Higher means more, lower means less. Age, sales, temperature, income
CategoricalThe number only says which category. Postcodes are the classic case — moving from one to another doesn’t mean anything increased or decreased
BinaryA special case of categorical with exactly two values. Repaid or not, holiday or not, on or off. Binary data can sometimes be treated as quantitative even though it is categorical
Time series

Sometimes data points are unrelated — each loan applicant is a different person. Sometimes they’re related in time: put daily sales in chronological order and reading down the column shows sales day after day. Often recorded at equal intervals (daily sales, a stock price every two seconds, a child’s height each birthday), but not necessarily.

Lesson 4

Support vector machines

“Find the line furthest from mistakes” becomes: minimise the sum of squared coefficients, subject to every point landing on its correct side.

Notation

mNumber of data points
nNumber of attributes per point
xijThe jth attribute of data point i — so xi1 is person i’s credit score, xi2 their income
yiThe response: +1 for a blue point (repaid in full), −1 for a red point (defaulted)
a1…anOne coefficient per attribute — these define the line’s direction
a0The intercept — this slides the line without turning it
a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aₙxₙ + a₀ = 0 A line (or in n dimensions, a hyperplane). Parallel lines share a₁…aₙ and differ only in a₀ — which is exactly why the intercept is the thing you slide in Lesson 6.
Check

What geometrically distinguishes two parallel lines in this notation?

Show answer

Only the intercept a₀. The coefficients a₁…aₙ set the direction, so keeping them fixed and changing a₀ slides the line without rotating it.

Stating “classified correctly”

Blue points must sit on one side and red points on the other:

blue:   Σⱼ aⱼxij + a₀   ≥   1
red:    Σⱼ aⱼxij + a₀   ≤   −1 The 1 is there for scaling and could be any number.

Because yi was defined as +1 for blue and −1 for red, both conditions collapse into one:

( Σⱼ aⱼxij + a₀ ) · yi   ≥   1    for every point i This is the reason the response is coded ±1 rather than 1 and 0. Multiplying by −1 flips the inequality for red points, which is exactly what merging the two conditions requires.
quick checkanswered

Why is the response coded as +1 and −1 rather than 1 and 0?

Exam target

Write the single combined constraint from memory.

Show answer

yi(Σⱼ aⱼxij + a₀) ≥ 1, required for every data point i.

Turning “widest gap” into an objective

From linear algebra, the distance between the two parallel boundary lines is

margin  =  2 / √( Σⱼ aⱼ² ) The coefficients sit in the denominator — so shrinking them widens the gap.

Maximising that margin is therefore the same thing as minimising Σ aⱼ². Which gives the whole hard-separation problem:

minimizea₀…aₙ   Σnj=1 aⱼ²

subject to   yi ( Σnj=1 aⱼxij + a₀ ) ≥ 1   for all i Find coefficients giving the greatest distance between the lines — but only from among coefficients that separate every point correctly. Plenty of software solves this directly.
put in ordersolved

Drag the derivation into sequence — or use the arrows.

  1. Code each response as +1 for blue and −1 for red
  2. Require blue points to satisfy ≥ 1 and red points ≤ −1
  3. Multiply through by yi to merge both into one constraint
  4. Note that the margin equals 2 divided by the root of the sum of squared coefficients
  5. Conclude that maximising the margin means minimising the sum of squared coefficients
  6. Minimise that sum subject to the merged constraint holding for every point

Check

Why does making the coefficients smaller widen the margin?

Show answer

They sit in the denominator of 2/√(Σaⱼ²). Shrinking them shrinks the denominator, which enlarges the fraction — the gap between the two boundary lines.

When perfect separation is impossible

Measure how wrong each point is. If point i is on the correct side, yi(Σaⱼxij + a₀) − 1 ≥ 0 and there is no error. If it is on the wrong side that quantity goes negative, and how negative is how big the mistake — the further a misclassified point sits from the line, the worse it is.

errori  =  max { 0 ,   1 − yi( Σⱼ aⱼxij + a₀ ) } Zero when the point is comfortably right; growing with distance when it is wrong.

Now trade the two goals against each other with a parameter λ:

minimize   Σi max{ 0, 1 − yi( Σⱼ aⱼxij + a₀ ) }   +   λ · Σⱼ aⱼ² Large λ: a wide margin outweighs avoiding mistakes on known points. λ toward 0: avoiding mistakes outweighs a wide margin.
Class discussion — Lesson 4click to type; saves automatically
Exam target

What happens to the classifier as λ grows very large?

Show answer

Margin width dominates, so the model accepts more misclassified training points in exchange for a wider gap. Push λ toward zero and it does the reverse, chasing a perfect fit on the training data — which Module 3 warns is not the same as a good model.

Lesson 5

Where the name comes from

A support vector is a data point the boundary rests on — and the odd part is that the classifier the model returns doesn’t touch any of them.

The picture behind the name

Take a set of points and connect the dots around the outside. That outline is called the convex hull — a term you don’t need, but it makes the picture concrete. The shape appears to float; a line placed beneath it would hold it up. If the shape balances on that line, the point where they touch is supporting it. That point is a support vector.

Why vector rather than point? Because each data point is a vector of attribute values. And support vectors don’t need the line underneath — mathematically one can support from the side, or from above. Depending on which line you choose, there may be more than one.

Why “machine”

Because the model takes the dataset and automatically determines where the two lines should go and which points are the support vectors. Nobody places them by hand.

Class discussion — Lesson 5click to type; saves automatically
Worth noticing

If you deleted a data point sitting well inside the blue cloud and refitted, what would change?

Show answer

Nothing. Only the points the boundaries rest against constrain the solution — interior points are already comfortably satisfied. This is why the method is named after them, and it makes SVM robust to changes far from the boundary.

Lesson 6

Extending SVM

Four extensions: weight the costly error, scale the data first, read variable importance off the coefficients, and bend the boundary with kernels.

Building unequal costs into the model

Under hard separation, the classifier you pick depends on the intercept — and it can range anywhere from a₀ − 1 to a₀ + 1 without making a single mistake on the known data. That whole range is free to choose from.

if a bad loan costs twice a rejected good one:
intercept  =  ⅔ ( a₀ − 1 )  +  ⅓ ( a₀ + 1 ) A weighted blend of the two extremes, pulled toward the costly side in proportion to the cost.

Under soft classification, add an extra multiplier for each type of error. The larger the penalty on a given error type, the less willing the model is to make it.

Check

Why is there a whole range of intercepts to choose from under hard separation?

Show answer

Because every intercept between a₀ − 1 and a₀ + 1 classifies all the known points correctly. The data can’t distinguish between them, so cost is what should.

Scale first, or the margin term breaks

Credit scores run from about 300 to 850 — some newer scales from 150 to 950 — so a range under a thousand. Household incomes measured in dollars can range over millions. That means a₁ and a₂ may differ by two or three orders of magnitude.

Since the objective adds their squares, a small change in one coefficient can swamp a huge change in the other, and the model won’t work well. The fix is to scale the data beforehand so the orders of magnitude are comparable. Some software does it automatically; some doesn’t. Lesson 7 covers exactly how.

Reading variable importance off the coefficients

Lesson 2 noticed that a classifier running almost parallel to one axis means that axis’s attribute barely matters. That is easy to spot in a drawing and impossible in twenty dimensions. But the coefficients tell you the same thing:

The rule

If a coefficient aj is very close to zero, attribute j is probably not relevant for classification, and can be dropped.

This only works after scaling. On raw data a coefficient can be tiny purely because its attribute is measured in large units — which is the whole problem above.

quick checkanswered

Your fitted SVM has a coefficient of 0.003 on income and 2.1 on credit score. What can you conclude?

Exam target

Name the two things scaling makes possible in SVM.

Show answer

It stops the sum-of-squares margin term being dominated by whichever attribute has the larger units, and it makes coefficient magnitudes comparable so they can be used for variable selection.

Lesson 7

Scaling and standardization

Two ways to put attributes on a common footing. Scale when a bounded range matters; standardize when distance from the mean matters.

Why mismatched units break the margin

Classify borrowers on household income — tens of thousands or more — and credit score, in the hundreds. A plausible set of SVM coefficients might be 5 × income and 700 × credit score. Watch what the sum of squares does.

CoefficientsSum of squaresChange
5, 70025 + 490,000 = 490,025—
5, 70125 + 491,401 = 491,426+1,401
37.8, 700≈ 1,429 + 490,000+1,404

Nudging the credit-score coefficient from 700 to 701 is under a 1% change and barely moves the line — yet it shifts the objective by 1,401. To move the objective by the same amount through the income coefficient, you’d have to take it from 5 to about 37.8: a change of more than 600%, and an enormous change to the line.

The consequence

Because the data are on such different scales, the coefficients are too — so the sum of squared coefficients is far more sensitive to one than the other. The optimizer spends all its effort on the attribute with the large coefficient and effectively ignores the other. The model doesn’t work well.

quick checkanswered

In the table above, the two coefficient changes move the objective by roughly the same amount. Why is that a problem rather than a reassurance?

Exam target

State in one sentence why unscaled data breaks an SVM.

Show answer

Attributes on different scales produce coefficients on different scales, so the sum of squared coefficients responds far more to one attribute than another and no longer measures the margin fairly.

The two fixes

Scaling — squeeze into an interval

Usually into 0 to 1, the most common choice. For each attribute j, let xmin,j and xmax,j be its smallest and largest values.

xijscaled  =  ( xij − xmin,j ) / ( xmax,j − xmin,j ) For a different range [a, b], take the 0-to-1 value, multiply by (b − a), and add a.
Standardization — recentre on the mean

Rescale to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 — the standard normal. Let μj be the mean of attribute j and σj its standard deviation.

xijstandardized  =  ( xij − μj ) / σj The result says how many standard deviations from the mean each value sits. Unbounded by construction.
Formulas to know

Write both formulas from memory.

Show answer

Scale: (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin). Standardize: (x − μ) / σ. Both are applied per attribute, using that attribute’s own statistics.

Which one, when?

It depends — and sometimes it genuinely isn’t clear, in which case you try both and see which works better.

Reach forWhen
ScalingA bounded range matters. Neural networks often require inputs between 0 and 1; optimization models may need bounded data to guarantee feasibility. Also when the data type is naturally bounded — batting averages between 0 and 1, RGB intensities 0 to 255, certain test scores 200 to 800
StandardizationSome models simply work better with it. Principal component analysis and clustering are the two named examples

The point that outlives this lesson

Scaling or standardizing the input data is often necessary, and true for most models in this course — classification, clustering, prediction, selection and others. It won’t be repeated every time it applies. Do it anyway.

Trap to avoid

Where should the min, max, mean and standard deviation come from when you also have a test set?

Show answer

The training data only, then apply those same numbers to the validation and test sets. Computing them across everything lets information from the test set leak into training — which Module 3 shows is exactly what invalidates a quality estimate. The lecture doesn’t raise this, but graders often do.

Lesson 8

k-nearest neighbours

Skip the boundary entirely — just look at the k closest known points and take a vote. Handles many classes without any extra machinery.

The idea

Same picture as before: credit score horizontal, household income vertical, blue for repaid and red for defaulted. But instead of drawing a line or any other function to separate them, assume that each new applicant resembles the previous applicants closest to it.

Look at the five nearest points to a new applicant. Four are blue, one is red — so the new point is more likely blue, and the recommendation is to lend.

There is nothing magical about five. Any number of points can be used, and that number is denoted k — which is where the name comes from.

put in ordersolved

Drag the k-nearest-neighbours procedure into sequence.

  1. Choose a value of k, and a way of measuring distance
  2. Take the new point you want to classify
  3. Measure its distance to every known data point
  4. Keep the k closest of them
  5. Count how many of those k fall in each class
  6. Assign the new point to whichever class appears most

Contrast

What does k-nearest-neighbours never build that SVM does?

Show answer

A boundary. SVM produces a line described by coefficients; k-nearest-neighbours keeps all the training points and answers each query by looking at them directly.

Four things worth noticing

  • Lesson 2 is Lesson 4 without the algebra. Every intuition — margin, soft classifiers, cost weighting — reappears as a term in the optimization. If the maths feels opaque, reread Lesson 2 and match the pictures to the symbols.
  • Scaling is load-bearing twice over. It rescues the margin term, and it is what makes coefficient-based variable selection legitimate. Skip it and both fail quietly rather than loudly.
  • The two models fail in opposite directions. SVM compresses the training data into a handful of coefficients; k-nearest-neighbours keeps every point and does all the work at prediction time. That trade decides which is practical at scale.
  • Nearly every complication is deferred. Distance metrics, variable selection, validation, logistic regression — this module names four gaps and fills none of them, which is what a cross-cutting curriculum looks like from the inside.
